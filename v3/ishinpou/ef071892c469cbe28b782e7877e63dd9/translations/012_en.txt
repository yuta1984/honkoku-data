## [General Principles of Emesis and Purgation]

It further says: As a general rule, in spring, emesis (inducing vomiting) is appropriate.

As a general rule, in all cases where the illness resides in the chest (*kyōchū*), it is appropriate to induce vomiting.

As a general rule, when one takes a decoction (*tō*) and vomits, this means the medicine has reached the illness; one may then stop [administering it], and it is not necessary to use up the entire preparation (*zai*). Those in whom emesis is indicated include: patients with *okori* (疟, malaria-like intermittent fever) and *shōkan* (傷寒, febrile illness) where the chest is full; those with accumulated phlegm (*shakutan*) and dry retching (*kan'ō*); those with phlegm-heat (*tannetsu*) in the chest and diaphragm area; those with coughing (*tensō*); and those with lung abscess (*haiyō*) expectorating pus — all of these are appropriate for emesis.

As a general rule, if there is retained food (*shukushoku*, food stagnation) in the stomach (*ikan*, the stomach vessel), one should cause it to be expelled — either by emesis or by purgation.

It further says: All persons with *shigyaku* (四逆, reversal cold of the four limbs — when all four extremities are cold) must not be subjected to emesis.

As a general rule, all persons who are deficient and emaciated (*kyorui*, weak and wasted) must not be subjected to emesis.

As a general rule, women who have recently given birth (*shinsan*) must not be subjected to emesis.

As a general rule, persons whose *kakke* (脚気, beriberi) is surging upward toward the heart (*shōshin*, the qi of beriberi striking upward toward the heart) should not be subjected to emesis.

As a general rule, a patient who has an aversion to cold and does not want clothing near them must not be subjected to emesis.

---

It further says: As a general rule, in autumn, purgation (inducing bowel movements) is appropriate.

As a general rule, when one takes a decoction (*tō*) with the aim of purging, if it reaches the illness [and takes effect], one should stop; one must not use up the entire preparation.

As a general rule, when disease flares up and there is much sweating, promptly purge the patient.

As a general rule, when a disease has persisted for five or six days and the abdomen is full and there is no bowel movement, promptly purge the patient.

As a general rule, when after a major purgation six or seven days pass without a bowel movement, and there is troublesome [unresolved] distress — the condition is not resolved, the abdomen is painful and full — this is because there is dry stool (*sōshi*, desiccated feces, hardened stool). Such a person originally has retained food (*shukushoku*); it is appropriate to use *Jōki-tō* (承気湯, Chengqi Decoction/Order-the-Qi Decoction) to purge it.

As a general rule, when a patient cannot urinate freely (*shōbenri*, dysuria), and the bowel movement is sometimes difficult and sometimes easy, and there is occasional mild fever that seems to be boiling in the stomach (*i wo wakasu*), and the patient is unable to lie down — this is because there is dry stool (*sōshi*). It is appropriate to purge [the patient].

As a general rule, in those who should be purged, a decoction (*tō*) is superior to pills (*gan*).

When there is localized pain in the lateral sub-costal region (*kyoku no shita*, below the hypochondrium) on one side, along with fever, this is a condition of cold (*kan*). One should appropriately purge using warming medicines (*on'yaku*). Even in cases of cold abdominal distension (*kantō*, distension due to cold), purgation is needed.

As a general rule, in all of the following conditions — constipation (*daiben jū*, difficulty with bowel movements) in various illnesses; abdominal fullness (*harami*) in various types of *shōkan* (febrile illness); abdominal fullness in *okori* (intermittent fever); drum-like abdominal distension (*kochō*, tympanites); water distension (*suichō*, ascites/oedema with abdominal swelling); and obstruction of the bowels (*daiben futsū*) — purgation is necessary.
