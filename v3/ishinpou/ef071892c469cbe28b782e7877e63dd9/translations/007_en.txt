**[Continuation from the *Taisu Jing* (Grand Basis Classic)]**

Because each [method] has obtained what is appropriate to it, the reason why diseases are all cured even though the treatments differ is that one has grasped the true nature (*qíng*) of the disease and knows the fundamental principle (*dàtǐ*) of treatment.

It is further said [in the *Taisu Jing*]: When examining a disease, one must always ask whether the patient was formerly noble and has since become humble. Even if one has not been struck by external pathogenic influences (*wài xié*), disease may arise from within. This is called "loss of glory" (*tuō róng* 脱栄). One who was formerly wealthy and has since become poor is called one who has suffered "loss of essence" (*shī jīng* 失精).

Yang Shangshan (*Yáng Shàngshàn*) says: One who was first noble and later became humble suffers the injury of *tuō róng* (loss of glory); one who was first wealthy and later became poor suffers the loss of *shī jīng* (loss of essence). *Tuō róng* is an injury [to the spirit], [arising from] the humiliation of lowly and base [circumstances]. *Shī jīng* is likewise an injury [to the spirit, arising from] the [hardship and disgrace] of poverty and deprivation. Even if one has not been struck by external pathogenic influences, [these conditions] are brought about by the suffering of both the body (*xíng*) and the spirit (*shén*).

**[From the *Suvarṇaprabhāsa Sūtra* (金光明最勝王経, *Saikōōkyō*)]**

*[Marginal note at top:] "The passage from the Suvarṇaprabhāsa Sūtra is absent in the Uji manuscript but present in the physician's manuscript."*

Disease is divided into four kinds: wind (*fū/fēng*), heat (*rè*), phlegm-rheum (*tán yǐn* 痰癊), and the combined (aggregate) disease (*sōng jí* 惣集), in which all of the above are gathered together. One should know the time [of the season] at which [each type] is activated.

In the middle of spring, phlegm-rheum (*tán yǐn*) stirs. In the midst of summer, wind disease arises. In autumn, yellow heat (*huáng rè*) increases. In winter, all three arise together.

In spring, eat [foods that are] astringent, warming, and pungent. In summer, [avoid] oily, hot, salty, and sour [foods]. In autumn, [eat] cool, sweet, and oily [foods]. In winter, [eat foods that are] sour, astringent, oily, and sweet.

If, in taking medicines and in eating and drinking throughout these four seasons, one follows the flavors [appropriate to] each season as described above, there will be no cause (*yóu*) for the various diseases to arise.

Post-meal diseases are caused by rheum (*yǐn* 癊); diseases arising when food is being digested are caused by heat; diseases arising after [food] has been digested are caused by wind. One must identify the disease in accordance with the [appropriate] time.

Once the source of the disease (*bìng yuán*) has been identified, one should then set forth (*shè*) the medicine in accordance with the disease. Even if the presenting symptoms are varied and unusual, one must first treat the root (*běn*) [of the condition].

For wind disease, take oily and fatty [foods/medicines]. For heat disease, promoting [downward] flow (*lì* 利) is considered the best [treatment]. For rheum disease (*yǐn bìng*), one should change the flavors [of diet and medicine]. For combined (*sōng jí*) disease, the three [types of] medicines are necessary.

When wind, heat, and rheum are all present together, this is called "combined [disease]" (*sōng jí* 惣集). Even if one knows the time at which the disease arose, one should still observe the patient's fundamental nature (*běn xìng*)—that is, their underlying constitution.

Once one has thus observed and understood [all these things], administer medicine in accordance with the [proper] season. If there is no error in [the prescribing of] food, drink, and medicine, such a person is called a "skilled physician" (*shàn yī* 善医).

Furthermore, one should know the eight arts (*bā shù* 八術). By comprehensively mastering the prescriptions and methods of all [traditions of] medicine, if one is thoroughly versed in these [arts], one will be able to treat the diseases of all sentient beings (*zhòng shēng*).
