## [Section Three: The Proper Measure of Taking Medicine, Continued]

[Continuing from the previous page:] …[their *qi* and blood] are declining and [they] cannot endure a heavy medicinal prescription. It is also appropriate to use [formulas in which] the quantities are simple and reduced.

Even if a person is one of hardship and toil (*shin-ku*, 辛苦), if that person rarely falls ill and has never been accustomed to taking medicine, then it is easy to establish a prescription of medicinal potency. In such cases too, it is appropriate to proceed in this manner.

Now, as for using a formula of small quantities when a person is vigorous and the illness is mild — if the person is strong, they will exceed the potency of the prescription; and when the quantities are simple and reduced, [the medicine] will then be unable to control the illness. Even if one takes it for a long time, its potency dissipates in proportion to [the body's strength], and in the end the illness cannot be controlled. It is for this reason that it is appropriate to take [a formula in which] the quantities are heavy and compounded.

Now, as for using a formula of large quantities when an aged and declining, depleted person has been ill for a long time and the illness has become serious — if the person is depleted and declining, and their *qi* and strength are weak, they cannot then endure the medicine. Before the medicine has been able to cure (*iyasu*, 遣) the illness, the person's *qi* and strength are exhausted first. When the person is exhausted, the illness prevails; and when the person then cannot bring themselves to take [the medicine] again, they will find no benefit from it. It is for this reason that it is appropriate to take [a formula in which] the quantities are simple and reduced.

It also states: There are those who are young and vigorous yet do not avoid wind and cold (*fū-ryō*, 風涼), transgress against prohibitions and taboos (*kin-ki*, 禁忌), and suddenly exhaust their vital essence (*sei-eki*, 精液 — the fundamental life-sustaining fluid). Even if they contract only a minor ailment (*bi-shitsu*, 微疾), in all such cases one must not lightly administer a drastic purgative (*ri-yaku*, 利薬) to purge them. If one purges [such a person], it will exhaust their vital essence, and as a result they will become bedridden (*shō-seki*, 床席 — confined to their sleeping mat), often for years on end. At the outset, it is in all cases appropriate to give them a mild, moderate medicine (*hei-yaku*, 平薬) and treat them accordingly. Only for those for whom purgation is deemed appropriate should one gradually proceed to purge them. Children, however, are exempt from this principle.

As a general rule (*ō-mune*, 大法), it is appropriate to understand things in the manner described here.

Now, even in cases where it is deemed appropriate for a person with a long-standing chronic illness (*chō-shuku*, 長宿) to take a decocted purgative medicine (*ri-tō-yaku*, 利湯薬), one must not necessarily exhaust an entire course of medicine (*ichi-zai*, 一剤) all at once. In all cases, one should observe the degree of purgation — whether much or little — and at the same time regulate and manage accordingly (*shō-soku*, 消息), within the latitude (*yuru*, 寛) of a single day. For those in whom the root source of the illness (*byō-gen*, 病源) has not yet been eliminated, the following day one should prepare another course of medicine.
