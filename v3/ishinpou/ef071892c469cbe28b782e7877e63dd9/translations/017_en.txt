## [Section Three: The Proper Measure of Taking Medicine, Continued]

[Continuing from the previous page:]

The foundation of keeping the body at peace necessarily relies upon food. The key to rescuing oneself from illness necessarily relies upon medicine. One who does not know what foods are appropriate is not capable of sustaining life. One who does not make clear what medicines are contraindicated (*kusuri no imi*, taboos in medicine) will be unable to remove illness. These two matters are truly of essential importance. If one neglects them and does not study them, it is truly lamentable.

It also states: In general, one who would be a physician must thoroughly see through (*tōshi*, penetratingly discern) the source of the disease, understand where the transgression lies, and treat it using food. If food therapy (*shokuryō*) does not bring about recovery, only then should medicine be prescribed. The nature of medicines is harsh and intense (*gōretsu*), much like soldiers (*hei*) used for defence. Given the ferocity (*mōbō*, violent force) of soldiers, how could one permit them to be deployed recklessly? If their use strays from the proper order (*gi*, correct propriety and procedure), the damage wrought will be all the greater. The administration of medicine to disease, if done recklessly, is likewise so.

It also states — Zhongjing (仲景, Zhang Zhongjing) said: "When one wishes to treat various diseases, one should first use a decoction (*tō*, boiled herbal preparation) to wash and clear the spaces between the five viscera (*gozō*) and six bowels (*roppu*), open and unblock all the channels (*myaku*, vessels), regulate and guide the *yin* and *yang*, flush out and break through the pathogenic evil (*ja*) from within, moisten and enrich what is desiccated and decaying (*kokō*), bring joy to the person's skin, and augment the person's vital energy (*kiryoku*). Water is capable of purifying all things — this is why decoctions are used. If the sick person's four limbs (*shishi*) are afflicted and wind-cold (*fūrei*) is stirred up, next one should use powder (*san*, powdered preparation). Powder is capable of expelling pathogenic wind-*qi* and damp-impediment (*fūki-shitsu-hi*), [conditions that] shift and move about through the exterior and interior without any fixed location — these should be levelled and settled with powder. Next, pills (*gan*, pill preparations) are used. Pills are capable of expelling wind-cold, breaking up accumulations and gatherings (*shakuju*, stagnant pathological accumulations in the body), dispersing all hard masses and concretions (*kenka*, hardened lumps/tumours), promoting the intake of food and drink, and regulating the *Yong* and *Wei* (営衛, *eiei*, the constructive and defensive *qi*). One who is capable of combining and employing all of these together may truly be called a superior physician (*jōkō*, a master practitioner). The word 'medicine' (*i*, 医) means 'intention' (*kokoro*, 意)."

[Interlinear note: Regarding "Yong and Wei" — the *Senkin-hō* (千金方, *Prescriptions Worth a Thousand Gold*) states: "*Yong* (*ei*) is the passage of *qi* through the *collateral vessels* (*rakumyaku*); *Wei* (*e*) is the passage of *qi* through the *main channels* (*keimyaku*). *Yong* emerges from the Middle Burner (*chūshō*); *Wei* emerges from the Upper Burner (*jōshō*)."']

### The *Yōjō Yōshū* (養生要集, *Essential Anthology on Nurturing Life*) states:

Zhang Zhongjing (張仲景) said: "When the human body is in a state of peaceful equilibrium (*heiwа*, harmonious balance), one should simply take good care of oneself (*shōyō*, nourishment and self-cultivation), and must not take medicine recklessly. The effect of medicine is partial (*katayori*, one-sided); in lending aid [in one direction], it will cause the visceral *qi* (*zōki*) of the person to become unbalanced, making one susceptible to receiving external afflictions (*gaikan*, illness caused by external pathogenic influences). Only those who are fasting from grains (*taniwa*) should take medicine regularly."

It also states — In the essay "On Taking Medicine" (*Ron Fuyaku*, 論服薬) by Xi Wu (郄悟), it is written: "In general, if one wishes to take medicinal preparations, one should first seek out what is appropriate to one's own nature and constitution (*seiri no yoroshiki tokoro*), and carefully discern what is suitable in terms of cooling and warming (*reidan no teki*, the proper balance of cooling and warming effects). One must not simply see someone else gaining benefit from [a medicine] and immediately take it oneself. When first administering medicine, begin with herbal (草, *kusa*, herbaceous plants) preparations, then proceed to woody (木, *ki*, arboreal/tree-derived) preparations, and then to mineral (石, *ishi*, stone/mineral) preparations. This is the general principle (*daikei*, broad guideline) of conducting medicine. This is what is meant by 'the refined and the coarse replacing one another, step by step' (*sei-so-sōdai*, ascending by stages…)"
