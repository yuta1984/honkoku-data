## [Section Three: The Proper Measure of Taking Medicine, Continued]

[Continuing from the previous page:] …[Those with lingering wind (*tai-fū*, 滞風) and hollow depletion (*dō-kyo*, 洞虚)] who have long taken scores of courses of medicine, or who require up to a hundred or more courses before they can recover — this is indeed how it must be. Nevertheless, one should increase or decrease [the medicine] as is appropriate (*zōson*, 増損), adjusting to the cold, warmth, dampness, and purgative condition (*kan-on shitsu-ri*, 寒温湿利) of that person's constitution.

The *Qianjin Fang* (千金方, *Prescriptions Worth a Thousand Gold*) states: In general, people who are forty years of age or below and who have illness should take purgative medicines (*sha-yaku*, 瀉薬); there is no great need for them to take restorative medicines (*ho-yaku*, 補薬). However, if there is definitely a place of injury or depletion, this rule does not apply. Those who are forty and above should not take purgative medicines; it is appropriate that they take restorative medicines. Those who are fifty and above should not go without restorative medicines throughout the four seasons. By following this practice, one may extend one's years and attain the arts of nourishing life (*yōjō no jutsu*, 養生の術).

It also states: If there are definitely accumulations and gatherings (*shakujū*, 積聚 — accumulations of *qi*, blood, and food in the viscera) in the organs, one must purge them regardless of whether one is young or old. If there is definitely deficiency and damage (*kyo-son*, 虚損), one must supplement regardless of whether one is young or old. Deliberate and reflect carefully (*shōryō*, 商量), and then use [the medicines] accordingly.

It also states: In both spring and autumn every year, it is appropriate to administer and take a purgative medicine just once each time, turning as occasion demands. By doing so, one will not be struck by the seasonal epidemic *qi* (*ji-ki*, 時気) that Heaven visits upon the world.

It also states: In general, when using medicines, one should always follow what is appropriate to the local land and soil. In the Jiangnan (江南) region and the area beyond the mountain ridges (*Reihyō*, 嶺表 — the far south), the land is hot and sweltering; the skin (*kifu*, 肌膚) is thin and delicate, and the pores and interstices of the skin (*sōri*, 腠理) are open and loose. Therefore, medicines used there should be light and sparing (*kei-sei*, 軽省). In Guanzhong (関中) and Hebei (河北), the land is elevated and dry (*kō-sō*, 堽燥); the people's skin is hard and firm (*ken-kō*, 堅硬), and the pores and interstices are closed and dense. Therefore, medicines used there should be heavy and plentiful (*jūfuku*, 重複 — applied in repeated, substantial doses).

It also states: When taking a purgative decoction (*ri-tō*, 痢湯), one wishes the medicine to penetrate quickly [into the gut]… [#1]

In general, when taking a decoction, one wishes to take it hot — as hot as a properly prepared decoction — and drink it down. Then it will easily be dissolved and descend (*shōka*, 消下), and one will not vomit. If it is cold, one will vomit and it will not descend. However, if it is excessively hot, it will damage one's throat (*inkou*, 咽喉). One must take care and apply one's attention to this matter. A decoction must be clear and settled (*chō-sei*, 澄清). If it is turbid, it will cause the person to feel stifled and the condition will not resolve (*tokenu*, 解けない).

The interval between doses should be approximately the time it takes to walk ten *ri* (十里). If the interval is extremely short and doses are taken in rapid succession [#2], the previous decoction will not yet have been digested before the next…
