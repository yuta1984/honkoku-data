## [Section Three: The Proper Measure of Taking Medicine, Continued]

[Continuing from the previous page:] …the [unconsumed] decoction will surge upward (*tsuki agaru*, 衝) and inevitably cause vomiting and counterflow (*tōgyaku*, 吐逆). Therefore, one should first inquire whether the patient's abdomen is in good health and whether the medicine has been digested and dispersed (*shōsan*, 消散) before proceeding with the next dose.

It also states: When taking a decoction, it is standard practice to divide three *shō* (升) into three doses. However, depending on the strength of the patient's grain *qi* (*kokuki*, 穀気 — that is, their digestive capacity and appetite), the first dose should always be the largest, the next dose should be somewhat smaller, and the final dose should be the smallest of all. By proceeding in this way, the patient will remain settled and at ease (*an'non*, 安穏).

It also states: When taking restorative medicines (*ho-yaku*, 補薬), it is desirable to consume a total of three and a half *shō* (升) [per day], taking three doses during the day and one at night, with meals interspersed between each dose. By doing so, the vapor of the decoction (*tō no ki*, 湯気 — the medicinal *qi*) will diffuse and irrigate (*gaikan*, 漑灌) all one hundred vessels (*hyakumyaku*, 百脈 — that is, all the channels and meridians throughout the body), making it easy to receive the power of the medicine (*yakuryoku*, 薬力). If proceeding in this manner, one must keep the pace very gradual (*yuruku*, 緩) and must not hasten or rush (*sokkyū*, 速急).

Furthermore, [after taking the restorative medicine,] one should lie on one's left side, then one's right side, then face up, then face down, spending approximately the time of one meal interval (*isshokukei*, 一食頃) in each position, so that the momentum of the decoction's effect may permeate and circulate throughout the abdomen. One should also walk around within the room, covering a total of approximately one hundred steps. Do not go outdoors for the entire day. [By doing all this,] there will be great benefit.

It also states: For three days after taking a decoction, one should abstain from alcohol (*sake*, 酒). This is because decoctions are incompatible with alcohol.

It also states: After taking one dose of a decoction for treating *fū* disorders (*chifū-tō*, 治風湯 — wind ailment decoctions), one should cover oneself thickly and induce perspiration. Upon obtaining perspiration, one should immediately lighten the covering so as not to cause excessive sweating (*taikan*, 大汗). Between doses, one should also intersperse meals. Failure to do so will cause a person to lose their strength, and will further increase their depletion and wasting (*kyo-rui*, 虚羸).

It also states: When taking decoctions and medicines, all accompanying gruel, food, and vegetables (*na*, 菜) should always be thoroughly cooked and well done. When they are well cooked, they are easy to digest and will be mutually agreeable with the medicine. If they are raw or undercooked, they will be difficult to digest and will also impair the efficacy of the medicine. Accordingly, it is best to eat a small amount of vegetables (*na*, 菜) when taking the medicine. It is also good to take a little salt and vinegar (*su*, 酢) [with the meal]. Furthermore, one must not exert oneself greatly in mental and physical effort (*shinryoku*, 心力), engage in sexual activity (*bōshitsu*, 房室), or give rise to intense emotions (*kido*, 喜怒 — joy and anger).
