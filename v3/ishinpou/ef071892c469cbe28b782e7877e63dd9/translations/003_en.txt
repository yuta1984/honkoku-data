**[Continuation of Chapter 1: General Principles of Treating Disease]**

[In the blood vessels and *shu* acupoints] there are differences of high and low, shallow and deep; in the skin, flesh, sinews, and bones there are differences of thick and thin, hard and soft. Only those who apply their minds with utmost precision and subtlety can begin to participate in this [art of medicine]. To seek matters of the utmost precision and subtlety through thought that is utterly coarse and utterly shallow—would that not be dangerous indeed?

If, when a patient is replete, one supplements further; when deficient, one still attacks; when the flow is open, one opens it further; when blocked, one blocks it further still; when there is cold, one cools; when there is heat, one warms—this is to aggravate the illness, and even if one wishes for the patient's survival, I [the author] foresee only their death. Therefore, among medicine, divination, and the various arts, there is none in which it is harder to achieve true mastery. If one has not been granted [insight] by Heaven, how can one ever attain its hidden subtleties?

It is also said: When a great physician treats illness, he must always calm his spirit, settle his will, be free of desires and cravings, and first arouse a heart of great compassion and empathy, vowing to save all living beings from their afflictions. Should someone come, suffering from illness and seeking relief, one must not inquire into their noble or humble status, their wealth or poverty, their age, their beauty or ugliness, whether they are enemies or kin, close friends or strangers, Chinese or foreign, wise or foolish. One must treat all equally and universally, as if each were one's most beloved relation. Nor should one look ahead to one's own advantage or be timid about what lies behind, calculating one's own fortune or misfortune, and guarding one's own life and safety [at the expense of the patient]. Upon seeing the suffering of another, one must feel it deeply in one's heart as though the affliction were one's own—never avoiding rough and dangerous roads, day or night, in cold or heat, following [the patient's call] as a shadow follows a form or an echo answers a sound, heedless of hunger, thirst, and exhaustion, going with single-minded devotion to give aid, and never acting with a mind set on [making a show of] one's merit or leaving traces of one's deeds.

It is further said: Even if gorgeous silks and brocades fill your eyes, do not let your gaze wander left and right to admire them. Even if the sound of strings and pipes is played for your ears, do not allow yourself to appear to be seeking pleasure in it. Even if rare and exquisite delicacies are offered at the table, eat as though they were without flavor. Even if fine wines and foods of all kinds are laid out before you, [remain equally unmoved]. The reason one must be thus is this: if even one person weeps alone in the corner, an entire hall full of people cannot be at ease in their enjoyment. How much more so when someone is ill…
