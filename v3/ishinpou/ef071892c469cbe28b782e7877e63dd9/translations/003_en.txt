## [Continuing from the previous page]

In the skin and muscles and bones, there are differences of high and low, shallow and deep, and variations of thick and thin, hard and soft. Only one who applies the mind with utmost precision and subtlety can begin to participate in [the art of medicine]. If one attempts to understand matters of the utmost precision and subtlety through thought that is exceedingly coarse and exceedingly shallow, is that not perilous indeed?

If one adds to what is already full, restrains what is already empty, drives through what is already flowing, blocks up what is already obstructed, cools what is already cold, and warms what is already hot—this is to aggravate the illness, and even if one hopes that the patient will live, I foresee only their death. Thus, among [all arts including] medicine, divination, and the various arts and skills, [medicine] is the most difficult in which to attain mastery.

If it is not divinely bestowed, how can one attain its subtle and profound depths?

The text further states: "When a great physician treats illness, he must always calm his spirit, settle his will, harbor neither desire nor personal ambition, and first give rise to a heart of great compassion and empathy, vowing to relieve the sufferings of all living beings far and wide. If there is one who comes afflicted with illness seeking relief, one must not ask about their rank or station, wealth or poverty, age, wisdom or foolishness, whether they are an enemy or a friend, an intimate or a stranger, a fellow countryman or a foreigner, clever or dull—but regard them all equally and universally, as if they were one's own dearest kin.

Moreover, one must not look ahead while glancing behind, calculating one's own fortune or misfortune, or guarding and treasuring one's own life and safety. Upon seeing the suffering of another, one should feel deep anguish in one's heart as if the affliction were one's own. One must not avoid rough or dangerous terrain, and regardless of day or night, cold or heat, like a shadow responding instantly to a form, even when hungry, thirsty, weary, and exhausted, one must go wholeheartedly to the rescue, and must not act with any thought of making a show of one's merit or deeds.

The text further states: Upon arriving [at a patient's home]…even if beautiful silks and finery fill the eyes, do not look left and right to admire them. Even if the sounds of strings and woodwinds are played for the ears, do not behave as though you are there to enjoy yourself. Even when rare and exquisite delicacies are offered and pressed upon you at table, eat as though they were tasteless. Even when a lavish array of fine foods and drink is spread before you, behave as though something were amiss [and you cannot partake freely].

It is said that when even one person sits weeping in a corner, an entire hall full of people cannot be merry. How much more so when a patient [is suffering]…" [continues on next page]
