## [General Principles of Purgation, Continued]

In cases of *rinbyō* (淋病, urinary difficulty/strangury), and after sweating when [the condition] is not resolved, the abdomen is full, or there is pain — it is appropriate to purge.

As a general rule, in those who are ill and have fullness and pain (*mantsū*) in the abdomen, this constitutes a cold (*kan*) condition; one should appropriately purge them. Even if the abdominal fullness does not decrease, or decreases only insignificantly, one should always purge them. Those whose pulse is rapid (*saku*) and slippery (*katsu*) have retained food (*shukushoku*); purge them, and they will recover.

As a general rule, for those in whom purgation is indicated, one uses the formula of *Jōki-tō* (承気湯, Order-the-Qi Decoction).

> **Formula for *Jōki-tō*:**
> *Daiō* (大黄, Rheum/rhubarb root) 4 *ryō* [soaked separately overnight]; *Kōboku* (厚朴, Magnolia bark) 2 *ryō*, roasted and sliced. Decoct in 5 *shō* of water, reducing to 1.5 *shō*. Add the *daiō*, bring to one or two more boils, remove the dregs, divide into two doses, and take. One should expect two or three bowel movements (*ri*), after which recovery follows.

It further says: In the summer months, one must not purge.

As a general rule, when a patient is wheezing (*aegi*, dyspnoea) and the chest is full, one must not purge them.

As a general rule, when a patient's epigastric region (*mune no moto*, below the heart) is hard, the neck and nape (*kubiunaji*) are stiff and rigid, and there is dizziness, one must not purge them.

As a general rule, those with *ketsugyaku* (厥逆, reversal-cold of the extremities with counterflow qi) must not be purged. Those who are deficient (*kyo*, vacuous) are likewise [contraindicated for purgation].

As a general rule, those who are ill and about to vomit must not be purged.

As a general rule, those who are ill and in whom the exterior manifestations (*gaishō*, exterior pattern/syndrome) have not yet resolved must not be purged.

As a general rule, those who are ill with abdominal fullness and are vomiting food — if one purges them, [the condition] will become even more severe.

As a general rule, in greatly emaciated people who have pain encircling the navel (*hoso*, umbilicus), there is certainly retained grain-qi (*kokuki*, food accumulation); if one purges them instead [of using the appropriate treatment], their qi will inevitably surge upward.

> **[Marginal annotation at top, note on *hi* (痞, epigastric glomus):]**
> "*Ho*, *hi*, *hei* [補被平]. As a general rule, when [qi] is instead bound up inside the abdomen and causes illness — the region below the heart (*mune no moto*) becomes *hi* (痞, blocked/congested; a sensation of obstruction and resistance in the epigastrium)."

As a general rule, when sweating is excessive, one loses one's *yō* (陽, yang qi). Those who speak deliriously (*tawakoto*, incoherent raving; delirium) must not be purged.

## Section Two: Signs of Incurable Diseases (*Shobyō Fuchi Shō*, 諸病不治証)

The *Imon-hō* (医門方, *Medical Gateway Formulary*) states, and the discourse (*ron*) says:

When a person has a disease, it always arises from the viscera and bowels (*zōfu*, the five viscera and six bowels). The signs (*shirushi*, indicators) of life and death are revealed in the countenance and complexion (*yōshoku*, facial appearance and skin colour). Just as a shadow invariably follows a form and a sound invariably follows its source — the response is certain and without any deviation or error. One must examine [these signs] carefully. In ten thousand cases, not a single one will be missed. The specific external signs and symptoms (*keishō*) [pertaining to these conditions] are listed in full in the sections that follow.
