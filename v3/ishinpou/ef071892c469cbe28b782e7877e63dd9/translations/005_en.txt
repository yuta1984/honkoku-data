**[Translation of the current page, continuing Chapter 1: General Principles of Treating Disease]**

…[A physician who,] when sweating is not needed, forcibly induces sweating, will cause the patient's *jin-ye* (bodily fluids and moisture) to be expelled and exhausted, leading to death. One who does not induce sweating when sweating is needed will cause all the pores to become blocked and obstructed, bringing the patient to a state of suffocation and death. Furthermore, one who does not administer a purgative when purging is needed will cause the patient's chest and interior to become afflicted with anguish and distress, abdominal fullness and distension, mental agitation, and edema, leading to death. One who forcibly administers a purgative when it is not needed will cause the patient's intestines to open uncontrollably [in unrelenting diarrhea], leading to death. Furthermore, one who forcibly applies moxibustion when moxibustion is not needed will cause the pathogenic fire (*huo xie*) to enter the patient's intestines, throwing the five viscera into disorder, further adding to the patient's distress and torment, leading to death. One who does not apply moxibustion when moxibustion is needed will allow cold congelation to accumulate and congeal ever more solidly over time, causing the qi to surge upward, striking the heart, with no possibility for the evil influence to dissipate and disperse—the illness will become critical and the patient will die.

The *Bencao Jing* (Classic of Materia Medica) states: In general, when one wishes to treat an illness, one must first clearly discern its source and root, and first observe the pathodynamic (*bìng jī*)—the mechanism by which the illness is progressing. While the five viscera have not yet become deficient, the six bowels have not yet become exhausted, the blood and vessels have not yet fallen into disorder, and the spirit and vitality have not yet scattered—if medicine is taken at this stage, the patient can certainly be saved. However, if the illness has already become fully established, recovery [becomes difficult]…; if the force of the illness has already passed the point of no return, it will be even more difficult to restore the patient to wholeness.

It is also said: Furthermore, one should observe the patient's deficiency or excess (*xu-shi*) and apply supplementing or draining treatments (*bu-xie*) accordingly. Men and women [differ], as do [those of ease and hardship, of flourishing and decline,] the local climate and soil, and customs and practices—none of these is the same. [The physician] Chu Cheng, in treating widows [and nuns and monks], used different methods from those used for wives and concubines. This is because he had fully grasped what their particular natures and dispositions (*xìng huái*) brought about.

The *Taisu Jing* (Grand Basis Classic) states: The Yellow Emperor asked Qi Bo: "When a physician treats illness, if for a single illness the treatments used are each different, yet all result in recovery, why is this so?" Qi Bo replied: "It is the configuration of the land that makes it so. Therefore, the eastern region, according to the principle of the earth, is where [all things] first come into being. It is a land of fish and salt, situated along the sea and beside the water…"
