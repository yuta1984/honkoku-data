## [Section Three: The Proper Measure of Taking Medicine, Continued]

[Continuing from the previous page:] …all those who eat [after taking purgative decoctions, pills, powders, medicinal wines, and the like] should first be given one mouthful of cold vinegared rice (*saku-han*, 冷酢飯), and only then may they proceed to eat a full meal. This is the best approach.

It also states: All pill medicines (*ganyaku*, 丸薬) should be made to the size of *goshi* (梧子 — the seed of the Chinese parasol tree, *Firmiana simplex*). For restorative (*ho*, 補) medicines, begin with ten pills, and gradually increase from ten, not exceeding forty pills as the upper limit. To go beyond this is excessive. Even for those with debilitated constitutions (*kyojin*, 虚人), administer [the medicine] three times a day. The ideal is to be able to continue taking the medicine over many days and for a long period without interruption (*yaku wo kakesazu*, 薬を欠かさず), so that the medicinal *qi* (*yakki*, 薬気) gradually accumulates and permeates and steams (*kunju*, 燻蒸) through the five viscera (*gozō*, 五臓). It is best to take the medicine over a prolonged period of time. It is not good practice to take it all at once (*tonpuku*, 頓服) and exhaust the supply quickly. To squander fine medicines (*meiyaku*, 名薬) in this way is to receive very little benefit.

It also states: When taking purgative pills (*sha-gan*, 瀉丸), the measure (*do*, 度) should be to take no more than what produces loose stools (*ri*, 痢). Take care never to exceed this. [To exceed it] will cause a person to suffer uncontrolled diarrhea (*kengeri*, 限り無き下痢) and will greatly harm the person.

It also states: When a person suddenly encounters the onset of a wind (*fū*, 風) [disorder — i.e., stroke or wind-induced ailment], and the body and mind suddenly deteriorate severely (*totsu ni aku naru*, 頓に悪なる), or the person becomes unable to speak, such a person should be given decoctions such as the Greater and Lesser Life-Prolonging Decoctions (*Daishō Zokumei-tō*, 大小続命湯), the Western Province Life-Prolonging Decoction (*Seishū Zokumei-tō*, 西州続命湯), the Peach Wind Decoction (*Tōfū-tō*, 桃風湯), and the Maidservant's Decoction from Beyond the Pass (*Eppi-tō*, 越婢湯). These should be administered within a draught-free, sealed room (*misshitsu*, 密室), four to five times per day and night, without counting the number of doses, and without worrying about the [patient's] weakened state (*kyo*, 虚). It is best to keep the head, face, hands, feet, abdomen, and back continuously perspiring without cease.

Each time the decoction is digested and absorbed, immediately give gruel (*kayu*, 粥) to eat; and each time the gruel is digested, immediately administer the decoction again. One may also give a small portion of mutton broth (*hitsuji no kaku*, 羊臛 — a thick meat soup of lamb) to supplement and nourish [the patient].

For those in whom the wind [disorder] is especially severe, continue administering the decoction for five days and five nights without interruption; then, after passing two days, stop the decoction, and use fine broth and thick soup (*bi-kōkaku*, 美羹臛) to nourish and restore the patient to a state of recovery and rest (*shōsoku*, 消息). If [the patient's] four limbs and body (*shitai*, 四体) show some recovery (*ie*, 差), immediately cease the medicine and gradually allow the patient to rest and recuperate (*shōsoku*, 将息). If there is no recovery, one should again administer the decoction to combat the disease, and the patient's recovery (*iyuru*, 差ゆる) shall serve as the measure [for when to stop].
