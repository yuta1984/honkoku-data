## [Section Three: The Proper Measure of Taking Medicine, Continued]

[Continuing from the previous page:] …the methods of treatment are not the same. When the pathogenic influence (*ja*, 邪) is still in the *háomáo* (豪毛, the body hair and pores / the most superficial level of the body), it is appropriate to take an ointment (*kō*, 膏) and also to rub (*ma*, 摩) it onto [the affected area]. If one does not treat it, within twenty days it will enter the *grandson vessels* (*son-myaku*, 孫脈). It is appropriate to take medicinal wine (*yakushu*, 薬酒). Medicinal wine is a well-ripened liquid (*jukueki*, 熟液); it first flows swiftly through the skin. Therefore, when the medicinal *qi* follows the momentum of the wine and enters the *grandson vessels*, the pathogenic *qi* is dispersed.

If one does not treat it, within forty days it will enter the *network vessels* (*raku-myaku*, 絡脈). It is appropriate to take a decoction (*tō*, 湯). If one does not treat it, within sixty days it will transmit and enter the *channel vessels* (*kei-myaku*, 経脈). It is appropriate to take a powdered medicine (*san*, 散). If one does not treat it, within eighty days it will enter the viscera and bowels (*zō-fu*, 蔵府). It is appropriate to take pills (*gan*, 丸). [A condition that has persisted for] one hundred days or more is called a *deep-seated chronic illness* (*chin-a*, 沈痾). It is appropriate to take a *decocted preparation* (*sen*, 煎).

It also states: In general, when taking *supplementing decoctions* (*ho-tō*, 補湯), one should take them over a long interval — taking them over a prolonged period with wide intervals between doses. When taking *draining decoctions* (*sha-tō*, 写湯), the interval should be short [i.e., doses taken in closer succession].

The *Shōhin-hō* (*Xiǎopǐn Fāng*, 小品方) states: In general, when a person's illness is severe, the person is necessarily weakened. If the person is weakened, they cannot then endure a [heavy] medicinal prescription. It is appropriate to use [formulas in which] the quantities (*bun-ryō*, 分両) are simple and reduced (*tan-shō*, 単省).

When the illness is mild, the person is then strong and can endure the medicinal prescription. It is appropriate to use [formulas in which] the quantities are heavy and compounded (*jū-fuku*, 重複).

In general, those with a prolonged illness (*kyū-byō*, 久病) have accumulated many months and years [of sickness], and have necessarily suffered loss of appetite; the strength derived from food is already weakened, and they too cannot endure a heavy medicinal prescription. It is also appropriate to use [formulas in which] the quantities are simple and reduced.

Those who have fallen newly ill have been ill for only a short time; even though their food intake may have suffered, the *grain qi* (*koku-ki*, 穀気 — the vital energy derived from food and drink) has not yet become depleted, and they can still endure a [heavier] medicinal prescription. It is also appropriate to use [formulas in which] the quantities are heavy and compounded.

For those who are young and vigorous (*shō-sō*, 少壮), even when the illness is serious, since the person is in their prime and their *qi* and blood are abundant, they can endure a heavy medicinal prescription. It is appropriate to use [formulas in which] the quantities are heavy and compounded. Even if a person is one of ease and comfort (*yū-raku*, 優楽 — one living an affluent and leisurely life), if that person falls ill frequently and takes medicine frequently, then it becomes difficult to establish the potency of a medicinal prescription [because the body becomes accustomed to medicine]. In such cases too, it is appropriate to proceed in this manner [i.e., with simpler, reduced quantities].
