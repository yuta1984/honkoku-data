— and with those helpless, throwaway words, he made his hasty departure. Left behind, Jūbei drew a long, deep sigh and turned the matter over and over in his mind. There could be no doubt about his son's change of heart. For if it were otherwise — the roster drawn up and recorded on all sides lists forty-seven riders, not one name different. The fact that neither "Oyamada" nor "Shōzaemon" appears among them leaves no doubt: blinded by greed, his heart corrupted, the boy had absconded. And all this while, not knowing any of it, Jūbei had heard the rumor of the vendetta raid and boasted proudly, even volunteering his own son's name himself. The shame of it was beyond all words. For a man of his age, not knowing from one moment to the next when death might come — to go on living after this disgrace would be unthinkable. Without dying, there could be no excuse. So deeply had Jūbei brooded himself into this resolve. How pitiful the depths of his heart. Gosuke, being a dull-witted

fellow, said what he had to say and went on his way home. Next door, at the familiar sundry goods shop, the people gathered there were talking of nothing other than the same subject — the vendetta. They were chattering away about it in their various ways, and among their talk, they began to whisper about the story of Oyamada that they had heard before. Just at that moment, as one of them happened to glance back toward the wall that adjoined the neighboring house — a gleaming sword blade came thrusting through the wall with a terrible lurch. Everyone cried out in alarm and fell into an uproar. In the midst of the commotion, Kohei, who lived in that house, rushed over to Jūbei's side — and what a sight met his eyes. Jūbei lay upon the floor, having attempted to cut open his belly but failing to complete the cut; he had then leaned against the wall and driven the blade up through his own throat, and the tip of the sword had pierced clean through to Kohei's side of the wall.
