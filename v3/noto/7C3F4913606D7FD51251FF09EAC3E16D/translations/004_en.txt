The splendors of Noto are so numerous and remarkable, yet though there is no shortage of those who travel there shallowly, without someone who records and describes them, one cannot truly be said to have exhausted their depths. Now, having encompassed and examined all of these (splendors), gathering them without omission into a single volume, (the author) may rightly be called one who has truly and thoroughly given them their due account.

Sixth month of Bunksei Kōshin (1820), Eastern Capital — Stone inscription by Ishihara Masushima Koasen

**Noto Yūki (A Record of Travels in Noto)**
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Composed by Kakumura (Tsurumura) Gen'yūhi

Since the year Kisai (癸歳), I have cherished a deep love for the singular beauty of mountains and waters. Whenever leisure permitted, I left no mountain or stream in the vicinity of my home province unvisited, penetrating deep into each and every one. Now, among the great northern landmarks of imposing and sacred character, Hakusan (Mount Haku) stands foremost; and among places abounding in remarkable scenery, Noto is foremost. I therefore desired to ascend Hakusan and to travel through Noto. Hakusan is not far from my home province — it can be reached at its summit within three nights' journey. Thus I first climbed Hakusan three times, lodging there for several days; and in its various quarters of outstanding scenery, heedless of any difficulty or険terrain, I reached every corner, made maps of all of them, and recorded the facts therein, compiling the "Hakusan Shi" (Records of Hakusan) in eight volumes. I then desired further to ascend Mount Tateyama and to travel through Noto, whereupon Bidō admonished me, saying: "As for the pleasures of traveling among mountains and streams..."
