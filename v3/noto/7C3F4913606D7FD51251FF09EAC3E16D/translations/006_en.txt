(The lord's) exceptional generosity was something I ultimately could not refuse, and so I found myself unable to withdraw from his service. My age was approaching sixty, and the hair at my temples was about to turn white. I sighed and said to myself: "I have grown old. The steep heights of Mt. Tateyama are beyond my ability to climb any longer. If only I could travel to Noto and fulfill my long-cherished wish, that would be enough." I therefore made a petition to my lord, requesting leave to journey to Noto. My lord took pity on my aspiration and graciously granted me sixty days of leave. At this, I was at last able to repay my old ambition, and my joy was beyond all words.

This year, in the third month of Heishi (丙子), a prolonged rain continued for over ten days, and so I had not yet fixed a date of departure. But spring was already on the verge of ending, and my heart was anxious and impatient — I had no leisure to wait for the skies to clear. At last I settled upon the twenty-first day. For this journey, several companions requested to accompany me; however, considering that the presence of companions might mean that neither coming nor going would go as one wished, I declined them all. I had only a single manservant carry the baggage, and nothing more.

On the night of the twentieth, friends from the poetry society came to bid me farewell. We composed verses and shared cups of wine until the middle of the night, and then dispersed.

On the twenty-first day, as I was about to set off, my lord sent a close attendant with gifts of silver and dried fish (provisions for the road) as a send-off. He also sent word: "There is no need for you to come and offer thanks in person." I bowed twice in deep prostration and reverently received the gifts. At the hour of the Dragon (approximately 7–9 a.m.), I took my leave of the household and passed through the gate. The rain had still not cleared; the peach and plum blossoms along the street were emerging over the fences and pushing through the walls, hanging low and drooping, like none other than the beauty Xishi (西施) pressing her hand to her ailing heart. Once past the castle, the rain gradually let up, and the rapeseed flowers by the waterfall and the cherry trees among the branches appeared dimly, half-hidden within a thin mist.

At noon, I arrived at the home of Shinozuka Koku in the village of Katakata (潟畑). Koku had once studied under me; overjoyed at my arrival, he brewed tea and set out sweets. We passed the time in leisurely conversation, and before long I felt quite fatigued and drowsy. I bent my arm for a pillow and rested awhile. Upon waking, I composed a quatrain:

　　The poet's couch shifts beneath the shade of a hundred flowers in bloom;
　　A midday nap upon a single pillow — born of the toil of hard-wrought verse.
　　The warbler calls to me — for what purpose, I wonder?
　　The dream dissolves: the spring garden lies hushed in the deep stillness of afternoon.
