(Continued from previous page) In a suguha (straight hamon) tachi, the kaeri (turnback of the bōshi temper) is not split open by tempering, and it resembles the tip style of Bizen Province swords. On katana with suguha, the bōshi is flatter than that of Kunitoshi, and the kaeri is tempered with a thick, split finish. The color of the jihane (surface metal) appears pale and somewhat coarse. When tempering the midareba (irregular hamon) on a katana, it appears as though great care was also put into the forging (kitae), and both the ji (surface) and ha (edge) are beautiful. Many fine swords are tempered with keiki (an energetic, lively quality) in the hamon. In this work as well, the midareba may be from the smith's younger years, as the kitae is somewhat coarse. Compared to Kunitoshi, there is more unevenness (mura) in the suguha, which does not accord with a fine jifū (surface character), and this is an undesirable quality.

There is one theory that he was the father of Kuniyuki, but this should not be accepted. They were contemporaries. It has been heard that they lent tachi to one another and struck each other's signatures (mei) on them when the occasion called for it. Even in his association with Sadatoshi, the form (sugata) of these swords is more refined (saeru) than Kuniyuki's middle-period work. There are several separate certifications (kiwame) for this. (End of oral transmission)

One entry: Kuninaga was active during the reign of Emperor Go-Fukakusa-in, around the Kōan era (1278–1288), and was a disciple of Kunitoshi, known as Nakajima.
