There are many tachi in which Hisanobu (久信) forged blades and struck the mei (signature) "Ryōkai" in imitation (forgery). Being father and son, he likely understood the essence of the forging techniques. And yet (the forgeries) resemble (the originals) well. Nevertheless, there are many points of difference in the use of the tōgane (chisel) for the mei, the treatment of the nakago (tang), the shishi-oki (flesh placement on the tang), and other details below. Moreover, the father's (Ryōkai's) nakago is deeper. Hisanobu's forgeries have a sharp and tight habuchi (hamon edge), and the kitae (forging texture) of the steel also has a stiff, hard quality. There are many tachi in which the kenpon (sword base) serves as a dividing point (kiri). Though father and son alike, both are skilled in the carving of hi (grooves). Further oral transmission exists.

One entry: Nobukuni (信国) [interlinear note: lower (rank)] — active during the reign of Go-en'yū-in (Emperor Go-en'yū), around the Ōei era (1394–1428). He is the grandson of Ryōkai.

The sugata (form) of the tachi is short, with a high sori (curvature). The kitae (forging) is masame (straight grain), though there are also areas resembling itame (wood-grain pattern), and mokume-hada (burl wood-grain pattern) is also present. The iori-mine (ridged back) is shallow. The ji color is white, with a hazy, swirling (urometaku) quality, as though smoke were rising. The hamon is tempered in both ō-midare (large irregular pattern) and notari-midare (gentle undulating irregular pattern). Niē (hard martensite crystals) are abundant. In suguha (straight hamon), the ji is fine and refined. The ji color also has a darkish hue, the hamon color is likewise dark, with the hard quality of steel.
