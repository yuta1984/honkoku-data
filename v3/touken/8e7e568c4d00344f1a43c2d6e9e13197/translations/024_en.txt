In the broad areas of the midare-ha (irregular hamon), the impression of where the tsuchioki (clay application for tempering) was removed remains as it is, and the niē (hard martensite crystals) appear separately at the hamon's edge (kiwа). In those areas, the niē are also fine and shallow. The hamon edge itself is sharp.

There are three generations sharing the same mei (signature) "Nobukuni." As for the points of appraisal described above: the uchi (blade-making / sword-striking quality) is skillful. The father (second generation) is slightly inferior. The grandson (third generation) is less skilled. The middle (second-generation) Nobukuni does not have ō-midare (large irregular hamon). The grandson (third generation) also tempers hiro-ha (wide hamon), but the jitetsu (surface texture of the blade) is poor. Overall, all three generations are skillful in terms of kirimono (cutting ability / overall blade quality). Most notably, the uchi (striking / sword-making) is outstanding. Forgeries (copies) of the mei of all three generations exist.

One entry: Kunitomo (国友) [interlinear note: Jōjō go-mono (the finest among imperial possessions)] — active during the reign of Go-Toba-in (Retired Emperor Go-Toba), around the Genryaku era (1184–1185). From Awataguchi (粟田口). Fujibayashi (藤林) [school/lineage name].

The sugata (form) of the tachi is slender, and the kissaki (tip) is also modest and refined. The kasane (blade thickness at the spine) is thick, and the iori-mine (ridged back) is deep. The kitae (forging) is masame (straight grain), and is exceedingly fine and refined. The ji color is a gleaming, clear blue. The sori (curvature) of the tachi is higher than average overall, and the shinogi (ridgeline) is slightly narrow. The hamon color is dark, with a slight bluish tinge on the outer edge, appearing □ (hard/solid?)...
