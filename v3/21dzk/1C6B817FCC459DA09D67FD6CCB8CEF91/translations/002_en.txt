### [Upper Section] An Inscription for the *Ritsuen Sōhō Den*

The Vinaya Master Kaizanzan Kenkō of Kotō was a man of broad learning with great aspirations. He was once moved to lament that in this latter age, many among the clergy, though possessed of wisdom and understanding, tended to neglect the rules and norms of the precepts (the "plumb-line"). He therefore compiled the *Ritsuen Sōhō Den* in fifteen volumes, with the intention of causing its readers to discipline their minds, sweep away old habits, and become pure and undefiled bhikṣus (monks). How could his achievement be considered shallow or insignificant? I admire his aspiration and have composed this verse (gāthā) for him:

*Skillfully raising the banner of the Vinaya, he plants it at the gateway to the sea;*
*I know that you, my friend, have long been numbered among the assembly at Jeta Grove.*
*There is no doubt that a successor to the Venerable Upāli has appeared;*
*A descendant (dharma-grandson) of the Great Master Chōzhào (Dàoxuān, Dōsen) now exists.*
*Rich in years and pure in virtue — who would not record this?*
*Steadfast in will and replete in learning — what more need I say?*
*He himself has compiled a history of monks within the Brahmic garden,*
*To shine forever as a lamp of light, dispelling the great darkness.*

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　Written by the Mountain Hermit of Butsukoku Kōsen

### [Lower Section] Author's Preface

The Tamer of the World (the Buddha) in the Central Heavens manifested birth and expounded the Dharma for forty-nine years. The vehicles through which the Correct Dharma flows and circulates are the Three Baskets (Tripiṭaka). These are called the Sūtras, the Vinaya, and the Abhidharma. The sūtras can be expounded by all Buddhas, bodhisattvas, devas, and sages alike. The Abhidharma can be composed and written by all worthy sages and monks. But the Vinaya alone was personally proclaimed by the Tathāgata with his golden mouth; even those of great authority and influence (avatārs and emanations) could not add a single word to it. When the Tathāgata demonstrated extinction at the Grove of Cranes (the śāla grove where the Buddha died), the Venerable Upāli recited the holy words from memory, and through the council of compilation they were gathered into a Piṭaka. This is called the Root Section, the Eighty-Recitation Vinaya, the Great Vinaya Piṭaka (*Mahāvinaya*). Compared to the first century of the Correct Dharma, it branched into five sections (pañca-nikāya). Later it further divided and branched still further, becoming the twelve, eighteen, and five-hundred various sections. Although their practical arrangements appear to differ in some respects, the means by which they subdue the afflictions (defilements) and cause one to ascend to Wondrous Enlightenment (*miaojue*) is one and the same. This is what is meant by the saying: "These various views do not obstruct the Dharmadhātu of all Buddhas and Great Nirvāṇa." Alas, unless one possesses great wisdom and is filled with great capacity, how could one be equal to the responsibility of this path?

Long ago, during the Jiāpíng era of Cao Wei (249–254 CE), Dharmakāla, a sixth-generation dharma-descendant of Kāśyapa (the "Drinking-Light"), came from India (Sindhu) and performed the full-ordination *karma* (ritual act of ordination) at Luoyang. It was from this that China (Zhēndān) first came to know the existence of Vinaya discipline (*śīlācāra*). Thereafter, those who "received the seal of the precepts from above" became as numerous as a forest. Among those praised as "fine successors," only Fǎcōng of Běitāi stands out. After Fǎcōng came Dàofù, who carried on his legacy. After Dàofù came Huìguāng, who continued the tradition. After Huìguāng came Dàoyún, who inherited it. From Dàoyún, after three transmissions, (the lineage) reached Zhìshǒu of Hóngfú (Temple). The foremost disciple of Zhìshǒu was called the Great Master Chéngzhào of Nánshān (the Vinaya Master Dàoxuān of the Southern Mountain). Relying on the meaning of the "Opening and Harmonizing" (Kaiе) teachings of the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, the Great Master proclaimed and clarified the essential purport of the school, standing singularly independent, and became the teacher of gods and humans of an entire age.

Thus the Way of the Dharma-Secret (the Vinaya) spread and filled all within the four seas. In our land of Japan, during the Tenpyō Shōhō era (749–757), the Great Master's dharma-grandson, the Venerable Ryūkō-shin (Ganjin, Jianzhen), responded to an invitation and crossed eastward to Japan, first transmitting the single lamp (of the Vinaya lineage).

Emperor Shōmu issued an imperial decree establishing the great monastery of Tōdai-ji as the standard and model for the entire realm. Those who hastened to study there were like water flowing powerfully downward. From that time onward, monks of outstanding brilliance and extraordinary ability arose one after another, proclaiming and spreading the Dharma of the Vinaya (*Vinaya-dharma*), becoming treasures of the nation, in no way inferior to India (Brahmin land) or China. Even today, their fame and reputation are brilliantly manifest between heaven and earth, shining resplendently like the sun and stars. How magnificent it all is!

I have at times examined the monastic histories of the three dynasties (of China) as well as the records of Buddhism in our own land, and have found that the great virtuous masters of our tradition are beyond counting one or two at a time. Yet I have never seen a single comprehensive and authoritative transmission covering the school as a whole. Could it not be that we are close to possessing an incomplete canon? Moreover, since the Genkō era (1321–1324) more than three hundred years have passed, and during that time one cannot say there were no worthy persons. Nevertheless, it is truly to be lamented that their excellent deeds and conduct have been fading away day by day. If we who call ourselves their descendants do not devote thought to this, to whom would the responsibility of upholding the tradition belong?

For this reason, I have broadly searched the historical records and chronicles of China and Japan, as well as various separate records and fragmentary writings; I have also sought out the remaining virtuous deeds in mountain forests and villages, and rubbed (made rubbings of) broken stele overgrown with grass and weeds. Beginning from Dharmakāla, I obtained (accounts of) more than three hundred persons, composed individual biographies for each, appended eulogies (*zan*), divided (the whole) into fifteen volumes, and entitled it the *Ritsuen Sōhō Den* ("Biographies of the Treasure-Monks of the Vinaya Garden"). I privately feel ashamed of my declining and feeble brushwork; although my intentions exceed what is written, the writing is insufficient to fully draw forth the profound and hidden brilliance of the former sages. Yet the single thought of upholding the tradition made it impossible for me to desist.

The *Book of Changes* says: "Broadly acquaint yourself with the former words and past deeds (of the sages), in order to cultivate your virtue." This compilation consists entirely of the former words and past deeds of the Vinaya Garden. Should there be any gentlemen who aspire to emulate Yan Hui or admire Lin Xiangru, and who, savoring (this text) without forgetting it, earnestly look up to the models set by the former worthies and change their course to follow them, then the true spirit (*shinpū*) of the successive patriarchs will surely be restored in the present day. My hope is that (we will)
