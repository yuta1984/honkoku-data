Regarding "The text: 'This master's intent says,' up to 'is not common characteristics' ": This is text added by the chapter author (*章家*). The term "expedient means" (*方便*) refers to the position of the preparatory mind (*加行心*) before entering meditative concentration (*定*). One first abides at this position [Note: the twelve characters beginning with "先住" (*first abiding*) are absent in one edition] and cognizes common characteristics; thereafter, upon entering the state of concentration, one cognizes only particular characteristics and does not cognize common characteristics. Hence the preceding position [of the preparatory mind] is called "expedient means." According to this master's intent, all minds of meditative concentration (*定心*) cognize only particular characteristics, because they are direct perception (*現量*). However, that it is said that the mind of meditative concentration cognizes common characteristics is due to its being able to eliminate afflictions (*煩悩*); and thus, what the *Abhidharmasamuccaya* etc. says — that "comprehensive-engagement mental attention (*惣縁作意*) eliminates afflictions" — is said with respect to the expedient means [preliminary stage], and therefore there is no contradiction. These two preceding positions [the first and second masters] are both not the correct teaching.

Regarding "The text: 'The third master says,' up to 'all are called direct perception' ": From this point onward, the third master expounds the correct teaching of the treatise master (*論主*). "That treatise on epistemology (*因明論*) etc." — [this refers to] the *Nyāyamukha* (*正理門論*) and the *Nyāyapraveśa* (*入正理論*), neither of which contains an extensive passage discussing the two characteristics. The *Nyāyamukha* merely says: "For the purpose of expounding to oneself, there are only direct perception and inference. Since things like analogy (*声喩*) and so on are subsumed within these, there are only two valid cognitions (*二量*). Through these, particular and common characteristics can be cognized; hence apart from these

The current reference to "that treatise on epistemology" (*彼因明論*) probably refers to the *Pramāṇasamuccaya* (*集量論*), [the work] by Dignāga (*陳那*). The reason we can know this is that Master Mài (*邁法師*) says: "The particular characteristics and common characteristics posited in that epistemological treatise, the *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, differ somewhat from this sūtra [i.e., the *Buddhabhūmi* sūtra]. According to that treatise's explanation, all the real meanings (*実義*) belonging to impermanence (*無常*), suffering (*苦*), etc., on [the level of] all dharmas, are each attached to [their respective] substance (*体*), and are all called particular characteristics (*自相*). This is because each particular and common characteristic of the various dharmas is attached to its own substance and is not shared with others (*不共他*). For example, just as the characteristic of resistance (*礙の相*) of the form-base (*色処*) is the particular characteristic of form, while suffering, impermanence, etc., are the common characteristics of form — even though, with respect to form, these are divided into particular and common characteristics, each is still attached to the substance of form and is not shared with sounds [Note: "声" (*sound*) is written as "趣" (*direction/tendency*) in one edition] etc. Therefore, the particular and common characteristics of form are, in the *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, all called particular characteristics (*自相*) and are entirely subsumed within direct perception (*現量*). If, in the discriminating, scattered mind (*分別散心*), one posits a single class (*種類*) as the signifier (*能詮*) and designates suffering, impermanence, etc. as the signified (*所詮*), then the meaning of suffering, impermanence, etc. follows the signifier and appears in the discriminating scattered mind. Therefore, this suffering, impermanence, etc. also pervades all eleven bases (*十一処*), just as a single thread strung through many flowers causes all the flowers, following the thread, to be together called common characteristics (*共相*). This is a conceptual designation (*仮立*) by discriminating scattered mind, and is the object of inference (*比量の境*). All minds of meditative concentration, whether of ordinary beings or sages, all leave this discrimination behind and are entirely called direct perception (*現量*). The details are as explained in that master's commentary on the *Buddhabhūmi* treatise (*仏地論疏*)."

Regarding "The text: 'Although [one] cognizes all dharmas,' up to 'neither identical nor different, hence' ": "Although one cognizes suffering, impermanence, etc. of all dharmas etc." means: even though one cognizes suffering, impermanence, etc. belonging to the eleven bases of all dharmas, at each and every base there are [their own] suffering, impermanence, etc., each attached to the dharma-substance (*法体*); hence this impermanence is called particular characteristic (*自相*). Regarding "The text: 'The meaning of this passage says,' up to 'because [one] can cognize it' ": "[Viewed as] what is altogether revealed by our common characteristics" — this presents (*標*) the common-characteristics observation (*共相観*) of the [*Buddhabhūmi*] sūtra on the grounds (*地経*). "What is individually verified, the substance is particular characteristics" — this presents the particular-characteristics observation (*自相観*) of epistemology (*因明*). "Thus it can universally be said that both observations are able to eliminate [afflictions]" — this comprehensively reveals the intent of both the *Buddhabhūmi* treatise and epistemology. "What can express [suchness] is the common characteristic, etc." — this further clarifies the above intent. If one follows this teaching, particular and common characteristics already have two interpretive approaches (*二門*). If one follows the sūtra's account of particular and common characteristics, one should say: "The observation cognizing common characteristics completely eliminates all afflictions." Since the observation of impermanence (*無常観*) can ably eliminate delusion, "comprehensive-engagement mental attention (*惣縁作意*) eliminates delusion" is precisely this meaning. If one follows the epistemological account of particular and common characteristics, one should say: "Only the particular-characteristics observation ably eliminates afflictions, because all minds of meditative concentration cognize particular characteristics."

Regarding "The text: 'Below the [passage from the] *Pratītyasamutpāda* Sūtra (*縁起経*),' up to 'hence there is no contradiction' ": This is a reconciliation of the apparent contradiction. "[In a single moment of] thought (*一刹那中*) and below" is the chapter author's text. If, following epistemology, only the particular-characteristics observation ably eliminates afflictions, why does the *Pratītyasamutpāda Sūtra* say: "The holy wisdom of absolute truth (*真諦聖智*) is comprehensive characteristics and comprehensive cognition (*惣相惣縁*); hence it is called instantaneous direct realization (*頓現観*)"? Therefore the text now reconciles this, saying: "If the one-moment wisdom of dharma-forbearance (*法忍*) [is regarded as] deriving from the prior preparatory [path], it is called comprehensive-characteristic cognition (*惣相縁*). This is because at the prior preparatory position one

Within the *Buddhabhūmi* treatise, however, there is no passage citing the *Pratītyasamutpāda Sūtra* to reconcile the contradictory text. What is meant by "the *Buddhabhūmi* reconciles it by itself" is what the *Buddhabhūmi* treatise, fascicle six, says: "Therefore, in that treatise [i.e., the *Pramāṇasamuccaya*], the real meanings (*実義*) present in all dharmas are all called particular characteristics. This sūtra [i.e., the *Buddhabhūmi* sūtra] is not the case. Hence there is no contradiction." Master Mài says: "Therefore that epistemological treatise, explaining all the real meanings present in all dharmas, each attached to its own substance, calls them all particular characteristics. This sūtra is not so: just as suffering, impermanence, etc. in the eleven bases, while not being [all] one, still have suffering, impermanence, etc. shared between them (*彼此相*) and are therefore called common characteristics." In the *Buddhabhūmi* treatise, both the epistemological intent and the sūtra-of-the-grounds intent are raised, and [the *Buddhabhūmi*] reconciles them itself. Hence there is no contradiction.

Question: "What is the difference between the two characteristics (*二相*) as explained in the *Buddhabhūmi* treatise, the *Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi* (*唯識論*), and the epistemological treatise (*因明論*)?" Answer [Head note: Huizhao's commentary refers to the *Bugue Yilin* (*補欠義林*); the relevant passage appears below at folio thirty-four, left]: Master Huizhao (*慧沼師*) says: "In the *Buddhabhūmi* treatise's intent, the dharmas of the five aggregates (*五蘊*) etc. are called particular characteristics (*自相*), and suffering, impermanence, etc. are called common characteristics (*共相*). In the *Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi*, what carries the verbal expression (*名言の詮*) is called common characteristics, and what cannot be verbally expressed is called particular characteristics. In the epistemological treatise, what is general (*通*) is called common characteristics, and what is specific (*局*) is called particular characteristics. There are four pairs (*四対*) [of distinctions] here in explaining the two characteristics, as explained in the [chapter on] elimination (*断*) [of the *Yilin zhang*]."
