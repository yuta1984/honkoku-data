**Question:** In the present sūtra (*Suvarṇaprabhāsottama-sūtra*, *Konkōmyō Saishōō-kyō*), regarding the clarification of the "Chapter on Buddha-Wisdom" (*Butsuchi-hon*, 仏智品): is there a meaning of "taking *prajñā* (慧, wisdom) as the master (*shu*, 主)"? How (should this be understood)?

**(The question goes) both ways.** If there is such (a meaning), "wisdom" (*chi*, 智) (in this context) refers to the *prajñā* (*e*, 慧) mental factor (*shinjo*, 心所) within the object-determining (*bekkyo*, 別境) (mental factors). Even at the stage of the Buddha-fruit's merits and virtues (*butsuka kudoku-i*, 仏果功德位), how can there be a meaning of "taking (it) as master"? Based on this, the Great Master Zīzhōu (慧沼, Huizhao) in another passage [Interlinear note: *Tōdai jū*, 燈題十] refutes the meaning of another master's view — (namely) "taking *prajñā* as master." If one follows this (reasoning), then the present commentary's (statement) "because at the stage of the Buddha-fruit, wisdom (*chi*, 智) is taken as master (etc.)" — how (should this be understood)?

**Personal view:** Even at the stage of the Buddha-fruit, the meaning of the "master-companion relationship between the mind-king (*ōshin*, 王心) and the mental factors (*shinjoshin*, 心所心)" (*ōsho shuhan*, 王所主伴) cannot differ from the causal stage (*in-i*, 因位). For this reason, in the *Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi-śāstra* (*Yuishiki-ron*, 唯識論) [Interlinear note: the tenth fascicle (*daijikkan*, 第十卷)] it is stated: "Because consciousness (*shiki*, 識) is the master, (the sūtra) speaks of transforming consciousness to obtain (wisdom)." It is established that the meaning of the master-companion, king-and-retainer relationship (*shuhan ōsho no gi*, 主伴王所之義) still exists even in the Buddha-fruit stage. Next, furthermore: "In the conditioned (*uro*, 有漏) stage, wisdom is weaker and consciousness is stronger; in the unconditioned (*muro*, 無漏) stage, wisdom is stronger and consciousness is weaker (etc.)." This means that, although the meaning of the master-companion relationship does not change, there is (a difference in) the strength or weakness of (their respective) functions. Taking this as a standard for reflection, the (Yuishiki-)ron's statement "taking wisdom (*chi* [Interlinear note: or 'the various teachings']) as master" expresses the intent of revealing the strong and superior function; it does not mean that "(wisdom) is (the absolute) master" in terms of the master-companion relationship. In the *Buddhabhūmi-śāstra* (*Bujichi-ron*, 仏地論) there is also a passage stating "taking wisdom as master."

**Question:** [Interlinear note: Shiki and Eishi compose this.] The sūtra text praises the merit gained by the Dharma-teacher (saying): "Surpassing all the holy assemblies, going beyond the three realms (*sangai*, 三界), one becomes the most supremely venerable (*saishōson*, 最勝尊) (etc.)." How does the commentary master interpret this?

**It is advanced:** (It refers to) the benefit obtained at the causal stage (*in-i tokuyaku*, 因位得益).

Regarding this: since (the text) already states "surpassing all the holy assemblies and going beyond the three realms," it should clearly be interpreted as the benefit of the fruit's complete fulfillment (*kamman no yaku*, 果満得益). Stating "becomes the most supremely venerable" surely cannot be the benefit obtained at the causal stage, can it? How (should this be understood)?

### [Lower Section]

**Personal view:** The commentary master's interpretation most especially accords with the sūtra text. The (phrase) "being seated at the Bodhi-tree king (*bodaiju-ō*, 菩提樹王) (etc.)" clarifies the benefit obtained upon the fulfillment of the fruit; and it is known that the present passage speaks of the benefit obtained at the causal stage. Since (the text) already states "fully equipped with (the necessities of) provisions (*shiryō*, 資糧)," would this meaning exist (in the context of) the Buddha-fruit? (No, it would not.) For this reason: because one surpasses the two vehicles (*nijō*, 二乗), one is said to "surpass all the holy assemblies"; because one is constantly born in the land of enjoyment-for-others (*tajuyū-do*, 他受用土), one is said to "go beyond the three realms"; and in comparison to the two vehicles and those of ordinary nature (*ijō*, 異生, i.e., ordinary beings), one is said to "become the most supremely venerable."

**Question:** The sūtra text (states): "Arousing the mind of great compassion (*daiji-shin*, 大慈心), one vows to grant peace and happiness (*anraku*, 安樂) (etc.)." How does the commentary master interpret this?

**It is advanced:** (The commentary) cites the deeds of compassion (*hi-gō*, 悲業) (etc.).

Regarding this: since (the text) already speaks of "great loving-kindness (*daiji*, 大慈)" [Interlinear note: perhaps "compassion" (*hi*, 慈歟)], how does (the commentary) speak of "compassion (*hi*, 悲)"? Since (the text) states "vowing to grant peace and happiness (*anraku wo atae*)", this most appropriately should not be "the deeds of compassion that relieve suffering (*baku-ku no hi-gō*, 拔苦悲業)." (The interpretation) is unclear. How (should this be understood)?

**Personal view:** Loving-kindness and compassion (*jīhi*, 慈悲) are a virtue of a single characteristic (*ichisō*, 一相); when they simultaneously give rise to the function of "relieving suffering and granting happiness" (*bakkū-yoraku*, 拔苦與樂), each (of the two) can carry the function of the other. Therefore, when the characteristic action of loving-kindness (*ji-gyōsō*, 慈行相) is cited, there is the interpretation (identifying it as) the deed of compassion (*hi-gō*, 悲業). That which is manifestly presented as citing the deed of loving-kindness (*ji-gō*, 慈業) and interpreted as "the deed of loving-kindness" is not the original intent of the (commentary's) interpretation (*honnai*, 本意). That it (the text) speaks of the deed of loving-kindness is certainly (the case). Above this, (the commentary) manifestly shows the place where it also carries the deed of compassion. The commentary master is the one who received the dictation (*hisshu*, 筆受) for the present sūtra's (translation); one should look up to and trust in this. Who would doubt it?

**Question:** In the present fascicle's sūtra text, among the six meanings of the Wheel of Dharma (*hōrin*, 法輪), (the text states:) "He is able to light the supremely and extremely brilliant Dharma-torch (*hōkyo*, 法炬) without equal (etc.)." If so, regarding the correspondence (*ai*, 合) between the Dharma (in question) and its simile (*hi*, 喩), how does the commentary master interpret this?

**It is advanced:** The commentary states: "The Dharma-torch (*hōkyo*) is the true principle (*shinri*, 真理) (etc.)."

Regarding this: when the light of wisdom (*chie*, 智惠) comes, the darkness of ignorance (*mumyō*, 無明) departs; since (the text) already states "he is able to light the supremely and extremely brilliant Dharma-torch without equal," this should certainly be taken as describing the function of wisdom. How can the true principle of the dharma-nature (*hosshō*, 法性) possibly have the capacity of being "supremely and extremely brilliant"? Furthermore, regarding the *Miaofa* (*Myōhō*, 妙経) Sūtra's phrase "like a torch removing darkness," the Great Master [Interlinear note: *Xuanzàn*, 玄賛] has judged it as "breaking the darkness of delusion (*chi-an*, 癡闇)."

(Who would say that) breaking through the darkness of ignorance and delusion (*mumyō-chi-an*, 無明癡闇) is not the light of wisdom (*chie-myō*, 智惠明)? If so, (the commentary's interpretation) is unclear. How (should this be understood)? [Interlinear note: Shiki and Jō'on compose this.]

**Personal view:** "The one who is able to erect the supremely excellent Dharma-banner (*hōdō*, 法幢) without equal" refers to bodhi (*bodai*, 菩提), which is wisdom. Bodhi means "lofty" (*kō*, 高); the unsurpassed bodhi (*mujō bodai*, 無上菩提) is precisely this. It is also said to "erect the Dharma-banner on high." Now, since (the text) already says "unsurpassed" (*mujō*, 無上) and states "Dharma-banner" (*hōdō*, 法幢), it appears that (the text is) clarifying bodhi. Therefore the next (phrase), "the one who is able to light the supremely and extremely brilliant Dharma-torch without equal," clarifies the true principle of nirvāṇa (*nehan no shinri*, 涅槃真理). In terms of the structure of the text, should this not be the natural progression (*bunshō tsugi ni shikaru*, 文相次可然)? However, calling the true principle the "supremely and extremely brilliant Dharma-torch" is comparable to naming the true principle of the dharma-body (*hosshin no shinri*, 法身真理) "Light Illuminating Everywhere" (*kōmyō-henshō*, 光明遍照) — because the true principle gives rise to the torch of correct wisdom (*shōchi no kyo*, 正智之炬) and dispels the darkness of ignorance. In reality (*jitsuni*), although the meaning of "supremely and extremely brilliant Dharma-torch" resides in wisdom (*chie*, 智惠), since the principle (becomes the object) realized by (correct wisdom) (*shōshō*, 所證) and (thus) gives rise to correct wisdom, thereby removing the darkness of ignorance — nirvāṇa too is given this name. If so, how would the *Miaofa* Sūtra's statement "like a torch removing darkness" constitute a contradiction? Because one cannot obstruct the fact that in original wisdom (*honji*, 本智) itself there is the meaning of the "supremely and extremely brilliant Dharma-torch."

**Question:** Do the Four Heavenly Kings (*shiten*, 四天) all together expound the divine dhāraṇī (*shinju*, 神呪) to protect sentient beings?

**It is advanced:** Vaiśravaṇa (*Tamonten*, 多聞天) alone expounds it (etc.).

Regarding this: the Four Heavenly Kings together (*tomo ni*, 俱) make vows (*seigan*, 誓願) to protect sentient beings and expound the divine dhāraṇī — how then is it limited to Vaiśravaṇa alone? Furthermore, at the time of turning the Vajra-longevity Wheel of Dharma (*kongō jumyō hōrin*, 金剛壽命法輪) on the banks of the Gaṅgā River [Note: the Ganges River], the Four Heavenly Kings all together confer the total-retention dhāraṇī (*sōji*, 總持). In both the chapter on the Wish-fulfilling Jewel (*Nyoi-hōju-hon*, 如意宝珠品) and the chapter on the Merchant's Son Flowing Water (*Chōjashi Ryūsui-hon*, 長者子流水品), the expounding of the divine dhāraṇī extends to all Four Heavenly Kings participating. The ten rākṣasī (*jūrasetsujo*, 十羅刹女) all together expound the divine dhāraṇī. If so, the teaching of the present chapter (limiting the dhāraṇī to Vaiśravaṇa) is unclear. How (should this be understood)?

### [Lower Section]

**Personal view:** The skillful means (*hōben*, 方便) of benefiting sentient beings follow the circumstances (*keizei*, 形勢) of the time and accommodate the inclinations (*iraku*, 意楽) of persons; one cannot object (to something different) by (insisting it should be) the same, nor can one censure (something different) by (insisting it should be) the same. For this reason, the present commentary, in interpreting Vaiśravaṇa alone expounding the dhāraṇī, (states): "In order to make (people) focus their minds single-pointedly, their prayerful requests will be more easily fulfilled (etc.)." The meaning is: if there are many (persons) on both sides (*hichi*, 彼此) expounding the dhāraṇī, the minds of those being taught will become scattered, and the single-pointed focus (*sennjin*, 專心) of prayerful request will become less (effective). The holy virtue (*shōtoku*, 聖德) is difficult to conceive. Even though there are eighty thousand bodhisattvas in the assembly, it is only the two bodhisattvas Bhaiṣajyarāja (*Yakuō*, 薬王) and Pradānāśūra (*Yūse*, 勇施) who expound the dhāraṇī, not the others. When the faculties (*kikon*, 機根) of those to be taught are such that many teachers on all sides expounding only dhāraṇīs together (is what is needed), and the time for faith to deepen (has come), then many teachers expound it together. Therefore, each sūtra expounds (things) differently; it is not a uniform standard (*ichijun*, 一准).

**Question:** The sūtra text (states): "The marks of excellence (*sōgō*, 相好) are like space and cannot be measured; (the light he emits) surpasses a thousand moons and radiates brilliance (etc.)." Among the three bodies (*sanshin*, 三身), which body is being praised?

**It is advanced:** The commentary states: "(It is) praising the dharma-body (*hosshin*, 法身) (etc.)."

Regarding this: the dharma-body is without characteristics (*musō*, 無相) and is entirely without marks of excellence (*sōgō*, 相好). How can one speak of "marks of excellence like space"? The true principle (*shinri*, 真理) is without form and is moreover separate from the form-body (*shikishin*, 色身). How can one judge (it to be) "radiating brilliance"? Furthermore, the "principle (*ri*, 理) that is solidly and permanently (*gyōnen jōjū*, 凝然常住)" cannot be said to be "all like a flame or an illusion (*en-gen*, 焔幻)." If so, the present interpretation must certainly have a deep and fundamental intent (*genshi*, 源心), and (this intent) should be revealed and established.

**Personal view:** The commentary master's interpretation of these five verses (*gojū*, 五頌) has two interpretations. The first interpretation praises (the Buddha) in terms of the two bodies of recompense (*hōshin*, 報身) and transformation (*keshin*, 化身). In the second interpretation, when assigning (the verses) to the three bodies: the first verse praises the transformation-body (*keshin*, 化身); the next verse praises the recompense-body (*hōshin*, 報身); the following two verses jointly praise the recompense-body and transformation-body; and the last verse praises the dharma-body (*hosshin*, 法身). Within the praise of the dharma-body, the first phrase praises the substance (*tai*, 體); the next two phrases praise the arising of function (*yū*, 用) through the recompense-body and transformation-body. However, since the dharma-body is the substance of the recompense-body and transformation-body, the name of function (*yūmyō*, 用名) is established upon the substance.
