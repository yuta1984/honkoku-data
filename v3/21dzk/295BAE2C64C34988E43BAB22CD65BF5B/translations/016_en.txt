For this reason, (Tzu-en) the Great Master (Kuiji, 窺基) judges that "receiving (birth in) the three evil destinies is precisely the birth of the actual body (*jisshin*)." This is the intent. The reason the *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra* (*Yuga-ron*) does not say "vowing to be born in evil realms" is because it discusses (the matter) in terms of general retribution.

A certain person says: The rebirth conditioned by karmic power (*gōriki-kessho*) is exhausted at the time of entering the path of insight. The rebirth conditioned by the power of wisdom (*chiriki-kessho*) still remains even after entering the path of insight. For this reason, "not being born in evil realms" is discussed in terms of rebirth conditioned by karmic power. The vow to take rebirth in evil realms pertains to the case of rebirth conditioned by the power of wisdom.

**Question:** The sūtra text explains that when the Buddha attained enlightenment, Brahmā (*Bonō*) and Indra (*Taishaku*) requested (the Buddha) to turn the wheel of the Dharma (*tenpōrin*). In that case, was this a request to turn the wheel of the four noble truths (*shitai-no-hōrin*) that all three vehicles practice in common, or was it something else? If (the request was) not for the wheel of the four noble truths, then based on the request by Brahmā and Indra to turn the wheel of the Dharma, must it not necessarily be the wheel of the four noble truths? Here, the Great Master of Zizhou (*Shishū-no-daishi*, Zhizhou 知周) interprets the sūtra text (saying): "If at the first (second?) accomplishment of enlightenment, (the Buddha) has not yet turned the wheel of the Dharma, (they) request (him to) turn the wheel of the Dharma" (etc.). Since it already says "(they) requested (him to) turn the wheel of the Dharma at the time of the first accomplishment of enlightenment," how could this not be the wheel of the four noble truths? Following the text of the *Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa* (*Daichido-ron*, *Dà zhìdù lùn*), it is judged that based on the request by Brahmā and Indra, (the Buddha) turned the wheel of the Dharma at Vārāṇasī (the Deer Park). The contextual meaning is undisputed, and it appears to point to the first turning of the wheel of the Dharma. If it is so, then there is (the statement) by the Great Master (Kuiji) in the "Chapter on the Twelve Categories of Scripture" (*Jūni-bungyō-shō*) that the first turning of the wheel of the Dharma was preached without waiting for a request — how should these be reconciled? [Marginal note: (Composed by) Shiki no Eikō.]

**A difficulty raised in the *Butsumo-shō* (*佛母抄*) states:** Looking at the account in the *Wonderful Dharma Lotus Sūtra* (Fascicle One), "At that time (the Buddha) invited Brahmā (*Bonō*), and also the Heavenly Lord Indra (*Taishaku*), the Four Heavenly Kings who protect the world, and the Great Self-Existent Heavenly Being (*Daijizaiten*, Maheśvara), and thereupon with reverence and joined palms, they bowed and requested me to turn the wheel of the Dharma" (etc.). This passage appears to make clear that the wheel of the four noble truths was turned based on a request. The "Chapter on the Twelve Categories of Scripture" (*Jūni-bungyō-shō*) states: "In the sūtras of the Lesser Vehicle also, the Buddha's compassion is manifested. All the characteristics of the Dharma are preached spontaneously without waiting for a request, as in the first turning of the wheel of the Dharma. Hence there is the (category of) spontaneous preaching (*jissetsu*, *udāna*)" (etc.). The Master Kyōgan (*Jinguan*, 經願) responds (by saying): Although this sūtra (i.e., the first turning of the Dharma-wheel) was preached based on a request from Brahmā and others, since they were not the intended audience (*shohiki-kongen*) (for that teaching), it is still called a "sūtra of spontaneous preaching without request" (*mumon-jissetsu-kyō*, *udāna-sūtra*). That is because that sūtra was preached for the five monks (headed by) Kauṇḍinya (*Kurin*) and others.

Furthermore (it is said): Although the celestial beings made a request, they did not specify which wheel of the Dharma (they were requesting to be turned). Therefore, it may be understood that that request extends to both the Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna, encompassing all the teachings of the three periods. Accordingly, in the *Mahāprajñāpāramitopadeśa*, the request of Brahmā extends even to the (teaching of) *Prajñāpāramitā* (*hannya*), and Jizang (*Kichizō*, 吉藏) also extends (the request) to the *Lotus Sūtra* (etc.). If that is so, then the request of the celestial beings does not specifically point to the first turning of the wheel of the Dharma. Since it is not the same as Subhūti (*Zenkō*, 善現) requesting the *Prajñāpāramitā* or Śreṣṭhin (*Tokubon*, 德本) asking about the three natures (*san-shō*), (the first turning of the Dharma-wheel) is perhaps rightly called a "sūtra of spontaneous preaching without request."

**Question:** The sūtra text (states) "not arising and not ceasing, neither existent nor non-existent" (etc.) — does this explain the *dharmakāya* (*hosshin*, the truth body), or is it something else? Both sides (are presented): If it is not the *dharmakāya*, then since the *dharmakāya* has no arising or ceasing, one contemplates "not arising and not ceasing," and since it is separated from characteristics such as coming and going, "neither existent nor non-existent" is explained. Therefore, what is being explained must naturally be the *dharmakāya*. Also, in other teachings (such as the *Prajñāpradīpa-śāstra*, *Hannya-tō-ron*), the statement "no arising and no ceasing, also no coming and going" is made — does this not explain the *dharmakāya*? On the other hand, if one says it explains the *dharmakāya* based on this, the present commentary (*疏*) interprets it as "the transformation body (*keshin*, *nirmāṇakāya*)." How (should this be understood)? [Marginal note: (Composed by) Shiki no Chinyo.]

**Personal view:** Reading the sūtra text, the three bodies (*sanshin*: the three kāyas) are each explained separately. "Not arising and not ceasing, neither existent nor non-existent" [marginal note: (by textual consideration) below "non-existent" (*mu*), there may be a missing character (*sha*, "者"), possibly a scribal omission] explains the transformation body (*keshin*, *nirmāṇakāya*). "Separated from defilement, pure, the mind having attained peaceful abiding" means contemplating the reward body (*hōshin*, *sambhogakāya*). "Transcending all characteristics, the mind fundamentally being thusness (*shinnyо*, *tathatā*)" means contemplating the truth body (*hosshin*, *dharmakāya*). These three passages as a whole contemplate the two truths — the ultimate (*shin*, *paramārtha*) and the conventional (*zoku*, *saṃvṛti*) — of all dharmas, and within each of the three passages the truth-wisdom and the two truths individually distinguish and classify the three bodies, explaining them in order of depth from shallow to profound. Regarding the explanation of "not arising and not ceasing" with respect to the transformation body, the commentary interprets this matter (saying): "Also contemplating all dharmas of the eight phases (of a Buddha's existence), (they are) not arising and not ceasing, and so forth, separated from false attachment and dwelling in equanimity, and so forth" (etc.). Even though the (transformation body's) mind is of conditioned existence (*uи*, *saṃskṛta*) and the conventional truth, because it is separated from false attachment, one contemplates "not arising and not ceasing." To be fixedly attached to characteristics such as arising and ceasing is false attachment; if one separates from this attachment, the conventional truth is then not definitively arising, nor definitively ceasing — it is like an illusion, a provisional existence. The sūtra text reveals precisely this (state of) mind (regarding the transformation body). Even if evidence is brought from other sūtra texts, it cannot serve as a counter-argument. **Question:** The sūtra text, in clarifying the sixth ground (*roku-chi*), says "non-conceptual contemplation (*musō-shiyui*), all and entirely manifesting before (one)" (etc.). In that case, is this "non-conceptual contemplation" (*musō-shiyui*) the cognitive mode (*gyōsō*) of subsequently-attained wisdom (*gotoku-chi*, *pṛṣṭhalabdha-jñāna*), or (is it something else)?

**It is advanced:** The commentary says "non-conceptual contemplation is precisely non-discriminating wisdom (*mufunbetsu-chi*, *nirvikalpa-jñāna*)" (etc.). Regarding this: since it already says "contemplation" (*shiyui*, *vitarka*), how can it be non-discriminating wisdom? Moreover, the *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra* states: "to contemplate thusness (*shinnyо*, *tathatā*) means to take the characteristic of thusness as its object; it does not (mean) to penetrate (*tsūtatsu*) thusness directly" (etc.). If so, this passage must surely be describing the cognitive mode of subsequently-attained wisdom. How (should this be understood)?

**Personal view:** The *Vijñaptimātratā-śāstra* (*Yuishiki-ron*, *Chéng wéishí lùn*), in clarifying the sixth ground, states: "The sixth, the stage of Manifestation (*Genzenjichi*, *Abhimukhī*), dwelling in the wisdom of dependent origination (*engi-chi*), draws forth the most excellent non-discriminating *prajñā* (*mufunbetsu-saikōhannya*), causing it to manifest before (one), therefore" (etc.). The meaning is: the wisdom of contemplating dependent origination serves as the "actively-drawing" (*nōin*) (wisdom), drawing forth

**Question:** In the sūtra of the present fascicle, regarding the explanation of why each of the ten grounds (*jūchi*, *daśabhūmi*) receives its name, how is the naming of the fifth ground as "Extremely Difficult to Overcome" (*Goku-nansho-chi*, *Sudurjayā*) to be interpreted?

**It is advanced:** "(Because) the skillful means (*hōben*, *upāya*) of practice, and the sovereign excellence of superior wisdom (*shōchi-no-jizai*), are extremely difficult to attain. The afflictions of the path of insight (*kenji-bonnō*, *darśana-kleśa*) and the path of cultivation (*shuji-bonnō*, *bhāvanā-kleśa*), though difficult to subdue, are subdued. Therefore the fifth ground is named Difficult to Overcome (*Nansho*)" (etc.). The meaning is: the wisdom of the two truths — ultimate and conventional — is extremely difficult to unify, yet (the bodhisattva) succeeds in unifying them; and the afflictions of insight and cultivation, though difficult to subdue, are subdued. Therefore it is named "Extremely Difficult to Overcome Ground." Regarding this, the two reasons are both unclear. First, regarding the initial reason: in the *Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra* (*Hannya-kyō*), the bodhisattva of the first ground (*sho-chi*) — "the Bodhisattva Zenngaku (*Śīlaskandhin*?), and the Four Heavenly Kings (*Shitennō*), illuminating the two truths together and treading the equal path" (etc.) — is mentioned, and contemplating the two truths (ultimate and conventional) together appears to occur at the first ground. Why then would this be the special capacity (*shōnō*) specific to this (fifth) ground? Second, regarding the subsequent reason, it is also unclear: the afflictions of the path of insight (*kenji-waku*, *darśana-kleśa*) are already cut off at the first ground, and the afflictions of the path of cultivation (*shuji-waku*, *bhāvanā-kleśa*) are cut off and suppressed at each stage before and after. How could this ground have the special ability (to do so)? How (should this be understood)?

At the fifth ground, (the bodhisattva) for the first time certifiably realizes the "thusness distinguished by category without distinction" (*rui-mubetsu-shinnyo*, 類無別真如) — therefore, the two wisdoms of ultimate and conventional are extremely difficult to unify, yet (the bodhisattva) achieves a unified contemplation — hence the name "Extremely Difficult to Overcome Ground" (*Goku-nansho-chi*) is established.
