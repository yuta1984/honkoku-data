The "faith and understanding" (*shinge*) after the Lotus Sūtra refers to the fact that the mind does not regress, but grows ever deeper. It is for this reason that it is expressed thus. (Summary of intent.)

**Question:** Regarding the inclusion of the present sūtra within the Third Period (*daisanji*, in the three-period doctrinal classification) — how does the commentary master explain this?

**Response (Answer):** The commentary states: "because there is the benefit of the Purification of the Dharma Eye (*hōgenjō*)" (etc.).

Regarding this: the benefit of the Purification of the Dharma Eye is the benefit of the first fruit (*shoka*) of the Hīnayāna. How can the mere presence of such a benefit serve to establish the meaning of the Third Period? Moreover, in the First and Second Periods as well, this benefit is made explicit. How is one to understand this?

**Personal note:** In general, the Third Period is a teaching intended universally for all vehicles (*shoujō*). The various vehicles each obtain benefit; what is taught is the Middle Way teaching of neither emptiness nor existence (*hi-kū hi-u*). That there is benefit for the Mahāyāna is beyond doubt. Because what is taught is neither emptiness nor existence, the benefit of the Hīnayāna also emerges. Since it universally enables all vehicles to receive benefit, the scope of benefit obtained is broad. Therefore, in order to manifest (the teaching's belonging to) the Third Period, the benefit of the Purification of the Dharma Eye is cited to establish this meaning. It is not merely because this benefit exists that it is called the Third Period. As for the objection based on the First and Second Periods as precedents: the Zichou Master (Huizhao), in another passage of the *Deng* (the sub-commentary to the *Shu*), judges: "In the Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra, although there are three vehicles and each obtains benefit, it has not yet been clearly and explicitly stated that dharmas are neither empty nor existent; therefore it belongs to the Second (Period)." The implication is: although the three vehicles obtain benefit in that sūtra, the doctrinal principles expounded there are not those of the Third Period, since (the Middle Way of neither emptiness nor existence) is not yet explicitly stated. Therefore it is still not subsumed under the Third Period.

**Question:** Was the present sūtra expounded at one time and one assembly (*ichiji ichie*), or how should this be understood?

If it was expounded at one time and one assembly: within the chamber of the great being Myōdō (Wonderful Banner Bodhisattva, *Supratiṣṭhita*), four Buddhas expound the principle of the great length of Śākyamuni's lifespan; and in the Pañcāla forest (*Hanchararin*), the teaching master (Śākyamuni) recounts the story of Prince Sattva. The place of preaching is already not limited to Vulture Peak (*Jūhō-zan*). How can it be called an exposition at one time and one assembly?

**Further inquiry:** Regarding the matter of the great length of lifespan being expounded in Myōdō's chamber — it was the four Buddhas who expounded it. How can one say that "Myōdō repeated it"? Furthermore, regarding "showing the appearance of the Pañcāla forest at the summit of Vulture Peak" — is there any textual evidence for this in the sūtra? Moreover, that passage exists in the new translation but not in the old translation. There is a discrepancy — how is this to be understood?

**Another person says:** Based on the fully elaborated correct interpretation (*heibei shōgi*), one should say it was expounded at two places and two assemblies: Vulture Peak and the Pañcāla forest. However, the commentary's statement "nevertheless, the previous interpretation (of one time and one assembly) is superior" was said in dependence upon the old translation sūtra. Since there is no Pañcāla forest assembly (in the old translation), if one relies upon the new translation, one should say two places and two assemblies.

The old translation has titles such as "Chapter on Praising the Buddha" (*Sanbutsu-hon*) and "Chapter on the Praise of the Ten Directions Bodhisattvas" (*Jippō Bosatsu Sandanbon*), and the four chapters of praise are not differentiated. Thereupon the Buddha withdrew his supernatural powers, and both teacher (*nōke*) and those taught (*shoke*) were together at Vulture Peak. Therefore it is clear that (the Pañcāla forest) was not a place of residence (and assembly).

**Furthermore:** The *Zan* (a commentary) explains the text "residing in Gṛdhrakūṭa (Vulture Peak) in Rājagṛha" as follows: "'Reside' (*jū*) carries the meaning of dwelling and remaining, and also of traveling to teach" (etc.). The implication is that the Buddha dwells at and remains on Vulture Peak, constantly residing here and expounding the Wonderful Dharma. Meanwhile, Rājagṛha (*Ōsha-jō*) is a place of traveling teaching (*yūke*), not a place of constant dwelling and remaining. Therefore that (Rājagṛha) is not taken (as the actual location). In the intent of our own school (the Dharmalakṣaṇa school, *Hossō-shū*), the Sacred Mountain (*Ryōzan*), the Pure Land (*Jōdo*), and Emptiness/Sky (*kokū*) — these are called the three places (*sansho*). The addition of that (Rājagṛha/Vulture Peak area) does not constitute a fourth place (*yosho*). Reasoning by analogy: although the Buddha manifested supernatural powers and went to the Pañcāla forest, since it was not a place of dwelling, it is not taken (as such), and is not called a place of the time and assembly.

**Question:** When Śākyamuni expounded the present sūtra, should he have manifested auspicious signs such as raining flowers and emitting light?

**Two positions:** If (he did) not manifest (them): emitting light to summon those with karmic affinity, manifesting earthquakes to awaken those whose faculties are mature — is this not (analogous to) the case where, when the Lotus Sūtra was preached, there were such auspicious signs? If, following this reasoning, (auspicious signs) were manifested: consulting the sūtra text and searching the present sūtra, people have not cited any such auspicious signs from it. If the Sanskrit original contains them, why are they not cited?

**Question:** The sūtra text lists among the "assembly of co-hearers" (*dōmon-shū*): "forty-nine thousand Garuḍa kings (*Kecharutu-ō*) · Fragrant Elephant Power King (*Kōzō Seiriki-ō*)" (etc.). In that case, is the Fragrant Elephant Power King the same as a Garuḍa king?

**Response:** The commentary treats them as separate (etc.).

Regarding this: "The forty-nine thousand Garuḍa kings" — this is a general listing of their name, with "Fragrant Elephant Power King" being separately cited as the foremost (*jōshu*, leader) among them, exemplifying one of the group. Reasoning by analogy: (the text) says "twenty-eight thousand dragon kings · Lotus Dragon King" (*Renge Ryū-ō*), and states "forty-two thousand heavenly beings · Joyful-Vision Heavenly Being" (*Kiken Tennin*) — in each case these are all within the general listing, and the Joyful-Vision Heavenly Being and Lotus Dragon King (are their respective leaders). How then would they be a separate category outside the general listing? Surely the commentary master's explanation has some profound import. How is this to be understood?

**Personal note:** The commentary master has already offered two interpretations. One interpretation is as (stated in) the objection.
