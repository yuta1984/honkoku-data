**Question:** In the commentary on the present sūtra, a passage from the Abhidharma Piṭaka (*Ronzō*, *Abhidharma-piṭaka*) is cited: "The Nirmāṇakāya (*ōjin*, Transformation-body) does not depart from the Dharmakāya (*hosshin*, Dharma-body). The Dharmakāya and the Nirmāṇakāya are neither identical nor separate (*fujoku-furi*)" (etc.). If that is so, what fault (*ka*, *demerit*) is there in saying that the Dharmakāya is absolutely (一向) inseparable from the Nirmāṇakāya?

**The *Butsumoshō* states:** "A certain person says: The intent of this treatise is to clarify the principle of origin and manifestation (*honmatsu no gi*). (The treatise) provisionally states that the Dharmakāya, with respect to the Nirmāṇakāya, has (the relation of) both separation and non-separation, while the Nirmāṇakāya, with respect to the Dharmakāya, is absolutely non-separate. If one follows the true meaning (*jitsugī*), the three bodies (*sanshin*), each in relation to the others, each possess both aspects: separation and non-separation. Now (the reason) this fault arises (when the Dharmakāya is said to be absolutely inseparable from the Nirmāṇakāya) is as follows: The Dharmakāya pervades (*zairu*) all sentient beings, and its essence is also one. If one considers this principle, all sentient beings are one in essence, not distinct. If a single sentient being manifests the Dharmakāya and attains Buddhahood, since through the Dharmakāya pervading all sentient beings (that Dharmakāya) is one essence with the Nirmāṇakāya, all sentient beings should likewise simultaneously attain Buddhahood. Since the Dharmakāya is the origin (*hon*), the manifestation (*matsu*) should follow the origin — this fault arises. Even though the Nirmāṇakāya, with respect to the Dharmakāya, possesses the one aspect of non-separation: when a single sentient being attains the Buddha of the Nirmāṇakāya (*ōjin-butsu*), there is no fault that all sentient beings should attain Buddhahood. Since the Nirmāṇakāya is not of the same essence as all sentient beings, even though the Dharmakāya pervades (all), the origin (*hon*) cannot be said to follow the manifestation (*matsu*). Therefore this fault does not arise. If one follows the true meaning (*jitsugī*), since the Nirmāṇakāya is non-separate from the Dharmakāya, one should (conclude) that this fault does not arise (— this is what is meant by that statement)." [End of citation.]

**Personal view:** Upon reflection on this principle: when the Dharmakāya is considered in relation to the Nirmāṇakāya, the Dharmakāya is the "that which is able to be identified with (*nōsoku*)," and the Nirmāṇakāya is "that which is identified with (*shosoku*)." The *nōsoku* is the origin (*hon*); the *shosoku* is the manifestation (*matsu*). Therefore, following the *nōsoku* (which is the) origin, the *shosoku* (which is the) manifestation should share (a common quality) with all (sentient beings). However, (when) the Nirmāṇakāya is considered in relation to the Dharmakāya, (if they are) absolutely identified (*soku*), then when the Dharmakāya is absolutely identified with the Nirmāṇakāya, (following) the *nō-*

**Question:** In the opening chapter (*shohon*, *shodai*) of the present scroll, (the text) "praises the merits of the Tathāgata using the lotus flower as a simile" (etc.). If that is so, is this lotus flower (*renge*) here the same as the *udumbara* flower (*udonge*), or is it something else? (Arguments for) both sides: If the lotus flower is indeed the *udumbara* flower, (one may note that) "udumbara" (*udon*) is a word from the Western (Indian) language, and in Eastern China it is translated as "auspicious responding flower" (*zuiō-ke*, 瑞應花). "Lotus flower" (*renge*) is a Chinese expression; in India it is called "*Puṇḍarīka*" (*Funda-rika*, 芬陀利迦). The Sanskrit and Chinese (terms) are already different, from which one knows that they are each distinct flowers. Moreover, in a certain sūtra (it is stated): "The *udumbara* flower is a tree flower" (etc.). The interpretations of the Great Master (Kuiji, Ci'en) are also the same as this.

If that is so (i.e., if they are the same flower), the present commentary contains (the phrase) "*udumbara*-lotus flower" (優曇鉢蓮花) (etc.). This interpretation appears to treat *udumbara* as the same as the lotus flower. How (should this be understood)?

**The objection in the *Butsumoshō* states:** "The *udumbara* flower first appears when a Wheel-turning King (*tenrin'ō*, *cakravartin*) appears in the world. Its leaves are of a thousand distinct (varieties); its fragrance perfumes ten-thousand *ri* (leagues). Its appearance is supremely wondrous; its form is lovely. Yet the lotus flower lacks all of these qualities. On what basis can they be called the same flower?"

**The response in the same work (*Butsumoshō*) states:** "They should be called flowers of distinct substance (*bettai no hana*). However, concerning the interpretation of Zīzhōu (Huizhao, 慧沼, 淄州): since Sanskrit (*bongo*) contains multiple meanings, the Sanskrit word *udumbara* contains (the meaning of) lotus flower. Relying on this interpretive approach (*gimmon*), (Zīzhōu) perhaps composed a single synthesizing interpretation."

**Personal view:** Both of these (i.e., the *udumbara* and the lotus flower) are the same flower. In the case of jewels, (a parallel example exists:) a superior (jewel) is called in Sanskrit "*cintāmaṇi*" (*jindamaní*, 辰陀摩尼), which in (Chinese) is called "wish-fulfilling jewel" (*nyoi-hōju*, 如意宝珠). An inferior (jewel) is called in Sanskrit "*maṇi*" (*mani*, 摩尼), which in (Chinese) is called "precious jewel" (*hōju*, 宝珠). Although the Sanskrit and Chinese (names) differ, they are both the same jewel (*gyoku*). Similarly, though they are both lotus flowers, the superior one is called the *udumbara* flower, and the inferior one is called the *Puṇḍarīka* (*Fundari*) flower — might this be the case? Also, as for lotus flowers, there may be (the two varieties of) lotus flowers growing in water and tree flowers.
