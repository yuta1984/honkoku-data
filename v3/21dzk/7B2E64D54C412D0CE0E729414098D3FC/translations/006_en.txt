*Question:* According to Dharmapāla's school, since the perceiving-part and aspect-part are both real and both equally arise from conditions, why is it called "consciousness-only"?

*Explanation:* Because (consciousness and its object) are not mutually separate.

*Question:* If so, since mind does not depart from object, should it not be called "object-only"?

*Answer:* In real principle, mind and object are certainly not mutually separate. Yet one says "consciousness-only." Consciousness is the root (*běn* 本) and object is the branch (*mò* 末). Based on the root, one says "only."

### Section on One Function or Many Functions (一用多用門)

*Question:* Is the function (*yòng* 用) by which the perceiving-part cognizes the aspect-part one or many?

*Answer:* According to the *Shenbang Ji* (神昉集, Collected Works of Shenbang) (Note: the note says "following the *Ki*'s quotation this is written as 'Shenbang'"), there are two explanations. One says: since the aspect-parts are many, the functions of the perceiving-part are also many. Another says: a single perceiving-part comprehensively cognizes many aspect-parts.

A determination: the view that the functions of the perceiving-part are also many is superior. The reason is this: the two grasping (tendencies) — clinging to self (*ātmagrāha* 我執) and clinging to dharmas (*dharmagrāha* 法執) — divide into two following the functions. How could it be that cognizing blue etc. does not give rise to multiple (functions)? The remaining questions and answers are as explained previously.

*Question:* Within the self-witness-part (*zìzhèngfēn* 自証分), does it have only a single function that comprehensively witnesses the second (perceiving-part) and fourth (witness-of-the-witness-part), or does it have two functions each separately witnessing the second and fourth? If it has only a single function that comprehensively witnesses the second and fourth, it would undermine the established (position of) the foundational school, which previously stated that there are multiple functions following the object. If there are two functions each witnessing the second and fourth — (Note: the editorial note says "the eighteen characters from 'ruò yǒu etc.' are probably interpolated/dittographic") — if there are two functions each separately witnessing the second and fourth parts, although there would be four functions, there would be no infinite regress fault.

*Answer:* There are two explanations. One says: two functions. However, since the fourth part has only a single function, there is no infinite regress fault. Another says: one function. (Whether) a single function knows many (things), or multiple functions know many (things) — both are self-consistent positions. Therefore there is no fault.

Master Kūkai (弘法師) determines: the latter view (one function) is superior. The reason is this: if there were separately distinct functions (Note: "yǒu 有" is probably an error for "yòng 用") for knowing the second (perceiving-part) and the fourth (witness-of-the-witness-part), then the function of knowing the second would constitute a third part (self-witness), and the function of knowing the fourth would constitute a fifth part. (But) a single function of the fourth part comprehensively knows (what would be) the third and fifth — and hence there is no sixth part. By this reasoning, the latter view is superior.

*Question:* Understanding (*jiě* 解) arises following the object. If the objects are already two distinct (things), how can the understanding be one?

*Answer:* There are naturally two kinds (of cases) in which the perceiving-part cognizes the aspect-part. First: the inferential cognition (*bǐliàng* 比量) mind cognizes aspect-parts such as suffering etc. — this is a case of one function cognizing many aspect-parts. Second: the direct cognition (*xiànliàng* 現量) mind cognizes aspect-parts such as suffering etc. — this is a case of multiple functions each witnessing multiple aspect-parts.

The reason is this: Inferential cognition operates toward external objects and constructs a single "suffering-aspect" (as a general concept), superimposing that aspect (*xíngyìyì xiāng* 行増益相) upon (multiple objects), and hence one function cognizes the many aspect-parts, because each of the various instances of suffering etc. has similar (shared) characteristics. Direct cognition internally witnesses each of the many aspect-parts, individually and separately, in internal experience — hence only multiple functions each cognize multiple aspect-parts. Whether (cognizing) blue, yellow, etc. through direct or inferential cognition, all (direct cognitions) use multiple functions to separately cognize multiple aspect-parts. Hence the *Abhidharmasamuccaya* (*Duì fǎ lùn* 対法論) states:

*Question:* At each sense-faculty door, various objects appear simultaneously in the present. With respect to these many objects, do multiple consciousnesses arise in sequence, or do they arise simultaneously?

*Answer:* There is only one consciousness, (yet) various cognitive modes (*xíngxiàng* 行相) arise simultaneously.

When the third (part), the self-witness-part, witnesses the second (perceiving-part) and fourth (witness-of-the-witness-part) — even though this is direct cognition, because there are two meanings, one function comprehensively witnesses (both). First: the two functions of the second and fourth are one and the same function; the aspects of their functions are similar (identical). Second: (it is) a self-reflection (*fǎnzhào* 反照) upon one's own substance; since the cognizer and the cognized are close (together), it is effortlessly easy to understand.

(When) the self-witness-part knows the perceiving-part: the perceiving-part has multiple functions. Although the cognizer and the cognized are close, (these) are distinct functions, and hence the aspects of the functions are not similar. Therefore, multiple functions know the multiple functions of the perceiving-part. Moreover, the perceiving-part has both substance (*tǐ* 体) and function (*yòng* 用). However, in principle, the substance and function are extremely close to each other. Therefore, when the self-witness-part reflects back upon itself, the cognizer and cognized are close. Hence (Note: "gù 故" is probably an error for "yún 云"), a single function comprehensively witnesses both the substance and the function. If one explains it this way, it accords with correct principle. Also, what is meant by "same substance" (*zhìtóng* 質同) is called "one and the same." It does not mean that multiple perceiving-parts together cognize one direct (*qīn* 親) aspect-part as the same. (Note: there seems to be missing text after "yě 也".)

The mind-substance (*xīn tǐ* 心体) is the same: it takes its own self-transformed seed as the direct object-condition and takes the transformation of the mental factor as the remote object-condition.

Next, **clarifying the pure stage (*jìng wèi* 淨位)**:

*Question:* How do the four parts of the same substance (*tóngtǐ* 同体) mutually cognize one another?

*Answer:* There are two interpretations.

**First, Kuiji's (基/窺基) explanation:** Among the four parts of the same substance, the perceiving-part can cognize the self-witness-part. It can also cognize (what is) the witness-of-the-witness-part (Note: the six characters in the text are corrected to read "证自证/证自证"). It can also cognize the aspect-part and the perceiving-part. This is only possible at the stage of buddha-fruition (*fóguǒ* 佛果). Those at other (stages) cannot do so. The intent here is to show that the remaining three parts mutually cognize all dharmas, and hence are called "universally cognizing" (*biànyuán* 遍緣).

*Question:* If the remaining three parts all cognize all dharmas, what is the distinction (among them)?

*Answer:* The distinction of the three parts is this: what was previously obtained is obtained directly; what is newly obtained among the rest is obtained by transforming a reflected image. Hence distinctions arise.

**Second, Ce's (側/測, probably the monk Woncheuk's disciple or another commentator) (Note: "cè 側" is probably an error for "cè 測") explanation:** There are four interpretations here. One says: among the four parts, (Note: "sān 三" is probably an error for "èr 二") two parts are equally defiled (*rǎn* 染) (i.e., functioning within the defiled stream), and only the perceiving-part alone can cognize the three parts. Excluding the perceiving-part, the cognizer and cognized would become confused/disordered.
