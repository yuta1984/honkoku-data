**Section Three: Deciding (the Meaning of) "Self-Accepted" (自許決 第三)**

Q: What is the distinction among the three types of inference — self-inference (*svārthānumāna*, 自比量), other-inference (*parārthānumāna*, 他比量), and common inference (*sādhāraṇānumāna*, 共比量)?

A: To begin with, in self-inference, the three members (三支, *trirūpa*: thesis, reason, example) are all accepted (by the proponent) and are all the more supportive of the thesis. Even if the other (party) finds (something) unestablished, there is no fault. The reason is as follows: at the time (of self-inference), one is merely establishing one's own school (宗), not refuting the other — and since it is an accepted meaning (of the proponent), even if the other does not commonly accept it, there is no error in the inference.

Q: If so, a self-inference ought not to be a "means of valid establishment" (能立, *sādhana*), since it does not refute the other's school nor cause the other to understand.

A: Even if (the proponent) has already known the meaning of his own school beforehand, he establishes it through the measure (量, *pramāṇa*) in order to be free of fault. Hence, though it functions as a means of establishment, it does not refute the other's school. Once the measure is established, what the other comes to accept thereby becomes that which causes the other to understand — hence it is also called "causing the other to understand" (他悟, *paravijñāpana*). Therefore, self-inference is called a "means of establishment" (能立).

As for other-inference, when the three members are commonly accepted (by the other party), the thesis and the rest are admirably established. Even if the self (proponent) does not accommodate it as a valid measure, there is no fault. The reason is as follows: other-inference is solely for refuting the other's school, not for establishing the meaning that one's own school accepts — hence even if the self does not commonly accept it, there is no error in the inference.

As for common inference, only when both proponent and opponent commonly accept the thesis, reason, and example — all three members — does the measure properly become established. If either party lacks one (of these elements), it is not established. The reason is as follows: by means of common inference, one (1) establishes the thesis and (2) refutes the other's meaning. Hence all (three) members within the three must be "established-in-common" (極成, *prasiddha*). If they are not established-in-common, they are called "quasi-thesis, etc." (*pakṣābhāsa*, 似立宗等).

Q: In establishing the thesis, if one says "The sound of the Buddhas in other directions, which is self-accepted, does not depart from ear-consciousness," and adds the expression "self-accepted" (自許), is the subject-term (有法, *dharmin*) established?

A: Even if one says "self-accepted," the other ultimately cannot establish (acceptance of) it — so even though one says "self-accepted," the common inference still cannot be established.

Q: If so, take (the syllogism) "True hence, established-in-common form does not depart from eye-consciousness, etc." — since this is already a common inference, if the reason states "self-accepted," it ought not to be "established-in-common."

A: The reason stating "self-accepted" (自許) does not mean it is called "self-accepted" in order to exclude the other's acceptance. That established-in-common form is encompassed within the first three and not encompassed by the eye-faculty is something commonly accepted by both self and other. Therefore the reason is not "unestablished" (不成, *asiddha*).

Q: If that is already the case, why is it necessary to state the expression "self-accepted"?

A: There are three explanations. First, Masters Ūn (応師, Dōshō) and Wŏnhyo (元暁師) and others all say: "The expression 'self-accepted' is stated in order to exclude the other's indeterminacy (他不定)." The Sarvāstivāda (*Sabbatthivāda*, 薩婆多) school and others construct the fault of indeterminacy (*anaikānta*) against this reason, saying: "Is (established-in-common form), like the truly unwholesome form of the final-birth bodhisattva of our school (小乗), which is encompassed within the first three and not encompassed by the eye-faculty, therefore necessarily departing from eye-consciousness? Or is it, like eye-consciousness, encompassed within the first three and not encompassed by the eye-faculty, therefore not necessarily departing from eye-consciousness?" In order to exclude this indeterminacy, the expression "self-accepted" is stated. Because, as indicated by what they (the Sarvāstivādins) point to, the truly unwholesome form of the final-birth bodhisattva is not (a form that) the proponent self-accepts as being encompassed within the first three and not encompassed by the eye-faculty — (the proponent's) reason does not apply to it (*hetu* is absent there), hence there is no indeterminacy. Moreover, through common inference, one does not merely establish the thesis one desires for oneself, but also refutes the other — hence the other's indeterminacy is called "non-established-in-common" (不□, likely 不極成).
