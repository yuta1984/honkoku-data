By this line of reasoning, one constructs a "contradiction with the intrinsic nature of the subject-term" (有法自相相違) saying: "Established-in-common form ought not to be established-in-common form. However, following the meaning in analogy to the fault of "contradiction with the qualification of the subject-term" (有法差別相違), there can be a fault of "indeterminacy" (不定). **Section Three: Deciding (the Meaning of) "Self-Accepted" (自許決 第三)**

Q: What is the distinction among self-inference (自比量, *svārthānumāna*), other-inference (他比量, *parārthānumāna*), and common-inference (共比量, *sādhāraṇānumāna*)?

A: In the case of a self-inference, the three members (宗・因・喩: thesis, reason, example) are all commonly accepted, which further supports the thesis. Even if the inference is not accepted on the other side, there is no fault. The reason is that the inference at that time serves only to establish one's own doctrine — it is not (aimed at) refuting others. Because it is a meaning one accepts oneself, even if the other party does not commonly accept it, there is no error in the inference.

Q: If so, a self-inference should not be a "valid means of demonstration" (能立, *sādhana*). Because it does not refute the other's doctrine and does not bring about understanding in others.

A: Even though one has already known (the truth of) one's own doctrine, one uses the inference to demonstrate that there is no error. So although (self-inference) is a valid demonstration, it does not refute the other's doctrine. Once the inference is established, what the other comes to accept as established can then bring about understanding in the other — hence (even self-inference) is called "bringing understanding to others" (他悟). Therefore a self-inference is (also) called a "valid means of demonstration."

In the case of an other-inference, when the three parts (宗・因・喩) are commonly accepted, the thesis and so forth are fittingly established. Even if the inference is not accepted on one's own side, there is no fault. The reason is as follows: the other-inference serves only to refute the other's doctrine — it does not serve to establish the meaning accepted by oneself. Hence even if one's own side does not commonly accept it, there is no error in the inference.

### Lower Section

In the case of a common inference, the thesis, reason, and example — all three members — must be commonly accepted by both proponent and opponent; only then is the inference properly established. If either party finds something absent (i.e., does not accept something), the inference does not hold. The reason is as follows: the common inference simultaneously (1) establishes a thesis and (2) refutes the other's meaning. Hence all three members must be "established-in-common" (極成, *prasiddha*). If they are not established-in-common, they are called "pseudo-thesis, etc." (似立宗等, *pakṣābhāsa*, etc.).

Q: As in the case of establishing a thesis, if one says "The sound of Buddhas of other directions, self-accepted, does not depart from ear-consciousness" — by adding the qualification "self-accepted" (自許) to the subject-term (有法), does the subject-term hold?

A: Even if one says "self-accepted," it still is not established on the other side — hence even saying "self-accepted," the common inference still cannot be set up.

Q: If so, in "(Form that is) established-in-common in the true sense (*shingo*) does not depart from eye-consciousness, etc." — since this is already a common inference, the reason saying "self-accepted" ought not to be "established-in-common."

A: The reason saying "self-accepted" does not mean "not accepted by the other side" — it is called "self-accepted." That established-in-common form is encompassed within the first three sense-fields and not encompassed by the eye-faculty is something commonly accepted by both proponent and opponent. Hence the reason is not "non-established" (不成, *asiddha*).

Q: If that is already so, why is it necessary to state the expression "self-accepted"?

A: There are three explanations. First, Masters Ying (圓應) and Wonhyo (元暁) and others all say: in order to exclude the "indeterminacy" (不定) on the other side, one states the expression "self-accepted." The Sarvāstivāda school (薩婆多, *Sarvāstivāda*) and others, with respect to this reason, construct a fault of "indeterminacy" saying: "Is it (perhaps) like the actually-unwholesome form of final-birth bodhisattvas according to our school — which is encompassed within the first three and not encompassed by the eye-faculty — and therefore necessarily departs from eye-consciousness? Or is it like eye-consciousness — which is encompassed within the first three and not encompassed by the eye-faculty — and therefore does not necessarily depart from eye-consciousness?" In order to exclude this indeterminacy, one states the expression "self-accepted." The "unwholesome form of final-birth bodhisattvas" pointed to by them is not something that the proponent (the Mahāyāna side) self-accepts as "encompassed within the first three and not encompassed by the eye-faculty." Because they lack this reason, (their case) is not an "indeterminacy." Since through a common inference one not only establishes the thesis one prefers, but also refutes others — an indeterminacy raised by the other side is called "non-▢" (non-established/eliminated). Third, Master Wenguei (文軌) says: the reason states "self-accepted" in order to exclude the fault of "other-side indeterminacy" and the fault of "contradiction with the qualification of the subject-term" (有法差別相違).
