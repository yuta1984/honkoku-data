(The latter half's three members of the syllogism) are presented in the lower scroll. The detailed explanation of the three members is as stated in the Treatise.

Master Jìnglián's commentary, upper scroll, says: Within the section "explaining both conditioned and unconditioned phenomena in general," (it) is further divided into two (parts). First, the verse is cited; second, the verse is explained discursively in prose. Within the first (part, i.e.,) the verse, the two characters "zhēn xìng" (真性, true/ultimate nature) constitute the basis-proposition (*suǒ yī zhī zōng* 所依之宗), and the eighteen characters from "yǒu wéi" (有為, conditioned) downward constitute the refuting syllogism (*néng qiǎn zhī liàng* 能遣之量). Within the refuting syllogism, the upper eight characters, relying on ultimate nature, refute all fixed attachments (*dìng zhí* 定執) to conditioned phenomena as having (existence) or not having (existence), etc.; the lower ten characters, relying on ultimate nature, refute all fixed attachments to unconditioned phenomena as having or not having (existence), etc.

Within the (section on the) upper eight characters relying on ultimate nature to refute all fixed attachments to conditioned phenomena as having or not having existence: "Conditioned phenomena are empty" is the establishment of the proposition (*lì zōng* 立宗). "Conditioned phenomena" (*yǒu wéi* 有為) is the subject (*yǒu fǎ* 有法), and "empty" is the predicate (*fǎ* 法). The inseparability of the predicate and the subject is called the "proposition" (*zōng* 宗). "Like illusions" is the affirming example (*tóng yù* 同喩). "Because they arise through conditions" is the establishment of the reason (*lì yīn* 立因).

(As for) "ultimate nature" (*zhēn xìng* 真性): here, in explaining the emptiness of conditioned phenomena, (the author) explains the emptiness of conditioned phenomena in terms of ultimate nature — not in terms of conventional truth (*shì dì* 世諦). The reason for this is: if the emptiness of conditioned phenomena were explained in terms of conventional truth, it would be in contradiction with the four kinds of valid cognition — one's own teaching (*zì jiào* 自教), one's own statements (*zì yǔ* 自語), direct perception (*xiàn liàng* 現量), and common consensus (*shì jiān* 世間). For this reason, when explaining the emptiness of conditioned phenomena here, it is explained in terms of ultimate nature.

Within the (section on the) ten characters relying on ultimate nature to refute all fixed attachments to unconditioned phenomena as having or not having existence: the statement "unconditioned phenomena have no reality" — this is the establishment of the proposition. "Unconditioned phenomena" is the subject, "having no reality" is the predicate. "Not arising" is the establishment of the reason. "Like flowers in space" (i.e., like optical illusions) is the affirming example.

The statement "unconditioned phenomena have no reality" — this too is stated in terms of ultimate nature, (and therefore) "unconditioned phenomena have no reality" is said. If the emptiness of unconditioned phenomena were explained in terms of conventional truth, it would likewise be in contradiction with the four kinds of valid cognition such as one's own teaching, etc. For this reason, the emptiness of unconditioned phenomena is explained in terms of the principle of ultimate nature. The phrase "having no reality" is simply another name for "emptiness," etc. (etc.) For details, see that commentary.

## Section One: Decision on *Zhēn Xìng* as the Subject (*Yǒu Fǎ*)

*Question:* Do the two characters "zhēn xìng" (真性, ultimate nature) enter into the subject (*yǒu fǎ* 有法) (of the syllogism), or not?

*Answer:* In explaining this matter, there are roughly two approaches. First, the interpretation of the (view that it does) "not enter"; second, the exposition of the (view that it does) "enter."

Regarding the first, the interpretation of the "not enter" view: there are four explanations.

**First**, according to the view of Master Mài (邁法師): the intention of the Treatise is that "zhēn xìng" does not enter the subject. That master's commentary says: "The two characters 'zhēn xìng' are the basis-proposition. The eighteen characters from 'yǒu wéi' downward are the refuting syllogism," etc. (etc.) Since it already says "the two characters 'zhēn xìng' are the basis-proposition," it is clearly known that (they) do not enter the subject. (They function) as a qualifying restriction (*jiǎn bié* 簡別) serving as the basis, and thereby block the faults (*guò* 過) such as (contradiction with) the world (consensus), etc.

**Second**, according to the view of Master Fǎng (昉法師): the intention of the Treatise is that "zhēn xìng" does not enter the subject. In that master's *Yuishiki Mongi Ki* (*Wéishí Wén Yì Jì* 唯識文義記), scroll five, it says: "The *Karatna-śāstra* says: '(What) cattle-herders (*mù niú rén* 牧牛人) and others commonly understand: the universally accepted (*jí chéng* 極成) eye, etc. (are taken) in total as the proposition.' Also it says: 'With respect to ultimate truth (*shèng yì dì* 勝義諦), the emptiness of their substance is explained.' Based on this, since 'zhēn xìng' is not within the subject, that subject (the eye, etc.) is not a case of 'non-universally-accepted,' etc." (etc.)

**Third**, according to the view of Master Taekhyŏn (*Taehyŏn* 太賢師) of Silla: the intention of the Treatise is that "zhēn xìng" does not enter the subject. In the *Taejŭng Simnо Jang Ki* (*Dàchéng Xīnlù Zhāng Jì* 大乗心路章記), Masters Lijian (*Lì jiàn* 利見), Xiyuan (*Xī yuǎn* 希遠), and others, expounding the view of Master (Tae)hyŏn, say: "The expression 'zhēn xìng' (in that text) is the qualifying predicate (*néng bié fēn* 能別分) and is not subsumed within the subject. One should say 'conditioned phenomena are empty in terms of ultimate nature.' Therefore it says: 'By means of the emptiness (pertaining to) ultimate nature, conditioned phenomena are differentiated.' Hence the *Karatna-śāstra* says: 'Cattle-herders, etc., understand that the eye, etc., (as) conditioned phenomena, are taken as the subject.' Therefore it is clearly known that 'zhēn xìng' is not subsumed within the subject. If the expression 'zhēn xìng' were the subject, then the reason 'because (they) arise through conditions' (*yuán shēng gù* 縁生故) would apply (*zhuǎn* 転) to conditioned phenomena but would not apply to 'zhēn xìng.' In that case, (the reason) should fail to be a universal characteristic of the proposition (*biàn shì zōng fǎ* 遍是宗法). Therefore it is known that the expression 'zhēn xìng' is not subsumed within the subject," etc. (etc.) (The above is from the *Ki* text. One should also consult the original chapter and can expound Master (Tae)hyŏn's meaning.)

**Fourth**, according to the view of Master Zhǎo (沼法師, i.e., Huizhao 恵沼): the intention of the Treatise is that "zhēn xìng" does not enter the subject. Hence the *Yuishiki Ryōgi Tō* (*Wéishí Liǎo Yì Dēng* 唯識了義燈),

(But the case of) 'ultimate nature' and 'conditioned phenomena' — their doctrinal level is different … (characters unclear) … (they are dealt with) separately, and therefore the fault of the subject is not the same as in the 'sound' proposition. ## Section Two: The Faults Revealed in the Three Members (三支顕過失)

*Question:* Among the three members (三支) of this syllogism establishing the emptiness of conditioned phenomena, in which member(s) and how many faults are there?

*Answer:* In explaining this matter, there are three views.

**First**, the view of Master Jī (基法師, Kuiji): In the proposition (*zōng* 宗) there is "the fault of one part of the subject being non-universally-accepted" (*yǒu fǎ yī fēn bù jí chéng guò* 有法一分不極成過), and also "the fault of contradicting the proposition." In the example (*yù* 喩), both the proponent's and the opponent's (elements) have the fault of non-establishment (*jù bù chéng guò* 倶不成過). Hence the commentary, scroll four, says: "What is called 'seeming inference' (*sì bǐ liàng* 似比量): according to our (*wǒ zōng* 我宗, Yogācāra) position, in terms of ultimate nature (*zhēn xìng* 真性), conditioned and unconditioned phenomena are neither empty nor non-empty. (There is) the fault of one part of the subject being non-universally-accepted. Because you (Bhāvaviveka) do not accept that our (*wǒ*) ultimate (*shèng yì* 勝義) exists. If (we follow) your own position of ultimate-nature emptiness (*shèng yì kōng*), (we) do not accept your ultimate-nature emptiness (*kōng shèng yì*), so it is also not universally accepted," etc. (etc.)

The lower (scroll of) *Sūyō* (*Shū Yào* 樞要, Kuiji's *Yinming Rumen Lun Shu Chuyao* 因明入正理論疏樞要) says: "If (we) discuss (the syllogism) in terms of middle-path (*zhōng dào* 中道) ultimate nature, there is also the fault of one-sided contradiction with the proposition. (The example's) non-establishment is as in the commentary. How so? Furthermore by our (*wǒ*) explanation: if (we speak) in terms of conventional reality (*shì sú* 世俗), both unconditioned and conditioned (phenomena) are existent. If (we speak) in terms of ultimate (*shèng yì* 勝義), (they are) neither empty nor (non-empty). You (Bhāvaviveka) now declare (them) empty. This is precisely (a case of) having the fault of contradicting one's own position, (which is) called 'seeming inference.'"
