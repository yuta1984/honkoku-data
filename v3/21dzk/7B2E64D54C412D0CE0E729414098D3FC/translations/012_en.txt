Furthermore, against the "expanded syllogism" (披比量), one constructs an "indeterminacy" (不定, *anaikānta*) saying: "Is established-in-common form — because eye-consciousness is encompassed within the first three sense-fields and not encompassed by the eye-faculty — (therefore) not a form that departs from eye-consciousness? Or is it — because the form of Buddhas in other directions, which is self-accepted (as existing), is encompassed within the first three and not encompassed by the eye-faculty — a form that does not depart from eye-consciousness?" If the hetu did not state "self-accepted," one could not use "form of Buddhas in other directions" as an "indeterminacy," because the common hetu could not be transferred to apply to the self-accepted example. 

"Encompassed within the first three" (初三摂) serves to qualify (and thereby exclude) the faults of "indeterminacy" and "contradiction with the thesis." **Section One: Deciding (the Meaning of) "True Hence" (真故決 第一)**

Q: The distinctive ultimate (*viśeṣārthatā*) of the Great Vehicle is not accepted by the Hīnayāna. How can a law not commonly accepted (by both parties) be used as a qualifying restriction (簡別)?

A: There are two meanings. First, there is (the fact that) what is originally present in the Great Vehicle is also accepted by Hīnayāna. The present deep and subtle dharmas of the Great Vehicle are not within the knowledge of Hīnayāna practitioners — hence the qualifying restriction is invoked to exclude the fault of the "learner's world." Second, (Xuanzang) had already, by means of thirty syllogisms, established that the Great Vehicle sūtras are the word of the Buddha. Hence the qualifying restriction is established (on that basis).

Q: Relying on "worldly ultimate truth" (世間勝義), there are three levels. On which of these levels is Consciousness-Only established?

A: The three levels of conventional-ultimate (世俗勝義) all rely on the "gate of expression" (*quán mén*, the linguistic/conceptual approach) to reveal dharmas — hence, relying on each of the three levels, one establishes in all cases "Consciousness-Only, the Three Categories (*tridhātu*), the Four Noble Truths, the Two Kinds of Emptiness, thusness (*tathatā*), etc." Since none of these departs from mind, they are called "Consciousness-Only." The fourth level (of the ultimate) abandons expression (*quán*) and directly addresses the intent (*zhǐ*) — hence relying on this gate, one does not establish "Consciousness-Only," because "Consciousness-Only" transcends the gate of its own expression.

Q: Do the two characters "真故" (true hence) enter into the subject-term (有法, *dharmin*)?

A: They do not.

Q: If so, how does the commentary (*shū*) say "the subject-term is called *true*" (有法言真)?

A: In the course of interpreting the passage, a separate doubt arises with respect to the text. I now consider: at the place where "极成色 (established-in-common form) is the subject-term," one says "真故 (true hence), etc." — from this it is clearly known that (the syllogism) is established relying on the ultimate, not relying on the conventional, and the "真故" is named "pertaining to the subject-term" (有法) because it displays the qualifying restriction. "真" (true) is said (in that context), but it does not function as the subject-term itself. The term "真性" (true nature) in the *Zhǎngzhēn lun* (掌珍論) can be understood following this same principle. If all qualifying-restriction expressions were to count as "subject-terms," then expressions such as "your attachment etc." would all have to be "subject-terms." If that were accepted, it would violate (the rule that): "the inseparable nature of predicate and subject-term is what is called 'establishing the thesis (*pakṣa*), etc.'" (And so on.)

Q: If "真故" (true hence) (denotes) the distinctive ultimate of the Great Vehicle, this syllogism ought to be a "self-inference" (自比量, *svārthānumāna*). How then can it be called a "common inference" (共量, *sādhāraṇānumāna*)?

A: The single expression "真故" contains two intentions. First, relying on the ultimate (勝義), it qualifies out the fault of the "non-learning (world)." Second, relying on the distinctive ultimate of the Great Vehicle (大乗殊勝義), it qualifies out

**Section Two: Deciding (the Meaning of) "Established-in-Common Form" (極成色決 第二)**

Q: "Established-in-common form" (極成色) — if this means the "original-substance form" (本質色, the actual external object), then eye-consciousness ought to apprehend objects external to the mind. If it means "reflected-image form" (影像色, the image within consciousness), then the Hīnayāna school says "the reflected image is precisely the cognitive aspect (*ākāra*)" — so the thesis (*pakṣa*) would commit the fault of "corresponding to what is already established" (相符過, *pakṣābhāsa*).

A: "Established-in-common form" (極成色) is precisely the "original-substance form." The Hīnayāna says the original substance (*mūlavastu*) is the "object-condition" (*ālambana-pratyaya*) and therefore accepts that the mind can apprehend objects external to the mind. Now, with respect to this form, (the syllogism) is called "(that which) does not depart from eye-consciousness."

Q: By what (evidence) does one know that, according to the Great Vehicle's intent, even the original-substance form does not depart from eye-consciousness?

A: The eye-consciousness of a bodhisattva with (supernormal) power takes the (sense) faculty (根, *indriya*) as its object; and the wisdom of accomplishing actions (成所作智, *kṛtyānuṣṭhānajñāna*) also universally takes the eye-faculty as its object. Could the "indirect object-condition" (疎所縁縁, *parokṣa-ālambana-pratyaya*) and the perceiving eye-consciousness be mutually separate? From this it is clearly known: the original-substance form, which is the indirect object-condition, also does not depart from eye-consciousness.

Q: Since "established-in-common / not established-in-common" is applied with respect to the form that is the *object* of cognition (所縁の色), is "established-in-common / not established-in-common" also applied with respect to eye-consciousness as the *subject* of cognition (能縁の識)?

A: Eye-consciousness as the subject of cognition also (admits of) "established-in-common / not established-in-common." The defiled eye-consciousness of all Buddha-bodies is what the Great Vehicle regards as "not established-in-common." The eye-consciousness of Buddhas in the ten directions, and the undefiled eye-consciousness of Buddhas, is what the Hīnayāna regards as "not established-in-common." If stated fully, one should say "form established-in-common in the ultimate sense (真故極成色) does not depart from eye-consciousness established-in-common (極成眼識), etc." — but the text abbreviates this for brevity's sake. If it were not so, it would commit the faults of "one-sided non-establishment of the predicate-qualifier on one's own side and the other side" (自他一分能別不成等の過失).

Q: The expression "existent nature" (有性, *sat*) is commonly accepted by both proponent and opponent, yet through the "challenge via the predicate-dharma" (能別難), because (the reason states) "departing from real existence" (*dravyasattva*), there arises the fault of "contradiction with the intrinsic nature of the subject-term" (有法自相相違, *dharmyātmaviruddha*). (By analogy,) the expression "established-in-common form" (極成色) is also commonly accepted by both proponent and opponent, yet through the "challenge via the predicate-dharma," (namely) "because it is form identical with consciousness" (即識色故), there ought (likewise) to be (this fault).
