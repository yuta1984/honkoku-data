### [Upper Section] (Continuation of the Gate of Divergent Theories on the Cognitive Mode)

[Shénfǎng] collects [the foregoing arguments] and refutes [them, saying]: Furthermore, Master Fàn says: one who explains that "acting upon the aspect-division constitutes the cognitive mode" ends up not extending this to correct wisdom (*shōchi*/正智), because correct wisdom has no aspect-division. Moreover, [as the text indicates,] the functions of receptive sensation (*ryōnō*/領納) and so forth each differ; by this principle, the interpretation that "acting upon the phenomenal characteristic constitutes the cognitive mode" is a *jigōshaku* (持業釈, *karmadhāraya* compound interpretation) [Annotation 1]. And since receptive sensation and the like belong not to the self-witnessing division and so forth but solely to the function of the seeing-division, there is no reason for the self-witnessing division and so forth to each have a separate cognitive mode. Therefore, there is no risk of confusion [etc.].

**The Doctrine of Master Yuance [Woncheuk]:** Now, regarding this cognitive mode and the object of cognition, there is the meaning of mind and object standing in relation to each other. The *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra*, fascicle one, says: "The mind and mental factors (*citta* and *caitasika*) have the same object of cognition but not the same cognitive mode. Unlike the Hīnayāna view [Note: the character 不 may be missing after 縁], [they do] not [have] the same cognitive mode for the same object of cognition." Based on that passage, there are three school interpretations:

**First interpretation:** "Same object of cognition" refers to the original substantial object (*honjitsu-kyō*/本質境), and "not the same cognitive mode" refers to the reflected image object (*eizō-kyō*/影像境). Why is this? For example, in the case of the mind and mental factors of the eye-consciousness, they all cognize [Note: □ is probably 緣] one and the same object that is the original substance — blue and so forth — and therefore it is said they have "the same object of cognition." Yet since the reflected image aspect-divisions (*sōbun*/相分) produced by each of the mind and mental factors respectively differ, it is said they "do not have the same cognitive mode." According to this interpretation, "action" (*gyō*/行) refers to the seeing-division, and "aspect" (*sō*/相) is the aspect-division. Since [the reflected image] is the aspect of the seeing-division, it is [Note: □ is probably 名] called the "cognitive mode." This is a *eishushaku* (依主釈, *tatpuruṣa* compound interpretation) [Annotation 2]. One should not raise the objection: "The eighth consciousness has no original substance [Note: the character 質 seems to be missing after 本]; how then can one speak of a same object of cognition?" — because the aspects transformed by the [other person's] eighth consciousness are used as the original substance.

**Second interpretation:** Taking only the reflected image [aspect-divisions], because they are divided into two [Note: 故 is probably 句], each transforms in a way that resembles one [Note: it appears that 為 is missing after 名, and 一 is missing after 同], and therefore they are called "the same object of cognition." Yet in reality they are not one. Therefore, it is said that "the cognitive mode is not the same." The interpretation of the meaning of "cognitive mode" is the same as the preceding master's explanation.

**Question:** When correct wisdom cognizes suchness (*tathatā*/真如), the substance of the object is one. How can it be called "resembling"? If it directly cognizes the original substance, should there not be no cognitive mode?

**Explanation:** The *Yogācārabhūmi* is provisionally speaking about other forms of consciousness. In reality, correct wisdom uses suchness as

**Third interpretation:** "Same object of cognition" has two meanings: (1) same because it is *one*, as in the case of the suchness-object; (2) same because it *resembles*, as in the case of the reflected image object. ### Gate of Differences in Cognitive Mode Between the Two Schools of Greater and Lesser Vehicle (大小二宗行相不同門)

Among the eighteen schools of Hīnayāna [Annotation 3], excepting the Saṃmatīya (*Shōryōbu*/正量部), all advance the following doctrine: when the mind and so forth cognize an object, there are [Note: the character 二 is probably superfluous] three meanings: (1) object of cognition (*shoen*/所縁), (2) cognitive mode (*gyōsō*/行相), and (3) substantial event (*taiji*/体事). The Saṃmatīya, however, does not establish the aspect-division. The intention of that school is: the mind and so forth directly cognize the external objects of mind; what need is there to additionally establish an aspect-division of the object?

Those (Hīnayāna) schools say: the external objects of mind are called the "object of cognition" (*shoen*); what Mahāyāna calls the aspect-division is called the "cognitive mode" (*gyōsō*); what Mahāyāna calls the seeing-division is called the "substance" (*tai*/体). "Substance" means substantial characteristic (*taiso*/体相). "Event" (*ji*/事) is the substantial characteristic of the aspect-division of the mind and mental factors.

**Question:** Among the Hīnayāna schools, are the names "aspect-division" and "seeing-division" used?

**Answer:** The commentary of Xīmíng [Woncheuk] [Annotation 4] says: there are two interpretations here.

**First interpretation:** That school (Hīnayāna) also uses [Note: the record-citation adds 名 after 分] the terminology of aspect-division and seeing-division, but the meaning differs.

### Gate of the Measure, Measurer, and Result of Measurement (能所量果門)

The three kinds — aspect-division, seeing-division, and self-nature division — are respectively the measured (*shoryō*/所量), the measurer (*nōryō*/能量), and the result of measurement (*ryōka*/量果) [Annotation 6]. They are assigned in that order. This is like using a ruler to measure a physical thing: the thing is the "measured" (aspect-division), the ruler is the "measurer" (seeing-division), and the wisdom that comprehends the numerical result is called the "result of measurement" (self-nature division / self-witnessing division). The same should apply to how the mind and so forth measure (*ryō*/量) — i.e., cognize — objects.

What does "three kinds of results" mean? It means the meaning of *fulfilling the cause* (*jōman'in*/成満因). When the seeing-division cognizes the aspect-division and acts as the measurer, what benefit is there to the measurer if it does not measure the object? It is like a person measuring a thing and thereby arising to a cognitive understanding of the measurement.

In Hīnayāna, the result of measurement is the seeing-division itself. The cognitive mode (*gyōsō*/行相) functions as the "measurer," and the external aspect functions as the "measured." This is somewhat different from [the Mahāyāna account].

Furthermore, various non-Buddhist schools (*gedō*/外道) take the object as the "measured," the wisdom (*chi*/智) as the "measurer," and the self (*ātman*/我) as the "result of measurement," because they calculate that the self is the knower.

**Master Fàn's explanation** [Note: The text cited in the *ki* reads "Master Fàn makes two interpretations, namely: first, the original substantial object is the measured, the aspect-division is the measurer, and the seeing-division is the result of measurement; second, etc." — This differs from the present interpretation; there may be a variant text.]: For one who establishes [two] divisions, the original substantial object [Note: the record-citation reads "aspect-division" for "original substantial object"] is the "measured," and the seeing-division is the "measurer." The clear and definitive character of this seeing-division is then the "result of measurement." Master Kuījī's intention agrees with this.

For one who establishes three divisions: the aspect-division is the "measured," the seeing-division is the "measurer," and the self-witnessing division (*jishō-bun*/自証分) is the "result of measurement."

For one who establishes four divisions: the aspect-division is the "measured [□, probably 量]," the seeing-division [Note: 是 is probably 為] is the "measurer," and the self-witnessing division is the "result of measurement." Further, the seeing-division is the "measured," the self-witnessing division is the "measurer," and the witness-of-witness division (*shō-jishō-bun*/証自証分) is the "result of measurement." Further still, the self-witnessing division is the "measured" [Annotation 7], the witness-of-witness division is the "measurer," and the self-witnessing division itself is the "result of measurement." And the witness-of-witness division is the "measured," the self-witnessing division is the "measurer [農量, probably a scribal error for 能量]," and the witness-of-witness division itself is the "result of measurement" [etc.].

**Question:** Since the seeing-division can serve as the result of measurement for the third division (self-witnessing division), why is it necessary to additionally establish a fourth division as the result for the third?

**Answer:** Because a *non-valid cognition* (*hiiryō*/非量) cannot serve as the result of *direct perception* (*genjō*/現量) [Annotation 8]. It would be contradictory for the seeing-division — which sometimes cognizes the aspect-division as inferential or non-valid cognition — to turn back and cognize the self-witnessing division and thereby become again a direct perception.

**Question:** If that is so, even if the seeing-division cognizes the aspect-division and is sometimes valid, sometimes inferential — what harm is there in the self-witnessing division, which is uniformly direct perception, serving as (the seeing-division's) result? The self-witnessing division is solely direct perception. The measurer can also take inferential or non-valid [cognition] as its result.

**Explanation:** The self-nature (*jitai*/自体) of a direct-perception mind, even when serving as the result for inferential or non-valid [cognition], may be solely direct-perceptual. But inferential and non-valid cognitions of the two kinds do not witness (*shō*/証) the substance. How could they serve as the result of direct perception? Direct perception can serve as the result of inference, but inference cannot serve as the result of direct perception.
