## Left Page: Yuishiki Bunryō-ketsu (Beginning)

# Yuishiki Bunryō-ketsu
## *On the Doctrine of the Four Divisions* / *The Shōjin Comparison* [remainder damaged and illegible]

This work opens the gateway to the treatise and distinguishes two gates of analysis. First, it presents the three divisions of the treatise; second, it explains the meaning of the four divisions.

**First: Presenting the three divisions of the treatise** [Note: the character 者 is likely missing]: The root verses of this treatise contain only the "main exposition" (正說), lacking both the opening and closing divisions. Examining the commentary text [Note: the two characters 判彼 appear as 論其 in one manuscript of the疏], it possesses all three divisions: the opening salutation verse and the following [Note: three characters unclear in original, supplied from the 疏] prose constitute the "Preface of Reverence and Narration" (序敬叙分). From "How is it that the world [Note: character unclear, supplied from 疏] and all the sacred teachings..." onward is called the "Division of Extensive Establishment Based on the Teachings" (依教広成分) [Note: three characters unclear, supplied from 疏]. From "The three divisions of this treatise establish Consciousness-Only..." onward, the third part is the "Division of Explanatory Conclusion and Dedication of Merit" (釈結施願分). This [Note: original completely unclear, supplied from 疏] is the judgment that the commentary text contains these three divisions.

**Second: Explaining the meaning of the four divisions**: Within this there are two parts. First, clarifying the self-nature (自體); second [Note: four characters unclear, supplied from 疏], the extensive question-and-answer.

**First: Clarifying the self-nature** [Note: □ is probably 自]: (1) the *nimitta*-division (相分, the aspect-portion); (2) the *darśana*-division (見分, the seeing-portion); (3) the self-witnessing division (自證分); (4) the self-witnessing-of-self-witnessing division (證自證分) [Note: thirteen characters completely unclear in original, supplied from the *ki*] — these are called the "four divisions."

The sacred teachings of the Buddha largely speak of "one division," not yet distinguishing between the aspect-portion and the seeing-portion. For example, the sūtras [Note: original completely unclear, supplied from the *ki*] say, "The three realms are mind only." From this point onward, until nine hundred years had passed, Asaṅga, Vasubandhu, and others opened this into "two divisions": the aspect-portion and the seeing-portion. "Seeing" (見) takes the meaning of perceiving and apprehending as the cognitive subject (能縁). Because the nature of mind is clear and bright, able to illuminate the objects before it, it is called "seeing." "Aspect" (相) takes the meaning of phenomenal appearance [□-division; Note: □ is rendered as 所縁 in the private commentary (*shiki*)] as its meaning. Because distinctions of phenomenal appearance serve as the objects of mental cognition, they are called "aspects."

However, these two divisions still did not exhaust the principle fully. Therefore, Dignāga composed the *Pramāṇasamuccaya* and other works, and established the "doctrine of three divisions."

To the previous two divisions he added the "self-witnessing division" (自證分). The function of the seeing-division's own nature is nothing other than itself, hence "self." This third division is what cognizes the seeing-division, and is therefore called "self-witnessing" (自證). After this [Note: original completely unclear; estimated as "已後至" or "已後更過"] one hundred years, the Bodhisattva Dharmapāla, relying on the *Ghanavyūha-sūtra* (厚厳経), composed the *Cheng Jia Lun* (成假論) and established the "doctrine of four divisions." To the [previous three divisions] [Note: three characters completely unclear; possibly "前三分" or "第三更三"] he added the "self-witnessing-of-self-witnessing division" (證自證分). The function of the third division's nature is called "self-witnessing." This fourth division cognizes that third division, and is therefore called "self-witnessing-of-self-witnessing" (證自證分).

If we identify the substance based on the names: the aspect-division takes the eighteen realms (十八界) as its substance in general. The remaining [Note: □ is probably 餘] three divisions take the eight consciousnesses and their associated mental factors (心所) as their substance in general. Although this is so in terms of general characteristics, one should elucidate their individual substances in detail according to the "gate of particular characteristics" — as fully described in the text.

**The latter question-and-answer section**: Within this there are in total fifteen gates. [Note: Based on this, this manuscript probably lacks one section on the "gate of mutual objectification of the four divisions" (四分相縁門).] As listed separately below.

### Gate of Clarifying the Name and Resolving Doubts (釈名決疑門)

**Question:** If all the Tathāgatas taught only "one division," not yet distinguishing aspect from seeing, then how does the *Ghanavyūha-sūtra* speak of "two divisions"? For that scripture says: "In all things there is only awareness; the objects of awareness have no existence at all. The aspects of the cognizing and cognized [Note: two characters completely unclear in the original, supplied from the Treatise, fascicle 6] naturally arise and transform." The master (Dharmapāla), in his commentary on this passage, says: "The upper two lines clarify that the inner mind exists and that external objects do not exist [Note: 'do not exist' is completely unclear in the original, supplied from three manuscripts of the 疏]. The lower two lines clarify that within the mind there exist the two divisions of seeing and aspect [Note: four characters completely unclear in original, supplied from the 疏]." [Note: 請 appears in the 疏 as 謂是也.] This resembles the characteristics of the cognizing subject and cognized object. And so forth. From this it is clearly known that the scripture teaches only "two divisions."

**Answer:** In truth, the Tathāgata also [Note: 且 appears as 亦 in the *ki*] taught "four divisions," but since the judgment is made from the perspective of the majority, it is simply [Note: 但 appears as 且 in the *ki*] stated as "one division." Just as the Prajñāpāramitā scriptures of the various schools [Note: the *ki* quote includes the character 経] fully expound the "three natures" (三性), yet the *Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra* judges all the Prajñāpāramitā [scriptures to...] (continues on next page)...
