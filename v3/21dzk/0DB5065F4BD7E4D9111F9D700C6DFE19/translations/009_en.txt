### [Upper Section]

(What exists as dharma-substance is solely) the two natures of dependent origination (*paratantra*, 依他起) and perfect accomplishment (*pariniṣpanna*, 円成実). If those two natures have dharma-substance and remain on the side of existence (*u*, 有), how could this constitute the Middle Way? I fear this would invite the ridicule of other schools. Using (the Sanlun master) Daoxuan's (*Dōsen*, 道詮) critique, one might challenge our school's Middle Way, saying "the Hossō school's Middle Way remains at 'existence,' since the dependent (*paratantra*) and perfectly accomplished (*pariniṣpanna*) are existent" — and so on. This criticism is quite reasonable; how could one deflect it? 

Next, regarding the Middle Way beyond language (*rigen chūdō*, 離言中道): within the two natures of dependent origination and perfect accomplishment, without relying upon the emptiness of the imagined (*parikalpita*, 所執), there is of itself the meaning of "neither empty nor existent." Since the dependent origination has the meaning of illusory-conventional (*koke*, 虚仮) emptiness, and the perfect accomplishment has the meaning of empty-natured (*kūshō*, 空性) emptiness, the dependent and the perfectly accomplished each do not remain at either extreme. To call this the Middle Way beyond language is a transmission of prior eminent teachers (*sende*, 先徳). Is there any meaning other than the three-natures-relational Middle Way that (I) am presently trying to establish? One should reflect upon the advantages and disadvantages relative to that transmission. How (can one) depart from the criticisms of other schools, and how (can one) not depart from them?

**Answer.** The principle that a single dharma, departing from the two extremes, is called the Middle Way goes without saying. What need is there to state it? What I mean by the present "three natures in relation" is precisely that a single dharma is "neither empty nor existent." Consider a single particle of material form (*shikijin*, 色塵) and observe it: it is illusory, empty, and coarse — difficult to determine as either empty or existent. Why so? Because dharmas produced by causes and conditions are not really existent, and because they are not altogether non-existent (*tonmu*, 都無).

That they are "not really existent" is because they are free from the deluded attachment (*mōsha*, 妄執) to a real self and real dharmas — this constitutes the meaning of "the emptiness of the imagined" (*shoshashu*, 所執空義). That they are "not altogether non-existent" is because they retain the dharma-substance of conventional self (*keiga*, 仮我) and conventional dharmas (*keihō*, 仮法) — this constitutes the meaning of "the existence of dependent origination" (*etatsu*, 依他有義). As for the perfectly accomplished (*enjo*, 円成): the ultimate principle that what arises from conditions is neither existent nor empty — precisely because it is far removed from the deluded attachments to a real self and real dharmas — constitutes the meaning of "the emptiness of the imagined." And because its principle is truly real, it constitutes the meaning of "the existence of the perfectly accomplished" (*enjou*, 円成有義).

This "emptiness of the imagined" is an emptiness that blocks and stops (misconstructions), and is by no means a one-sided, partial emptiness. That is to say, blocking and stopping all deluded attachments — whether to existence, non-existence, identity, difference, both together, or not together, and so on — is collectively called "emptiness." Such deluded attachments have no separate meaning other than: grasping the dependent and perfectly accomplished as existent, or grasping them as non-existent, or grasping them as both existent and non-existent, or grasping them as neither existent nor non-existent;

or similarly grasping them as identical or as different, and so on. These are various and unlimited, immeasurable and boundless. After all these boundless deluded attachments are entirely blocked and stopped, would the dependent and perfectly accomplished that are thereby revealed be one-sidedly existent? Now these three natures are likewise "not one, not different." Within a single particle of material form there exist such ineffable and ineffable wondrous principles. Exhaustively discussing them, there are three levels of principle within a single particle of material form. Examining them in fine detail, within each of those three levels, each and every one departs from the two extremes — there is not a single moment of thought that is not in accord with the Middle Way. Discussing the ultimate, all (these principles) arrive at wordlessness (*zetsugon*, 絶言). All phenomena are completely like this. What intelligent person could criticize this as "a teaching of mere existence or mere emptiness," or as "a school whose dharma-substance is one-sidedly existent"?

It is simply a matter of failing to understand the main path of the teaching that "self and dharmas are not existent, and consciousness-emptiness is not non-existent," and rashly doubting the true teaching of Maitreya (*Jishi*, 慈氏) transmitted from the Avataṃsaka (*Kegon*) tradition (*Kegonzō denrai*, 华蔵伝来). As for what Daoxuan criticizes: when he says "(our school) remains at existence," what does he mean by "remains" — does it mean remaining at the existence of the Middle Way, or remaining at a one-sided, partial existence? If he says "remaining at the existence of the Middle Way," then I (= the Hossō school) place (existence) within the Middle Way, and he (= Daoxuan) may well be remaining at partial, one-sided emptiness — where is the gain and where is the loss? How much more so in the case of the Middle Way beyond language, whose substance is wondrous and transcends speech. If (he means) "remaining at the existence of one-sided partial existence," then (note that our school) blocks all deluded attachments to existence, non-existence, identity, difference, and so on — how could the dependent and perfectly accomplished that we uphold be said to "remain at partial existence"? What is meant by "existence that is not (merely) existent" (*fuyu no u*, 不有之有) is precisely this.

If one says "because the three natures are one-directionally each distinct (*kakubetsu*, 各別), they do not constitute the Middle Way," then one does not see the teaching of "neither identical nor separate" (*fusoku furi*, 不即不離). The root sūtras, root treatises, the ten ancillary treatises (*jūshi no shōron*, 十支諸論), and the interpretations of the school's patriarchs provide (this teaching in) a richness that does not require special advocacy. Not only do they clarify the meaning of "neither identical nor separate," but regarding the notion of "one-directional mutual identity" (*ichijō sōsoku*, 一向相卽), the Tathāgata himself offered various refutations, and those warnings are especially severe. One should open (these texts) and examine them. If one says "I too establish the meaning of 'neither identical nor separate' and am not (advocating) one-directional mutual identity,"

If one says "in the Hossō (school's teaching of) 'neither identical nor separate,' although it says 'neither identical nor separate,' (individual) distinctness (*kakubetsu*, 各別) is in fact still the foundation" — well, upon what authority is that judgment made? Evidence can be produced from the root sūtras and root treatises.

Furthermore, (one might grant that) even if you say "neither identical nor separate," it is doubtless the case that mutual identity (*sōsoku*, 相卽) is the underlying principle (in the teachings you study). [Note: annotation suggests 段 may be a miswriting of 設] Since such discourse (*dan*, 談) is consistently present in the teachings you study. If that is the case, on what basis of your (own) view do you single out our school to vigorously criticize (its teaching of) the Middle Way? The individual characteristics (*shōsō*, 性相) of the eight consciousnesses, mind-kings (*shinō*, 心王), mental factors (*shinsho*, 心所), and so on, are all matters of the gate of conventional designation (*sezoku seshō*, 世俗施設). They are not discourse at the gate of the true body (*shinjitsu shin no mon*, 真実身門). That conventional discourse about individual distinctions is all illusory and fictitiously differentiated; because there is no establishment (*anryū*, 安立) as a wondrous existence (*myōu*, 妙有), when it is pushed into the gate of the true, it is all "neither identical nor separate." The many passages of commentary in the *Cheng weishi lun* and elsewhere are also clear on this principle.

However, the Middle Way beyond language (*rigen chūdō*) is precisely the meaning of completely abandoning the gate of verbal expression (*senmon*, 詮門) — (which includes formulas) such as "self and dharmas are not existent," "emptiness-consciousness is not non-existent," and so on — stopping all language, and cutting off all discrimination. "The path of language is severed, the functioning of mind extinguished" — that is this. This constitutes the Fourth (level of) Ultimate Truth (*daishi shōgi*, 第四勝義), the realm of the right wisdom (*shōchi*, 正智) of the one-mind Dharma-realm (*isshin hōkai*, 一心法界). This is entirely and exclusively a matter of self-inner-realization (*jinaishō*, 自内証), and is not a gate opened and presented to others. The present (question concerns) the opening and exposition to others of (what is in itself) self-inner-realization — that is, the teaching (*hōmon*, 法門) of the three natures in relation (*sansen sōtai*, 三性相対): neither empty nor existent, neither identical nor separate. Therefore, within the gate of exposition (*kaisetsu no mon*, 開説門), the Middle Way of the three natures in relation is taken as the ultimate. The two-line verse (*nigyō no ju*, 二行頌) of the Venerable Maitreya (*Jison*, 慈尊), when opening and revealing the self-inner-realization to the faculties (*ki*, 機) of Asaṅga (*Mujaku*, 無著) and others, explains the meaning of the three natures in relation, and has a particularly deep purport. When one inwardly realizes and knows this three-natures relation, it is precisely the Middle Way in which "in the true ultimate (*shin shōgi*, 真勝義), the path of mind and language is severed." Therefore, the two-level Middle Way is in reality one single principle — it is merely the difference between relying upon verbal expression (*sen*, 詮) and abandoning verbal expression (*hisen*, 廃詮). As for relying upon verbal

expression: those who verbally express the gate of the meaning of "not empty" correspond to the dependent and perfectly accomplished, while those who expound the gate of the meaning of "not existent" correspond to the imagined — and so (the teaching) verbally expresses (formulas) such as "self and dharmas are not existent" (*gahō hiu*, 我法非有), and so forth. "Abandoning verbal expression" means abandoning the wisdom (*chi*, 智) of such verbal deliberation, (arriving at) the meaning of having nothing whatsoever that is attained.

Now, with regard to the one tradition of prior eminent teachers that holds the dependent and perfectly accomplished to inherently have the meaning of "neither empty nor existent" — I do not intend to block this from the outset. That the dependent and perfectly accomplished have the meaning of emptiness by nature is beyond question; if it were not so, and they were one-directionally existent, how could the dependent and perfectly accomplished converge on existence (as the Middle Way)? This goes without saying. Since they already have a middle-way existence, even though existent they are also empty. Therefore the dependent is "neither empty nor existent," and the perfectly accomplished is "neither empty nor existent." However, when one speaks of "the emptiness meaning of the dependent," what is being emptied? If one says the emptiness meaning lies in "real non-existence" (*jitsumu*, 実無), what does that emptiness point to? What is absent in that non-existence? Is it not the absence of rigid attachment to a real self and real dharmas? Illusory-conventional dharmas are not inherently solid and real; dharmas arising through conditions do not naturally arise by themselves — this is the meaning of "real non-existence." Is this not precisely "the emptiness of the imagined"? The meaning of "not existent" in the perfectly accomplished is likewise precisely the meaning of being far removed from the imagined (*parikalpita*). What other separate meaning could there be beyond this? "Empty-nature (*kūshō*, 空性)" — is this not the meaning of "empty-nature" as an object of attachment?

One must understand: the dharma-body (*hōtai*, 法体) is subtly wondrous and inherently neither empty nor existent; those who verbally express (it) converge upon the three natures, while those who (go) beyond verbal expression converge upon the one reality (*ichijitsu*, 一実). If one says "directly empty out the portion of conventional existence in the dependent, and empty out the portion of true existence in the perfectly accomplished," there is absolutely no basis for this. The two natures — dependent origination and perfect accomplishment — are already within the realm of saintly wisdom (*shōchi kyōkai*, 聖智境界); their substance is not a deluded object (*mōkyō*, 妄境). What fault (can one find to justify) dismissing them? If one dismisses them without justification, there is a risk of completely negating all (dharmas) and falling into the view of annihilationism (*mōken*, 妄見). Clearly one should know: the emptiness meaning upon the dependent and perfectly accomplished is the meaning that the dependent and perfectly accomplished are, from the outset, free from deluded attachment (*mōsha*, 妄執). It is for this reason that (anyone who), without relying on the "emptiness of the imagined" (*shoshashu*, 所執空), separately discusses the emptiness of the dependent and perfectly accomplished outside and apart from the "emptiness of the imagined," would likely contradict the (spirit of the) text and principle (*bunri*, 文理).
