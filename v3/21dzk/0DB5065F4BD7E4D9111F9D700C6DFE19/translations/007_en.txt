## Right Page (Zoku Hossō-shū Shoshin Ryakuyō, Lower Volume — Section 12)

### [Upper Section]

Regarding (the relationship between) principle (*ri*, 理) and phenomena (*ji*, 事), scholars of our school (Hossō-shū) from ancient times have discussed this matter and established various positions. Some say there is a separate substance (*bettai*, 別体); others say (the true nature is) the principle inherent in all dharmas (*shohō no dōri*, 諸法道理). Within these two positions, there are further various divergent views. Some say it is "the principle of real non-existence" (*jitsumu no dōri*, 実無道理); some say "the principle of conventional existence" (*keū no dōri*, 仮有道理); some say "the principle of conventional existence and real non-existence" (*keū jitsumu no dōri*, 仮有実無道理); some say "the individual principles of form (*rūpa*), mind (*citta*), and so on" (*shikishin-tō kakukaku no dōri*, 色心等各各道理); some say "the principle of dependent origination" (*ensh no dōri*, 縁生道理); and some say "the natural principle of *dharmatā*" (*hōni no dōri*, 法爾道理). Each of these positions cites supporting textual evidence and establishes a reasoned argument; the points of entry differ in each case. Yet the destination they ultimately arrive at will in the end not contradict one another. Nevertheless, following the transmission of my teacher, I maintain the position of the "principle of dependent origination." Its general outline is as follows.

All conditioned (*saṃskṛta*, 有為) dharmas: when conditions are present they necessarily arise; [Note: The commentary suggests a character, likely *mu* 無, may be missing here] when conditions are absent, they definitely do not arise. If this principle (*ri*) were not (inherently) present, then even when conditions exist, (dharmas) could not arise; and even without conditions, they would be able to arise. And yet, when conditions come, they necessarily arise; when conditions are absent, they necessarily do not arise. This is precisely because there is necessarily an inherent principle. This principle (*ri*) does not arise. It does not cease. It is not that this principle comes into being for the first time when a dharma arises. The principle is inherently present from the beginning; when conditions come and accord with this principle, that event (*ji*, 事) is able to come to be. Therefore, events have arising, but the principle has no arising. Nor is it that when a dharma ceases, this principle ultimately ceases. For those (events) where conditions have expired and no longer accord with the principle, while those events have cessation, the principle has no cessation. Here one should come to understand: the principle of arising and ceasing is, conversely, non-arising and non-ceasing. That which is non-arising and non-ceasing — the (principle as) event (*ri-ji*, 理事) — conversely undergoes arising and ceasing (in terms of phenomena). Phenomena (*ji*) serve as characteristic (*sō*, 相); principle (*ri*) serves as nature (*shō*, 性). Nature is of one flavor (*ichimi*, 一味). The principle of dependent origination is one and not two; therefore (in terms of nature) there are not many characteristics. The phenomenal characteristics of dependent origination are various and not one; therefore (in terms of characteristics) they are manifold.

When four conditions (*shien*, 四縁) combine, the event of mental activity (*enryo no koto*, 縁慮事) comes to be; when two conditions combine (i.e., direct cause and dominant condition), the event of material resistance (*shichige no koto*, 質礙事) comes to be. Within mental activity (*enryo*), there are the many varieties of mind-kings (*cittarāja*, 心王), mental factors (*caitasika*, 心所), and the eight consciousnesses; within material resistance (*shichige*), there are the countless differentiations of the five sense-faculties (*pañcendriya*, 五根) and five sense-objects (*pañcaviṣaya*, 五塵). All come to be as individual events through their respective causes and conditions (*innen*, 因縁). The two types of seeds — innately present seeds (*ponnu*, 本有) and newly habituated seeds (*shinkun*, 新熏) — [together with]

the three kinds of habituating impressions (*vāsanā*, 習気) — those of designations (*nāmavāsanā*, 名言習気), of self-grasping (*ātmagrahavāsanā*, 我執習気), and so on — each come to be as individual seeds through conditions such as the perfuming present-activities (*nōkun no gengyō*, 能熏現行) and the cognizing eighth consciousness (*nōen no daihachi*, 能緣第八) and so forth.

In this way, though the countless instances of dependent co-arising (*pratītyasamutpāda*, 縁起) and dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpanna*, 縁生) are various and manifold, the principle of dependent origination (*ensh no ri*, 縁生理) is of one flavor (*ichimi*) and free from the differentiation of many characteristics. Although it is without the differentiation of many characteristics, it constitutes the nature (*shō*, 性) of each individual dharma; the principle of dependent origination of form, the principle of dependent origination of mind, and so on, each give rise to their respective individual events.

This principle, being already (the) nature (*shō*), has no characteristic (*musō*, 無相). Being without characteristic, it is not an object for the deluded mind (*meijō no kyō*, 迷情境). Not being an object for the deluded mind, it is far removed from (the deluded mind's) grasped constructions (*shosha*, 所執). The dependently arisen (*paratantra*, 依他起) — being like an illusion — is also not an object for the deluded mind; yet because it has a conventionally fictitious (*koke*, 虚仮) characteristic, the deluded mind becomes confused by it and takes it as the direct object of its conceptual proliferation (*parikalpita*, 遍計). Because (the dependently arisen) is not the same as the ultimate truth's (*shinri*, 真理) lack of characteristics, the expression "far removed (from grasped constructions)" is applied exclusively to the perfectly accomplished (*pariniṣpanna*, 円成) ultimate truth. One should know: the perfectly accomplished ultimate truth is the nature (*shō*) of the principle of dependent origination possessed by the event of dependent co-arising and dependent origination — (the nature of) being permanently far removed from grasped constructions. The statement "(The perfectly accomplished real nature) is always far removed from the former nature (the imagined nature) with respect to the dependently arisen" conveys precisely this meaning.

Therefore, the *zhèng* (正, "correct") (in *biànijsuoshu*, 遍計所執) refers to the principle of dependent origination, and the *biàn* (遍, "pervasive") (in *biànijsuoshu*) refers to the principle of the delusive grasping of "what is present in subjective experience (*jō*, 情) but absent as principle (*ri*, 理)." Now, this principle of "present in experience but absent as principle" ultimately returns to the principle of dependent origination itself. If one fully knows the principle of dependent origination, then the grasped-at quality of "present in experience but absent as principle" must necessarily be known along with it. That the principle of delusively grasping at the principle of dependent origination is itself the *tathatā* (真如) and also the perfectly accomplished real nature — this is precisely what is meant.

**Question:** That the principle of conditioned existence (*ue no dōri*, 有為道理) is itself the ultimate truth (*shinri*, 真理) is open to various doubts. To begin with, distinguishing dharmas into the three levels of phenomena (*ji*, 事), principle (*ri*, 理), and suchness (*nyoi*, 如) is standard teaching. Among these, "principle" refers to the meanings of suffering (*duḥkha*, 苦), emptiness (*śūnya*, 空), impermanence (*anitya*, 無常), and so on, inherent in all dharmas — (a principle) that has no substance apart from the substance of the dharmas themselves. If this is taken to be identical to "suchness" (*nyoi*, 如), then how is the two-level distinction between principle (*ri*) and suchness (*nyoi*) to be made? The two dharmas of conditioned and unconditioned (*ue* and *mue*) would be thrown into great confusion. *Tathatā* (真如) would also end up having no substance. Furthermore, such a principle is already known by ordinary people (*bonbu*, 凡夫), who are also able to speak of it. If that

principle is the *tathatā*, then how could an ordinary person be said to have realized (證) the principle? And how could a foolish person be able to expound the principle of reality's true nature (*jissō no ri*, 実相理)? Next, regarding this principle: the notion that all things are inherently subject to eventual cessation and extinction (*sha-metsu*, 謝滅) also cannot be established. If, through this gateway (of "the principle of dependent origination"), one takes the meaning of "the ultimate truth's non-arising and non-ceasing" as established, then the individual principles of form (*rūpa*), mind (*citta*), and so forth, and indeed even the various principles (*dōri*) established when discussing right and wrong (*zehi*, 是非) with regard to all worldly things such as goods, implements, and dwellings — all of these would have to be *tathatā* (真如). Why then is the "principle of conventional existence and real non-existence" (*keū-jitsumu-dōri*, 仮有実無道理) rejected? Next, what the present (teacher's position) is trying to establish is the principle of dependent origination as the natural principle of *dharmatā* (*hōni*, 法爾); then why would the position of the "natural principle of *dharmatā*" (*hōni-dōri*, 法爾道理) itself be rejected? Next, even granting that the principle of dependent origination is of one flavor (*ichimi*, 一味), (the claim that) this can establish the meaning of the individual natures (*shō*, 性) of form, mind, and so on remains unclear in its purport. If the substance of the principle (*ritai*, 理体) were not each distinct, it would be in total opposition to the many and various phenomenal characteristics (*jisō*, 事相); how could it serve as their nature? Therefore, in the *Yilin zhang* (*Girinju*, 義林章), it is stated: "However, on each dharma there is individually its own principle; it is individually certified in inner (realization) and cannot be said to be common (to all)." Furthermore, if one were to use the passage "because principle is one and not two" as evidence, one should respond: "The passage in question is a text that recognizes both the 'one' and the 'different' sides with regard to principle — it would be altogether inconsistent with the text to forcibly establish the position that (the principle) has a single substance." Above all, if the ultimate truth is uniformly "one," then it can be expressed by the word "one" — why is it called "beyond words" (*rigen*, 離言)? And since it already has "one characteristic" (*isso*, 一相), why is it called "without characteristic" (*musō*, 無相)? Next: if the perfectly accomplished real nature (*pariniṣpanna*, 円成実性) is not the imagined (*parikalpita*, 遍計所執), then why do (certain people) grasp at Buddha-nature (*busshō*, 仏性) as "self" (*ātman*, 我)? And when the mental factors (*caitasika*, 心所) of ordinary people contemplate the ultimate truth with defiled intelligence (*sāsravajñāna*, 有漏智), how could that not be (an instance of) the imagined (*parikalpita*)? Next, as for the claim that when one knows dependent co-arising and dependent origination, the grasped-at quality of "present in experience but absent as principle" (*jō'u ri'mu*, 情有理無) must necessarily be known along with it — what is the meaning of this? If we follow the commentarial explanations (*shoshaku*, 疏釈), deluded grasping and dependent origination each have their own principle, and both are called "ultimate truth." Why then does one forcibly take the principle of dependent origination and attempt to subsume within it the principle of deluded grasping?

If (the deluded grasping) returns (to the principle of dependent origination) on the grounds that it is "one principle," then conversely one could take the principle of deluded grasping and make the principle of dependent origination return to it — making everything (amount to) the principle of deluded grasping. Could one then say that the principle of deluded grasping is named the "ultimate truth"?

### [Lower Section]

**Answer:** If one were to say that "apart from the conditioned (*saṃskṛta*) dharmas of the conventional truth, there exists separately a real dharma called *tathatā*" — this is precisely the position established by the other vehicles (i.e., the lesser vehicles), and is it not the case that this is not the Great Vehicle (*Mahāyāna*, 大乗) understanding? The statement "unlike other schools, there is no real, permanent dharma apart from form (*rūpa*), mind (*citta*), and so forth that is called *tathatā*" refers to the text where the author of the treatise (i.e., Dharmapāla and other Yogācāra masters) refutes the lesser vehicle's position of "real, existent unconditioned (dharmas)," and its import is clear. As for the (view) "apart from conditioned dharmas — that is, apart from form, mind, and so forth as such — there exists a non-arising, non-ceasing dharma called *tathatā*": this is not asserted at all. 

However, *dharmatā* (法性) is subtle and difficult to know. Even though one must bring discursive thought (*genryo*, 言慮) to a halt, one cannot know what is "beyond words" (*rigen*, 離言) (by) not knowing it; and one who does not engage in contemplation (*ryo*, 慮) cannot arrive at the state "beyond contemplation" (*furyo*, 不慮). When (the matter) must be expressed in words, one conveys, through the principles of conditioned existence (*ue no dōri*, 有為道理), the *dharmatā* and ultimate truth that one wishes to know. Therefore, at the time of initial study (*shoshin*, 初心), what fault is there in using the principles of ordinary things to infer and understand the "suchness of ordinary principles" (*jōri*, 常理) of true reality?

Therefore, regarding "ultimate truth" (*shinri*, 真理): "true" (*shin*, 真) means true reality (*shinjitsu*, 真実); "principle" (*ri*, 理) means reasoned principle (*dōri*, 道理). It is the "true and real principle" (*shinjitsu no dōri*, 真実道理) that is called "ultimate truth." This true and real principle is not something constructed by the transformations of mind (*shin no henjisa*, 心変作), nor is it the functioning-use (*giyō*, 義用) of a dharma; rather, it is, within all dharmas, the true meaning (*shingi*, 真義) that is naturally, permanently, and unmovingly (*gyōnen*, 凝然) present and completely accomplished (*guzu jōju*, 具足成就) in the manner of "as always" (*jōjo*, 如常). This true meaning is precisely the component of the common-characteristic principle (*kyōsō no dōri*, 共相道理) that has gone to the ultimate extreme and transcended the flux (*sENRYU*, 遷流) and fabrication (*zōsa*, 造作) (of conditioned existence). This component is already the true reality within dharmas. How can it be said to have "no substance"? And since it is already distinct from conditioned (dharmas), how can one fail to distinguish the two dharmas of conditioned (*ue*) and unconditioned (*mue*)?

Therefore, in the three-level schema of phenomena (*ji*, 事), principle (*ri*, 理), and suchness (*nyoi*, 如): what is called "principle (*ri*)" is the constructed principle of mind — the functioning-use of impermanence, suffering, and so on. This is a dharma of arising and ceasing (*shōmetsu-hō*, 生滅法). When this is analyzed in terms of the two-level schema of phenomena and principle (*jiri niju*, 事理二重), it conversely belongs to "phenomena" (*ji*). What is called "suchness" (*nyoi*, 如) is that unmoving principle (*gyōnen no ri*, 凝然理) of non-arising and non-ceasing. This is the true *dharmatā* (法性). When this is distinguished in terms of the two-level schema of phenomena and principle, it conversely belongs to "principle" (*ri*). (This principle continues...)
