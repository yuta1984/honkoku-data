## Right Page (Zoku Hossō-shū Shoshin Ryakuyō, Lower Volume — Section 14)

### [Upper Section]

(The "principle" [*ri*, 理]) is the meaning (*gi*, 義) upon phenomena (*ji*, 事); and "phenomena-and-principle" (*jiri*, 事理) means phenomena that exist as substance (*tai*, 体) upon the principle. This purport is necessarily so. Therefore, when one speaks of "phenomena and principle" (*jiri*), conditioned (dharmas) (*ue*, 有為) serve as the substance of the dharma, while the principle-meaning (*giri*, 義理) is unconditioned (*mue*, 無為) above it. Now this "phenomena-and-principle" is also called "characteristic-and-nature" (*sōshō*, 相性). "Characteristic" (*sō*, 相) is the characteristic-functioning (*sōyū*, 相用) upon the nature; "nature" (*shō*, 性) is the substance-nature (*taishō*, 体性) below the characteristic. Therefore, when one speaks of "characteristic-and-nature," the unconditioned serves as the substance of the dharma, and the conditioned serves as its "principle-suchness" (*rinyoi*, 理如) above it. When (one further distinguishes) into three levels — analyzing "suchness" (*nyoi*, 如) from out of "phenomena" so as to form the three-level schema — it is a matter of opening up "suchness" from "phenomena" to make three levels. Saying this does not deny that "the ultimate truth is (a form of) principle" — there is absolutely no contradiction.

When an ordinary person (*bonbu*, 凡夫) deliberates about the principle of things, there is necessarily a mental transformation of the aspect-part (*sōbun*, 相分 — the objective representation). This aspect-part is itself conditioned. Confused by this illusory dependently-arisen (*paratantra*, 依他起) characteristic, (the ordinary person) is imbued with the characteristics of self (*ātman*, 我) and dharma (*dharma*, 法), and gives rise to words in accordance with (their) grasped constructions (*shosha*, 所執). Since (such a person) does not yet comprehend even the dependently-arisen characteristic that appears in the mind, how much less could they comprehend the truly real principle (*shinjitsu no dōri*, 真実道理)? It is difficult for an ordinary person to realize the principle, and (that realization) cannot easily come about. Moreover, when a foolish person argues about right and wrong concerning property and so forth, even though some may articulate a principle, they mostly go against the highest principle. And even if they occasionally happen upon the right principle, it is by no means the ultimate manifestation of the inconceivable dependent co-arising and dependent origination of all dharmas. How could one say they are "expounding the manifestation of ultimate reality (*jissō no gen*, 実相現)"?

Next, as for the position that the individual principles of form (*rūpa*), mind (*citta*), and so on, and the "principle of conventional existence and real non-existence" (*keū-jitsumu-dōri*), are all called "ultimate truth" — I certainly do not intend to block this position. What the present (teaching) is trying to establish is that all such principles of this kind ultimately return to the single principle of dependent origination. Because the principle (of dependent origination) has no characteristic and is of one flavor (*ichimi*, 一味), that is fundamental. Because phenomena have characteristics, being each distinct is fundamental. When one wishes to understand the principle of one flavor, what fault is there in pointing to the single principle of dependent origination underlying all phenomena?

"Conventional existence and real non-existence" is precisely because (all things) are dharmas produced from conditions (*innen shōhō*, 因縁所生法). What separate meaning could there be outside the principle of dependent origination? Let me indicate this by pointing to a single example. When the ancients first constructed a boat, the principle by which carving wood and placing it upon the water could enable it to float, carry loads, and move — this did not come into existence for the first time when (the boat) was then being made. That principle had existed from the beginning. However, when the skilled craftsman, using the conditions of hands, feet, axe, saw,

### [Lower Section]

and so forth, accorded with that principle, the event of a ship (*fune*, 船) was able to come into being. When this thing finally ceases (is destroyed), the principle that making it in such a way can conform to such a state does not itself further cease. It is simply that the force of those conditions was exhausted, and so that event came to a stop. From this, one broadly understands that while phenomena arise and cease, the principle does not arise and cease. Moreover, phenomena take the principle as their nature (*shō*, 性), and do not depart from the principle. (Phenomena) arise from the principle, are not identical with the principle, and are not separated from the principle. To speak of "principle" is simply to mean "how phenomena ought to be." "Phenomena" are the form (the actuality) of the principle already being so. Yet arising-ceasing and permanent-abiding, having characteristic and being without characteristic, are difficult to identify as identical and difficult to separate. It is like water and waves. Waves are produced (*zōsa*, 造作) by conditions such as wind, have arising and ceasing, and have form and appearance. Water is not so. Yet waves are nothing but water, and outside of water there are no waves. The relationship between the principle of dependent origination and the phenomenal characteristics of dependent origination is one of neither identity nor separation. The meaning can be understood from this.

The indication by pointing to a single example is now complete. As for all other phenomenal characteristics — one should know that each and every one is likewise as follows. That is: the principle that when four conditions combine, the event of mental activity (*enryo*, 縁慮) can come to be, is inherently and naturally (*hōni*, 法爾) present. For material form (*shiki*, 色): the principle that when two conditions combine, the event of material resistance (*shichige*, 質礙) can come to be, is inherently and naturally present. For the eight consciousnesses within mind, the five sense-objects (*gojin*, 五塵) within form, and so on — each and every one has its own set of conditions, and the principle by which their respective events can come to be is inherently and naturally present. The countless principles are all dependent co-arising and dependent origination, and thus are (all) conventional existence and real non-existence. Therefore, the individual principles of form, mind, and so forth, and the general principle of dependent origination, are not at all separate principles. Speaking from the perspective of phenomena, they are individual natures (*kakukaku no shō*, 各各性); pointing directly to the principle, they are of one flavor and not two. The commentary in the *Yilin zhang* (*Girinju*, 義林章) explains this meaning. In the passage that describes the gate of individual distinction, it says "however, on each dharma there is individually its own principle"; in the passage that takes conditioned dharmas as the starting point and illuminates the gate of one-flavor, it says "because principle is one and not two." The intent of speaking while pointing directly to the principle as its own substance is clear.

As for what is each and individually distinct, there must necessarily be its direct causal condition (*shinninen*, 親因縁). Next, when one grasps at Buddha-nature (*busshō*, 仏性) as "self" (*ātman*, 我), what is directly confused is the dependently-arisen aspect-part (*sōbun*, 相分) that appears within the mind. The same may be applied when a scholarly ordinary person (*bonbu*, 凡夫) contemplates the ultimate truth. The statement "the ultimate truth is not the imagined (*parikalpita*, 遍計所執)" blocks, from the outset, being directly (*shin*, 親) the object of imagined (conceptual) construction. Becoming (its object) in an indirect (*tenten*, 展転), secondary sense may indeed occur.

Next, conditioned (dharmas) that arise through conditions (*ensh no hō*, 縁生法) definitely do not arise spontaneously (*jinen ni shōzu*, 自然生). Since they do not arise spontaneously, they are not really existent (*jitsuu*, 実有). Therefore they are necessarily conventionally existent (*keū*, 仮有). When the mind of delusion (*mōjō*, 妄情) discriminates them as really existent, the characteristic of real existence (*jitsuu no sō*, 実有相) appears conforming to (the structure of) the deluded mind. How could this characteristic exist as principle? Since this meaning is necessary (i.e., necessarily follows), one who knows the principle of dependent origination also knows from it the principle of deluded grasping (*mōsha no dōri*, 妄執道理). If one opens up and discusses them (separately), there should be the principle of deluded grasping and the principle of dependent origination (as two). But if one subsumes (them) according to what is real, it is the single principle of dependent origination. The meaning that the principle of deluded grasping returns to the principle of dependent origination is also not something I intend to block. However, the meaning of that (alternative reading) is still confused. (The position of) taking (all as) the single principle of dependent origination is better, and one can understand it in that way too.

Next, I have not yet studied the transmission concerning the "principle of natural *dharmatā*" (*hōni dōri no den*, 法爾道理之伝) in a broad way. However, it is doubtlessly the case that the meaning of the principle of natural *dharmatā* and the meaning of the principle of dependent origination constitute separate transmissions (*kakubetsu no den*, 各別伝). It is also necessarily the case that the meaning of the principle of dependent origination ultimately returns to the principle of natural *dharmatā*. Reasoning from this, the transmission of the principle of natural *dharmatā* is perhaps only concerned with entrusting phenomena to "natural *dharmatā*" (*hōni*, 法爾), and does not necessarily seek (to investigate it) in relation to the principle of dependent origination.

## On the Middle Way (*chūdō*, 中道)

The transmissions of the prior eminent teachers (*sende*, 先徳) are each different. Scholars also hold divergent views. However, examining the text as it stands in the (*Yilin*) *zhang* (章), the Middle Way of our school (Hossō-shū) has, in broad outline, two gates. The first is the Middle Way expressible in words (*gonsen chūdō*, 言詮中道). The second is the Middle Way beyond words (*rigen chūdō*, 離言中道).

The Middle Way expressible in words: with the three natures (*sansen*, 三性) set in relation, the imagined (*parikalpita*, 所執) is not existence, the dependent (*paratantra*, 依) and the perfectly accomplished (*pariniṣpanna*, 圓) are not empty; by departing from existence (*u*, 有) and departing from non-existence (*mu*, 無), it is called the Middle Way. This accords with the verse (*kǒng*, 頌) of the *Madhyāntavibhāga* (*Chūbenron*, 中辺論):

> "Deluded discrimination (*abhūtaparikalpa*, 虚妄分別) exists here [= has existence].  
> The two (subject and object, *grāhya-grāhaka*, 二取) are entirely absent.  
> Within this (deluded discrimination) there is only emptiness (*śūnyatā*).  
> Within that (emptiness) this (deluded discrimination) also exists.  
> Therefore one says all dharmas are neither empty nor non-empty,  
> Because of the existence of (*abhi-parikalpa*), the non-existence (of the two), and the existence (of emptiness)."

This is precisely in accord with the Middle Way. [Annotation: citation from text]. This expounds the Middle Way expressible in words. Therefore the (*Yilin*) zhang says, "Relying upon verbal expression (*gonsen*, 言詮)," and cites the verse taught by Maitreya (*Jishi*, 慈氏): "Deluded discrimination exists..." (etc.).

The Middle Way beyond words (*rigen chūdō*, 離言中道): when the Middle Way of this three-natures relation is inwardly realized (*naishō*, 内証), (the realizing wisdom) does not reach the discriminations of the emptiness of the imagined (*shosha*) and the existence of the dependent and perfectly accomplished (*ien*, 依圓). Because it depends upon the profound principle of the three natures, the wisdom has nothing whatsoever that is attained. Because mind and language (*shinpgen*, 心言) are cut off, it is called the Middle Way. This is the passage in the *Cheng weishi lun* (*Yuishikiron*, 唯識論): "In the true ultimate (*shin shōgichi*, 真勝義中), because mind and language are cut off..." and so forth. Therefore the (*Yilin*) zhang says: "In the realm of ultimate truth (*shōgitai*, 勝義諦), although the substance of all dharmas either exists or does not exist (and so forth), because language does not reach (it), it is neither empty nor existent. This establishes the teaching of consciousness-only (*yuishiki*, 唯識). In the realm of ultimate truth, because mind and language are cut off, it is neither empty nor existent." [Annotation: citation from text.]
