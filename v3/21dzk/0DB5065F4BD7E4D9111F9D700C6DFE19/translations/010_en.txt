## Right Page (Zoku Hossō-shū Shoshin Ryakuyō, Lower Volume — Section 18)

### [Upper Section]

(If one deeply seeks and studies the transmissions of prior eminent teachers,) in the end there will likely be no contradiction (with our school's position).

**Question.** The principle that the Middle Way of the three natures in relation ultimately returns to the Middle Way beyond language (*rigen chūdō*, 離言中道) is not yet fully clear. Please explain it in detail. Next, regarding the intent of Bhāviveka (*Seiben*, 清弁) and others: they establish the position that in a single dharma at a single moment, at the conventional level (*zoku*, 俗) it is "existent," and at the ultimate level (*shin*, 真) it is "non-existent." Since already within a single moment of thought the two gates of emptiness and existence encompass each other without mutual contradiction, when discussed together this is precisely "neither empty nor existent." How then can one say that your view is in accord with one-sided emptiness? If, because of being empty at the ultimate level (*shōgi*, 勝義), (Bhāviveka) is said to accord with one-sided emptiness, then Dharmapāla (*Gohō*, 護法), being existent at the ultimate level, would also accord with one-sided existence. How then can one say that our view accords with the Middle Way? Furthermore, is the view that the dharma-substance (*hōtai*, 法体) of the dependent and perfectly accomplished is directly emptied altogether absent in our own school? If one says it is absent, then the criticism that the dharma-substance remains at existence would still be inescapable. For this reason, among the two transmissions of "the two naturelessnesses," the "dharma-substance-emptiness transmission" (*taikū-den*, 体空伝) directly empties the dharma-substance. If this meaning exists, how can one say "outside the emptiness of the imagined there is no separate meaning of emptiness"? What is being established here seems to favor only the "attachment-emptiness transmission" (*shūkū-den*, 執空伝); how can it be that the "dharma-substance-emptiness meaning" (*taikū-gi*, 体空義) is not known?

**Answer.** That the Middle Way of the three natures in relation returns to the Middle Way beyond language — the detailed particulars of this are exceedingly deep and subtle. Let me set forth my own reflections. In general, when the two gates of the three natures (the gate of existence, *ūmon*, 有門) and the three naturelessnesses (the gate of emptiness, *kūmon*, 空門) are placed in relation, the gate of existence of the three natures constitutes the Middle Way, and the gate of emptiness of the three naturelessnesses constitutes the Middle Way. The Middle Way of the gate of existence is a dharma-gate that positively reveals (*hyōshō*, 表彰) all three natures. Hence it is collectively the gate of existence. Within that existence, because the imagined (*parikalpita*, 遍計所執) is empty it is not existence; because the dependent (*paratantra*, 依他起) and the perfectly accomplished (*pariniṣpanna*, 円成実) are existent it is not emptiness. The Middle Way of the gate of emptiness is a dharma-gate that blocks and stops all three naturelessnesses. [Note: the annotation suggests "性" (*shō*) may have dropped out after "無"] Hence it is collectively the gate of emptiness. Within that emptiness, because the characteristic-naturelessness (*sōmu-shō*, 相無性) is empty it is not existence; because the latter two (the production-naturelessness and the ultimate-naturelessness) are existent, it is not emptiness. This is the general framework and main path of the two gates. Examined in detail, each and every one has the meaning of "neither empty nor existent." To begin with the three-natures gate: what is called "the emptiness of the imagined" blocks and stops the deluded attachments of superimposition (*zōyaku*, 増益) and deprecation (*songen*, 損減). The gate of "blocking superimposition" is the gate of "not existence," and the gate of "blocking deprecation" is the

gate of "not emptiness." Since (the imagined) pervades both, even though all (within the imagined) are empty, since within the attachments being blocked there is already a division between both the empty and the existent forms of attachment, the emptiness that is thereby revealed is necessarily "neither empty nor existent." Likewise, the two natures of the dependent and perfectly accomplished that are upheld by means of this blocking of superimposition and deprecation are necessarily also "neither empty nor existent." Because (the gate of existence) reveals the dharma-substance, even though it is altogether (one of) existence, the dharma it reveals is subtle; hence it is necessarily "neither empty nor existent." This is still only the main path within the finer-grained discussion (above).

If one discusses this yet more finely: within the blocking of the attachment of superimposition, since one already blocks the superimposed existence, one does not block the non-superimposed existence. Therefore, even though it is "not existence," it still carries the meaning of "not empty." Likewise, within the blocking of deprecation, since one already blocks the deprecating emptiness, one does not block the non-deprecating emptiness. Therefore, even though it is "not empty," it still carries the meaning of "not existence." In this way, the dependent and perfectly accomplished that are revealed carry layer upon layer of subtle meanings that are equally necessary. The "not-empty" meaning that is carried, and the "not-existence" meaning that is carried, are each in turn emptiness-meanings that depart from superimposition and deprecation; and the existence-meanings that (result from) "blocking deprecation" are also each in turn freed from superimposition and deprecation. Reasoning and searching onward in this way, step by step, one ultimately arrives at wordlessness (*zetsugon*, 絶言), and conceptual deliberation suddenly ceases. This is the Middle Way beyond language (*rigen chūdō*) within the single gate of the three natures' existence.

Therefore, taking the conventional existence (*keu*, 仮有) and real non-existence (*jitsumu*, 実無) of the dependent as a case in point: conventional existence serves as the "meaning of existence," and real non-existence serves as the "meaning of emptiness" — this is one meaning of the main path. Discussed in detail, conventional existence also has the meaning of "neither empty nor existent," and real non-existence also has the meaning of "neither empty nor existent." Each of those individual "neither empty nor existent" (instances) again each has the meaning of "neither empty nor existent." The "neither empty nor existent" of the perfectly accomplished should likewise be applied by analogy. All of this can be understood through the principles already established above. [End of the section on the three-natures gate.] Within the gate of emptiness of the three naturelessnesses, each of the two gates of emptiness and existence again has the two gates of emptiness and existence; this is a layer upon layer of subtle and inexpressible principle. Now, within these two gates of the three natures and the three naturelessnesses: if one discusses the main path, the gate of the three natures blocks deprecation, while the gate of the three naturelessnesses blocks superimposition — because what their original meanings are directed toward is a necessary principle. However, when discussed in detail, the three natures also block both superimposition and deprecation, and the three naturelessnesses also block both superimposition and deprecation. These two gates now, although provisionally distinguished as separate aspects, are in reality one single gate. Why? Because from the gate of emptiness of the imagined (*parikalpita*) within the three natures, the three naturelessnesses are opened forth; and from the gate of existence of the latter two (naturelessnesses) within the three naturelessnesses, the three natures are opened forth. They mutually interpenetrate and are not separate dharma-gates. When one grasps the meaning in this way, all the gates of existence, however layered and inexhaustible, when bundled together all enter fully into the gate of existence of the dependent and perfectly accomplished. All the gates of emptiness, however layered and inexhaustible, when bundled together also all enter fully into the gate of emptiness of the imagined. Therefore (the teaching) says "self and dharmas are not existent, emptiness-consciousness is not non-existent" — this is the essential verbal expression (*sen*, 詮) of the Middle Way. Having thus expounded (the teaching), all meanings of the inexpressible are fully equipped; and one necessarily arrives at the abandoning of verbal expression (*hisen*, 廃詮). The verse of Maitreya (*Jishi*, 慈氏) is precisely this intention.

Next, regarding the meaning of "conventional existence and ultimate non-existence" (*zoku'u shinmu*, 俗有真無): (Bhāviveka and others) open (their teaching) exclusively at the conventional level (*zoku*, 俗) and at the ultimate level (*shōgi*, 勝義) do not at all admit the meaning of existence. Our school's two truths (*nitai*, 二諦) are both Middle Way. That is, (our school) admits both the gates of emptiness and existence within the ultimate (*shōgi*). This principle is as established above. If that is so, then comparing our school's ultimate to that school's ultimate: that school accords with one-sided emptiness, while this (school) maintains the Middle Way — this principle is surely blazingly evident. (To say that) the Hossō school's ultimate truth is limited to (mere) existence means one does not understand the deep purport of our school, as established above.

Next, regarding the meaning of "dharma-substance emptiness" (*taikū*, 体空): the three naturelessnesses take the three natures respectively as their substance (*tai*, 体) in sequence. The latter two naturelessnesses are precisely the two natures of dependent origination and perfect accomplishment. However, what is emptied in each case is entirely the imagined (*parikalpita*, 遍計所執). That is: the reason that the dependent origination is called "production-naturelessness" (*shōmusshō*, 生無性) is that dharmas arising through conditions have no nature of arising spontaneously (*jizenni shōzuru shō*, 自然生性). That "spontaneous arising" is precisely the

The reason that the principle of perfect accomplishment is called "ultimate-naturelessness" (*shōgi-musei*, 勝義無性) is that the true principle without characteristics (*mūsō no shinri*, 無相真理) is far removed from the imagined. Beyond this, a transmission that directly points to the dharma-substance itself as empty — I do not see it in the private records (*shiki*, 私記). Many people may be mistaken about this.

**Question.** The intent of Bhāviveka and others is to establish the "Middle Way of the eight negations" (*hachi-fu chūdō*, 八不中道). This is of course a discussion at the ultimate level. Then why, at the ultimate level, does it open a one-directional (teaching of) emptiness?

**Answer.** The bodhisattva Bhāviveka is a great master who has attained true sainthood (*ōjin daishi*, 応真大士) [as explained by Kuiji (*Jion*, 慈恩) in his commentary on the *Lǐqù fēn shū* (理趣分疏)]. Nāgārjuna (*Ryūmyō*, 龍猛) and Āryadeva (*Daiba*, 提婆) are monastics of high spiritual attainment. In the line of transmission from master to disciple, there can surely be no error. It is said that the Sanlun (Three Treatises) tradition that circulated at the time was not the tradition of Bhāviveka. Yet the Middle Way of the eight negations is indeed their established position. If that is the case, there must surely be a deep intent behind what Daoxuan (*Dōsen*, 道詮) criticizes as well. How could one, with the shallow understanding of a junior student, presume to measure right and wrong? I am deeply, deeply humbled. But although (we) exhaustively seek to study the legacy of (our) predecessors, even if worthy scholars belong to another tradition, if those who hear criticisms do not open (the texts) to examine them, who will protect the life of the Dharma (*hōmyō*, 法命)? Hence, with all due reverence, (I set forth) the extent of my reflections.

If that is so, the "Middle Way of the eight negations" is a dharma-gate that purely and exclusively blocks and stops. Although it is called "Middle Way," (as a means of) verbal expression (*sen*, 詮) it is only this much; (whereas) in our own school's abandonment of verbal expression (*haisen*, 廃詮) — (though it) stops conceptual deliberation (and yet verbally expresses something) — it is precisely "self and dharmas are not existent, emptiness-consciousness is not non-existent." Our (school) from the outset admits both the gates of emptiness and existence; that school (*Sanlun*) from the outset discusses only the single gate of emptiness. Where (both teachings) arrive — at wordlessness (*rigen*, 離言) — there is altogether no dispute; but as for the gate of entry (into the teaching), which accords more with the Middle Way is surely clear, is it not? However, this too is only a provisional discussion. What seems one-sided is not truly one-sided; what seems to accord with (the Middle Way), when one clings to it, becomes delusion through and through.
