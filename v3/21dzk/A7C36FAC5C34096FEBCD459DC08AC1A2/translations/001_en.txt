**Prefatory Words for the Engraving of the Preface Commentary on the *Cheng Weishi Lun***

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* (Discourse on the Establishment of Consciousness Only) represents the profound realm of the bodhisattvas, and is beyond the comprehension of the two vehicles and ordinary beings. The Tripiṭaka master of the Tang dynasty [Xuanzang] once gathered the commentaries of ten masters and compiled them into a synthesized treatise. The great Kuiji, master of Mahāyāna, personally received secret instructions [from Xuanzang] and composed a commentary (*shu*) on it, thereby pointing beyond the finger to the moon. Nevertheless, the writing style is archaic and the doctrinal content is profound and recondite, and students have still been unable to fully comprehend it upon reading. Therefore, the disciples of Puyang of Zizhou successively compiled the *Cheng Weishi Lun Yanmi* and *Cheng Weishi Lun Zhanghong Shuao* (the "Lamp" and "Secret"), probing the hidden subtleties of the treatise and commentary. Alas, even in those luminous works—the *Yanmi*, *Liaoyi Deng* (Lamp), and *Lingbian*—the preface to the commentary went unexplained; the ancients too lamented this. Here in our country, the僧正 (Archbishop) Zhunju of Akishino was marvelously conversant with the profound principles of the three natures, and clearly understood the formal structure of the eight gates. In all, his authored chapters and commentaries amount to more than ten works. The present *Preface Commentary* is one of them. It is regrettable that the old copies are incomplete in their characters, and the punctuation is difficult to comprehend. During the days when I was at the academic institution in the Southern Capital [Nara], I gathered various copies and corrected them according to their meaning. In places where distinguishing corrupted characters proved difficult, I retained the old text and did not insert my own interpretation. Having already added reading marks alongside [the text], I have had it engraved on woodblocks and disseminated widely. Later scholars may obtain a fine copy and correct it once more. This is a great boon for the Dharma.

Winter, tenth month of Genroku 9 (1696) — Shaku Tsūin Zuigen, inscribed at a lodging in the Zen grove of eastern Kyoto.

**Commentary on the Preface of the *Cheng Weishi Lun Shūki* (述記)**

　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　Compiled by Shaku Zhunju of Akishino

Humbly reflecting: the "six positions" (*liuwei*, the six lines of the *I Ching* hexagrams) are subtle and refined — relying on the *Xiang* and *Yi* [commentaries] to seek the underlying principle; the "two texts" (*erpian*, the two books of the *Daodejing*) are mysterious and wondrous — drawing upon the *Meng* [hexagram] and [works like] *Liezi* to probe their essential points.

The present preface is divided into seven sections:

**Section One**: From "竊以六位" ("Humbly reflecting, the six positions...") to "湛幽章而海澹" — this clarifies that the principle realized by the Tathāgata is profound and difficult to fathom.

**Section Two**: From "匪屬六体" to "帰真上遷" — this clarifies that the Bodhisattva Vasubandhu composed a treatise (the *Triṃśikā*, thirty verses) to expound the mind-only (*vijñaptimātratā*) teaching of the Buddha's scriptures.

**Section Three**: From "義繁文約" to "競響而已" — this clarifies that the ten bodhisattvas led by Dharmapāla composed treatises to explain the meaning of the root verses.

**Section Four**: From "在昔周皇" to "其詳可略" — this clarifies that the translations made by earlier-generation Tripiṭaka masters were incorrect.

**Section Five**: From "惟我親教" to "云爾而已" — this explains that the translation by the Tripiṭaka master Xuanzang is correct.

**Section Six**: From "斯本彙聚" to "依士釈也" — this provides a general clarification of the title *Cheng Weishi Lun*.

**Section Seven**: From "基学慙融愷" to "詳幽致爾" — this narrates how Master Kuiji humbly described his own composition of the work.

Within Section One there are two parts: first, [the text] cites the subtlety and unfathomability of the outer writings — the two teachings of Confucianism and Daoism — and then, from "how much more so" (*kuanghu*) onward, it clarifies that the inner scriptures (Buddhism) are profound and difficult to comprehend.

Within Section One there are again two parts: first, it cites the six lines (*liuyao*) of the Confucian text (*I Ching*) as the "six positions," and then it cites the five-thousand-character text of the Daoist scripture (*Daodejing*) as the "two texts."
