*Yí* 儀 is pronounced *yǔqí* (語奇切); the *Ěryǎ* states: "*yí* is *pǐ* 匹 [a match; a peer]." *Pǐ* means "to compare." *Róng* 融 means "to illuminate; to make clear." Thus the phrase "with" (*yǐ* 以) [read in Japanese as *mako-u-to-ni* 麻公止爾], and *yùn* 孕 means "to carry within; to embrace."

**"How could it merely rival the river Huang in its flow, or compete with thunder and lightning in its resonance?"**

*Huáng* 潢 is pronounced *hòuhuāng* (後荒切); the *Shuōwén* states: "a pond of collected water." There is also an alternative pronunciation *húguǎng* (胡廣切). *Léi* 雷 is pronounced *lìhuí* (力回切): a trembling/shaking is called *léi* (thunder). *Tíng* 霆 is pronounced *dádīng* (達丁切): a fast-striking thunderbolt is called *tíng*.

**"In former times, the stars of Zhou concealed their light; the ultimate Way was pent up and had not yet spread. When the sun of the Han illumined its radiance, the teaching of images [i.e., Buddhism] was proclaimed and reached afar."**

What follows is the fourth section, clarifying how the translators of the Three Baskets (*Tripiṭaka*) in previous generations were deficient. 

Regarding "the stars of Zhou concealed their light" (*Zhōu xīng bì sè* 周星閟色): The *Zuoshi Zhuan* [*Commentary of Master Zuo*] mentions "the two Lords Zhuang of Zhou and Lu." The tenth year of King Zhuang of Zhou corresponds to the seventh year of Duke Zhuang of Lu — namely the fifth day of the fourth month of summer, the dawn of *Xīnmǎo* (辛卯). It was at this time that the Buddha was born in the Western Land (India). Thus King Zhuang of Zhou and Duke Zhuang of Lu were contemporaries. According to this text, on the dawn of the day of the Buddha's birth, stars fell in the night like rain, and they flashed together with the rain (*霅*, meaning "falling") — that is all. *Xié* 偕 means "together."

Furthermore, the *Zhōushū Yìjì* [*Miscellaneous Records of the Zhou Documents*] states: "In the twenty-fourth year of King Zhao of Zhou's reign, in the year *Jiǎyín*, on the eighth day of the fourth month — rivers, springs, and pools suddenly overflowed, and all the well-water overflowed entirely. Palace halls, dwellings, mountains, rivers, heaven and earth all shook. That night, a luminescence of five colors entered and pierced through the Imperial Precinct (*Dàwēi* 大微) and extended throughout the western regions, forming patterns in blue-red (*qīnghóng*) hues. King Zhao asked the Grand Historian (*Tàishǐ*) Su You: 'What omen is this?' Su You replied: 'A great sage has been born in the Western Land; hence we see these auspicious signs. As for what this means for the realm under Your Majesty's rule —' He answered: 'At present

**[Right Page, Lower Section]**

there will be no transformation [through this teaching]. But after a thousand years, its voiced teachings will reach this land.' That was the time of the Buddha's first birth."

*Bì* 閟 is pronounced *bǐyì* (鄙異切) and means "to close; to shut." *Yù* 鬱 means "obstructed; dense; flourishing."

Regarding "the sun of the Han illumined its radiance" (*Hàn rì tōng huī* 漢日通暉): this refers to how the Buddhist Dharma arrived at Luoyang during the reign of Emperor Ming of the Han. The *Hànfǎ Nèizhuàn* [*Inner Transmission of the Han Dharma*] states: "In the tenth year of Emperor Ming's Yongping era, [the emperor] dreamed at night of a golden figure sixteen feet tall, whose radiance was wondrous beyond compare. Upon awakening, Emperor Ming was uneasy and could not settle himself. The following morning he assembled his ministers in great numbers to interpret the dream. The *tōngshì shěrén* [palace attendant-secretary] Fu Yi respectfully replied: 'Your servant has heard that in the western regions there is a divinity called Buddha. What Your Majesty has dreamed must surely be this.' Thereupon eighteen envoys were dispatched to seek out the Dharma of the Buddha. They arrived in the land of Tianzhu (India) and encountered two monks, Kāśyapamātaṅga (*Jiāshé Mátèng* 迦葉摩騰) and Dharmarakṣa (*Zhú Fǎlán* 竺法蘭), and [these monks] came to Luoyang. Emperor Ming was greatly delighted and honored them deeply. Immediately a monastery was established to the west of Luoyang city — this is the present-day White Horse Temple (*Báimǎ Sì*). The name originated from the white horse that carried the scriptures [here]. Once Kāśyapamātaṅga arrived, he translated numerous scriptures."

Regarding "the sun of the Han" (*Hàn rì* 漢日): the golden figure that Emperor Ming saw in his dream had, above its crown, a mark resembling the sun — this refers to the radiant marks and characteristics (*lakṣaṇa*) at the crown of the Buddha's head. Pointing to those marks, [the preface] calls it "the sun of the Han." *Bèi* 被 means "to extend to; to reach."

**"They translated sūtras, vinaya, and *abhidharma* (*yì* 義 / treatises), continuing the footsteps and following in the traces. They mostly encountered eminent persons to the right [i.e., west] of the Onion Range (*Cōnglǐng*), but rarely heard of the outstanding [masters] of India itself."**

Regarding "translating scriptures, vinaya, and *yì*" (*yì jīng lǜ yì* 譯經律義): there are two interpretations. One interpretation holds that *lǜ* 律 means "to expound" (*shù* 述) and the phrase means "explaining the meaning of the sūtras." Another interpretation holds that *jīng* 経 refers to the *Sūtrapiṭaka* (*sūtra-piṭaka*), *lǜ* 律 refers to the *Vinayapiṭaka* (*vinaya-piṭaka*), and *yì* 義 (i.e., treatise / *lùn* 論) refers to the *Abhidharmapiṭaka* (*abhidharma-piṭaka*) — that is, all three baskets (*Tripiṭaka*) were translated.

Regarding *jì wǔ* 繼武 ("continuing the footsteps"): *wǔ* 武 means "footsteps; traces." The *Shū* [*Book of Documents*] states: "King Wen treads in Wu's footsteps." "Wu" here refers to the tracks of the preceding Son of Heaven. *Lián* 聯 is pronounced *lìrán* (力然切) and means "linked; connected." *Zōng* 蹤 [Japanese: *ayumi* 阿由美, "footsteps"]. *Dí* 覿 is pronounced either *túdá* (徒答) or *dájì* (達寂) and means "to meet; to see."

**[Left Page, Upper Section]**

*Cōng* 葱 refers to the Cong Range (*Cōnglǐng*, the Pamir Mountains). *Yòu* 右 ("right [i.e., west]") means the land west of those mountains, generally called the "Hu lands" (*Húdì* 胡地). That is, the territory between the Cong Range to the west and the Five Indias to the east is called the "Hu lands." The meaning is that [the translators] encountered outstanding persons of the Hu lands, but rarely heard from those who excelled in India itself.

**"The phonological systems are separated as by the earth itself — confusing the sounds of *gōng* and *yǔ* [i.e., language systems] between India (*huá*) and the Hu lands (*róng*). The scripts are as different as heaven from earth — obscuring the principles of *xíngshēng* [phono-semantic characters] between the Hu script and Chinese (*Jìn*)."**

Regarding *rǎng gé* 壤隔 ("separated by the earth"): *rǎng* 壤 is pronounced *rúzhǎng* (如掌切) and refers to large clods of earth. [The phrase] says that the western regions (*xīfāng*) and this land [China] are separated in their phonological systems, as if divided by the very earth itself. *Hùn* 混 means "to mix together; to combine" [Japanese training gloss: *hitatake* 比多多氣].

Regarding *gōng yǔ* 宮宇 [literally "palace and abode"]: this refers to the first of the five tones (*wǔ shēng*), *gōng* 宮, and the fifth, *yǔ* 羽. The *Hànshū* (*Lǜlì Zhì*) [*Treatise on Harmonics and Calendar in the Han History*] states: "*Gōng* is 'center' (*zhōng* 中). It resides in the *yang* of the central position; it is the beginning of the impulse (*chàng*) in the four directions and the origin of life; it is the governing string (*gāng* 綱) of the four tones." "*Yǔ* is 'house' (*yǔ* 宇). Things are gathered and stored; the roof (*yǔ*) covers them."

Their correspondences with the Five Phases are: *Jiǎo* 角 corresponds to wood, east, and spring; *shāng* 商 corresponds to metal, west, and autumn; *zhǐ* 徵 corresponds to fire, south, and summer; *yǔ* 羽 corresponds to water, north, and winter; and *gōng* 宮 corresponds to earth and the central region. In terms of ruler, minister, people, affairs, and things: *gōng* corresponds to the ruler, *shāng* to the minister, *jiǎo* to the people, *zhǐ* to affairs, and *yǔ* to things.

Here the text picks out only two tones [*gōng* and *yǔ*]. Why not take the other tones? Because [the text] selects the first and the last among the tones. Furthermore, "宮宇" also carries the sense of a house or repository. The reason the *yǔ* 羽 tone is glossed as *yǔ* 宇 ("house/roof") is that the principle of *yǔ* belongs to winter, and in the winter season things are stored away — just as a roof (*yǔ*) covers and conceals things.

As for why the five tones differ in pitch and gradation: thread drawn from ten cocoons is called "one *hū* 忽" [Japanese: *hitoyori no ito* 比止餘利乃糸, "a single-ply thread"]. Eighty-one *hū* constitute the *gōng* tone, which is placed in the central position. Dividing the *gōng* tone into three parts and adding one more part produces the *shāng* tone — that is, seventy-two *hū*.

**[Left Page, Lower Section]**

Dividing the seventy-two *hū* into three parts, removing one part, and taking the remaining two parts produces the *zhǐ* tone — forty-eight *hū*. Dividing these into three parts and then adding one part produces the *yǔ* tone — sixty-four *hū*. [The principle is that] increasing or decreasing the thread makes the sound large, medium, or small — and thus the pitch and modulation of the tones becomes manifest.

Regarding *Huá-Róng* 華戎: *Huá* 華 means "the capital [the cultured center]" — here it indicates India. *Róng* 戎 is pronounced *rúzhōng* (如終切) and means "barbarian" (*yí* 夷) — here it indicates the Hu lands. The Nine *Yí* (eastern barbarians), Eight *Dí* (northern barbarians), Seven *Róng* (western barbarians), and Six *Mán* (southern barbarians) are collectively called "the Four Seas" [i.e., the peoples at the four extremities]. [Note: "The Nine *Yí* are in the east, the Eight *Dí* in the north, the Seven *Róng* in the west, the Six *Mán* in the south — these indicate the four wildernesses (*sì huāng* 四荒)."]

*Tiān xuán* 天懸 means "separated as vastly as [the distance to] heaven."

Regarding "speaking of *xíngshēng*" 形声: the preface to the *Shuōwén [Jiězì]* states: "First is *zhǐshì* 指事 [ideographic indication]; second is *xiàngxíng* 象形 [pictographic representation]; third is *xíngshēng* 形声 [phono-semantic composition]. *Xíngshēng*: using an event/object as the name and taking a phonetic analogy to complete [the character]. *Jiāng* 江 (river) and *hé* 河 (river) are examples."

By way of explanation: "*Zhǐshì*" refers to characters such as 上 (above) and 下 (below). Because a thing is in the upper position, the dot of the character is placed above; because a thing is in the lower position, the dot is placed below — and so forth. "*Xiàngxíng*" refers to characters such as 日 (sun) and 月 (moon). Because the moon is crescent-shaped (lacking), its character-form is made long; because the sun is round, its character-form is made short. "*Xíngshēng*" refers to characters such as 江 and 河. In the character 江, the semantic element is placed on the left and the phonetic element on the right: the water radical (氵) on the left is the semantic element (*xíng* 形), and the component 工 on the right is the phonetic element (*shēng* 声). Characters such as 河 may be understood by analogy.

Regarding "Hu and Jìn" (*Hú Jìn* 胡晉): *Jìn* 晉 here refers to the Great Tang [China], and *Hú* 胡 refers to the Western Land (India).

The great principles were in some cases distorted, and subtle expressions gave rise to errors. Grand doubts and major obstructions loomed like mist and congealed like clouds. The hidden intricacies frequently manifested [in all their confusion]. One may well omit the details [of these problems]."**
