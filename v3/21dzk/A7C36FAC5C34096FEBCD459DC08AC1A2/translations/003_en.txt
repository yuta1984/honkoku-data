**[Right Page, Upper Section]**

If there were not one who possesses [the ability to] embody and approach [the wisdom of] insight — to unfold [what is] profound and hook out [what is] deep — then the ultimate meaning (*liaoyi* 了義) of the nature of emptiness would have almost ceased [to exist].

The following [section] is the second [part], which clarifies that the Bodhisattva Vasubandhu composed the *Thirty Verses* to expound the teaching of the principle of "mind-only" (*vijñaptimātratā*) in the Buddhist scriptures.

"*Fei*" 匪 means "not" (*fei* 非). "*Shu*" 属 means "to belong to" or "to be attributed to"; here it refers to Bodhisattva Vasubandhu. The phrase "possessing the embodiment and approaching wisdom" (*juti linzhi* 具体隣智) universally praises the fact that Bodhisattva Vasubandhu is fully capable of embodying and understanding the principle of Vijñaptimātratā and of expounding the teaching of mind-only as described in the sūtras. Hence the phrase "possessing the embodiment" (*juti* 具体). [Vasubandhu's] stage of practice was at the end of the Ten Dedications (*daśa-praṇidhāna*, 十廻向), and his mind was close to (*linji* 隣近) the undefiled wisdom (*anāsrava-jñāna*) of the Path of Seeing (*darśana-mārga*). Hence the phrase "approaching wisdom" (*linzhi* 隣智).

The phrase "unfolding the profound and hooking out the deep" (*yanze goushen* 演賾鉤深) comes from the *Zhouyi* (Book of Changes). The *Yi* says: "Probe the profound (*ze* 賾: *zhimai* cut, meaning 'deeply hidden'), seek out (*suo* 索: 'to seek') the hidden, hook out what is deep, and lead to what is far." When something is in a heavily barred and hidden place, one finds the key and brings it out. When a fish is in the depths of a spring, one uses a hook to draw it out. The Bodhisattva's composing of the treatise to expound the sūtra is similarly [an act of retrieval].

"*Jihu xi yi*" 幾乎息矣: "*ji*" means "close to" or "nearly." This means: if the Bodhisattva had not composed a treatise to expound the profound meaning of the Buddhist scriptures, the meaning of those scriptures would have been close to dying out and disappearing. Hence the phrase "if there were not one who possesses embodiment and approaches wisdom, etc."

**"The *Vijñaptimātratā-triṃśikā* (Thirty Verses on Consciousness-Only): one branch among the ten branches, composed by the Bodhisattva Vasubandhu."**

The above praised the person who composed the treatise. The following now identifies the treatise that was composed.

It is said that Bodhisattva Vasubandhu, having been guided by [his elder brother] Asaṅga, deeply regretted his past errors, refined his thoughts with careful concentration, and composed over a hundred parts of Mahāyāna treatises. Later he moved to the country of Kauśāmbī, where he broadly illuminated the profound import [of the Dharma], gave full expression to [its] supreme depths, culled its subtle workings, and grasped its essential profundity. He thereupon composed the *Vijñaptimātratā-triṃśikā* (Thirty Verses on Consciousness-Only), refuting the other vehicles, crushing the heterodox teachings (*tīrthika*), and declaring the wondrous purport of the Mahāyāna. Though the summary verses (*kārikā*) were completed, the detailed commentary had not yet been finished when he ascended to the Tuṣita Heaven.

**[Right Page, Lower Section]**

He was born among the inner assembly of Maitreya, born within a lotus flower. When the lotus flower had just barely opened, the Venerable Maitreya [Citrabhānu] praised [him], saying: "Welcome, O Widely Wise One!" After six days [in which he heard the Dharma], he descended to report [what he had learned] to Asaṅga. At that very time, there also appeared two great masters of the treatises — Dharmapāla (*Huobian* 火辨) and Bandhuśrī (*Qinsheng* 親勝) — who composed direct commentaries on the root verses, probing the intent of the treatise. This is as recorded in the *Xiyu ji* (*Record of the Western Regions*) (and so forth).

Regarding the "ten branches" (*shizhi* 十支): "*zhi*" means "branch" or "division." These are the branch-divisions of the *Yogācārabhūmi* [Treatise]. The *Cheng Weishi Lun Shuji* (*Treatise on the Lamp of Consciousness-Only*) states: "[These are] the *Baifa* (Hundred Dharmas), *Wuyun* (Five Aggregates), *Xianyangsheng jiao lun* (Treatise on Revealing the Teachings), *She Dasheng lun* (Mahāyānasaṃgraha), *Za ji* (Compendium), *Zhongbian* (Middle and Extremes), *Ershi weishi* (Twenty Verses on Consciousness-Only), *Zhuangyan* (Ornament), and *Fenbie Yujia* (Discriminating Yoga)." Alternatively, by removing the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* and adding the *Nyāyapraveśa* (*Zhengli men lun* 正理門論), one arrives at ten.

**"The white rainbow flew its inauspicious vapors; the white [hair between the brows] dissolved its radiance. The subtle-threaded floral profound import was transposed from 'phoenix' to 'wind' [by scribal error]. The sacred leaves of the spirit-texts went astray between 'fish' and 'sun' [by scribal error]."** (The character "日/*ri*" in one version is written as "魯/*lǔ*".)

Regarding "the white rainbow flew its inauspicious vapors" (*baihong fei jin* 白虹飛祲): "*Jin*" 祲 (pronunciation: *zizhèn* cut). At the time of the Buddha's *parinirvāṇa*, twelve paths of white rainbows appeared in the west, stretching up to Heaven. "*Jin*" refers to yin and yang qi penetrating each other, gradually forming a disaster. It also means "inauspicious vapor." Within the [classic of] Rites there is the chapter on "*guanjin*" 観祲 (observing inauspicious signs), in which the Grand Historian (*taishi*) ascended a high terrace to observe the heavens above; when there appeared strange and inauspicious *jin*-vapors in the sky, one knew that some ill-fated event would occur.

The *Zhoushu Yiji* (*Anomalous Records of the Book of Zhou*) states: "In the fifty-third year of King Mu of Zhou, on the fifteenth day of the second month [of the year] *renchen* (壬申), just before dawn, a sudden violent wind arose, destroying people's homes and breaking trees. Mountains, rivers, heaven, and earth all shook together. In the afternoon, the sky grew dark, and in the west, twelve paths of white rainbows appeared, passing from north to south, and through the night there was no brightness. King Mu asked his Grand Historian Pao Duo (庖多): 'What is this omen?' Pao Duo replied: 'There is a sage in the west. This is a sign (*biǎo xiāng*) of his *parinirvāṇa* (*miè dù*). He has entered *nirvāṇa* at this very time. Counting up to the time of Emperor Ming of Han, it would amount to one thousand and twenty years.'"

Regarding "the white [hair-mark] dissolved its radiance" (*suhao xiao jing* 素毫銷景):

**[Left Page, Upper Section]**

This means that the white *ūrṇā*-hair (the hair tuft between the Buddha's brows) had faded and disappeared, and there was no longer any period during which it emitted light. "*Su*" 素 means "white." "*Xiao*" 銷 means "to disappear/be hidden." "*Jing*" 景 means "light."

Regarding "the subtle-threaded floral profound import" (*xianhua aozhi* 綫華奧旨): [This is] like a thread stringing together flowers, and so on. This is one of the five meanings of the character "*jing*" 経 (sūtra/thread).

Regarding "[the phrase] from 'phoenix was transposed' to 'sun was made erroneous'" (*chuan feng to miu ri* 舛鳳…謬日): This refers to the fact that after the Buddha's *parinirvāṇa*, those who transmitted the sūtras and teachings made many mistakes and distortions. For example, it is like a person who, when trying to write the character for "wind" (*feng* 風), mistakenly writes the character for "phoenix" (*feng* 鳳). Because they resemble each other, [the text] says "the phoenix (*fèng*) was transposed (*chuan*), the wind (*fēng*) was corrupted" (the Japanese reading being *ayamatsu* 阿夜末都, "to err").

Regarding "the sacred leaves of the spirit-texts — fish and sun in error" (*beiyi lingpian, guai yu miu ri* 貝葉靈篇乖魚謬日): As for the phrase "sacred leaves" (*beiyi* 貝葉), some people say that the boards of the Sanskrit [palm-leaf] manuscripts resemble shellfish (*bei* 貝) and their leaves resemble tree bark — but this is incorrect. Examining the phonetic glossary (*yinyi*) in the upper fascicle of the *Huayan jing* commentary by Master Huiyuan (*Xinhuayan jing*, *Huiyuan shi yinyi*), it says: "*Bei die* (貝牒): '*bei*' [pronounced] *béigài*-cut; '*die*' [pronounced] *tújiá*-cut. '*Bei*' refers to the leaves of the *pattra* tree (*beiduo* 貝多, i.e., *tāḍī*-palm), meaning [the text is written on] the leaves of the Sanskrit original. '*Die*' (tally) refers to a tablet; '*die*' is a general term for written scriptures" (end of citation).

Upon examination: when one says "*bei*," [the word] "*beiduo*" 貝多 is already a Sanskrit word. "*Beidāra*" 貝多羅 is a tree name, which is already Sanskrit — so why [does anyone] say it "resembles a shellfish" (*bei* 貝)? Moreover, [the error] is like the case of the character "*lǔ*" 魯, which is originally a single character, yet suddenly someone who does not understand it splits it into two characters — namely "*yú*" 魚 ("fish") and "*rì*" 日 ("sun"). A popular saying has it: "When a text is copied three times, '*dì*' (帝, 'emperor') becomes '*hǔ*' (虎, 'tiger'), and '*yú*' (魚, 'fish') becomes '*lǔ*' (魯)." Buddhist scriptures are likewise: the farther [transmission] moves from the Sage [the Buddha], the more those who transmit make errors and distortions, and it becomes difficult to distinguish the pure from the impure. "*Xiě*" 寫 (to copy/write): *sūyú*-cut [= pronunciation *xiě*].

**"Looking back upon the treasure of the Dharma, this text was about to be lost. Then there arose one of integrated understanding (*róng shí* 融識), whose traces shone forth in transmitting the lamp [of the Dharma]."**

This passage praises both Vasubandhu and [his] parents. The glosses are as follows: "*Wéi*" 惟 (Japanese: *omoimiba* 於毛比美波 — "considering/thinking"); "about to be lost" (*xíng zhuì* 行墜) (Japanese: *ochinamu to suru ni* 於知奈牟土須流爾); "*dàn*" 誕 "arose" (Japanese: *umite* 有美弖); "*xiù*" 秀 "shone forth" (Japanese: *hiidétari* 比伊天多利); "*jì*" 迹 "traces" (Japanese: *ato o* 阿土乎). The character "*xíng*" 行 carries the primary reading of "about to" (*jiāng* 將, "on the verge of"). In an old document (*jō* 帖) of the Great King, the phrase "*xíng lǎo*" 行老 appears, meaning "about to grow old" — simply "on the verge of aging." The intent here is the same. [The meaning is:] "Looking back upon this text of the Buddhist sūtras, it was about to fall into ruin." "Then there arose (*dàn cí* 誕茲)..."

**[Left Page, Lower Section]**

"...one of integrated understanding" (*dàn cí róng* 融誕/誕茲融): This clarifies that Bodhisattva Vasubandhu appeared and arose in the world during the [period] nine hundred years after [the Buddha's *parinirvāṇa*], composing treatises to expound the sūtras. "Integrated understanding" (*róngshí* 融識) means that the Bodhisattva was himself able to integrate and merge his own mental consciousness (*citta-vijñāna*) and transmit the lamp [of the Dharma] to the world. Moreover, among the twenty-four persons who transmitted the Dharma, Vasubandhu was the twentieth.

**"In the *Abhidharmakośa* (*Jusha*), he concealed his solitary illumination and, manifesting conformity with the worldly dust, he expounded [the doctrine of] existence (*u*). In the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* (*Shōron*), he untied the bonds of understanding (*e*-baku) and, expressing liberation from [even] the saintly, he discoursed on emptiness (*kū*)."**

This Bodhisattva was a native of the country of Gandhāra (*Jiandu luoguo* 健駄羅國). He originally left home [as a monk] within the *Sarvāstivāda* (*Youbu* 有部) school, and accordingly he received and upheld the *Tripiṭaka* of that school. Since it was only through the *Tripiṭaka* of [the *Sarvāstivāda*] that one could understand the principle of true emptiness, he relied on skillful means (*upāya*) and, dwelling in the same [assembly], he expounded [the doctrine of] existence.

Hence the "Wall Record of Ximing" (*Ximing biqi*) states: "This Bodhisattva first studied the Hīnayāna, becoming thoroughly immersed in names and marks (*nāma-lakṣaṇa*), clinging to the view that dharmas possess an existent nature (*dharma-svabhāva*), and composing five hundred treatises on the Hīnayāna. The Bodhisattva had an elder brother named Asaṅga (*Wuzhu* 無著), who deeply pitied his younger brother and, wishing to turn him away from the lesser path, feigned illness in order to awaken his deluded mind. Upon hearing of his elder brother's grave illness, [Vasubandhu] immediately came to inquire about his condition and asked what had caused it. [Asaṅga] replied: 'Because of your petty ambition in exclusively studying the Hīnayāna, you have no share in entering the great path — that is why I grieve.' Vasubandhu said: 'I have concealed the Mahāyāna and glorified the Hīnayāna; I have sinned deeply through my tongue. The tongue is the root of this sin. Now I should remove and sever it.' He then took up a sharp blade and was about to cut off his own tongue. Asaṅga said again: 'With this same tongue you glorified the small and hid the great. With this same tongue, make known the great and illuminate the small. Then my illness will be cured; there is no need for this effort.' And so [Vasubandhu] did not cut off his tongue, but instead followed [his brother's direction] and composed a further five hundred treatises on the Mahāyāna. Through this, the Mahāyāna spread ever more widely, and the people of that time gave him the title 'Master of a Thousand Treatises' (*qianbu lunshi* 千部論師). Furthermore, through [the cause of] compassionate consideration (*lǜ mǐn* 慮愍), [Asaṅga] transmitted the *Daśabhūmika Sūtra* (*Shidi jing* 十地経) and the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* (*Shè lùn* 攝論), through which [Vasubandhu] finally attained understanding and enlightenment. In accordance with reality [(*tathātā*)...]"
