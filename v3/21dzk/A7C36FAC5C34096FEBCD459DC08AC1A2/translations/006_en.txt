**[Right Page, Upper Section]**

(Continued from the previous page) "...resided in a high tower, reading aloud from a book in a loud voice. At that time, beside the tower there was a wheelwright who, having finished his work, looked toward the high tower, heard the king reading, and immediately asked the king: 'For what purpose do you do this?' The king answered: 'I am reading the writings of the former sages.' The wheelwright said: 'What Your Majesty is reading now contains only the dregs and husks (*zāopò*). The truly profound matters lie only within the hearts of the former sages. Reading brings no benefit. Why engage in this empty pursuit?' The king was immediately furious and was about to punish him for his crime. The wheelwright said: 'I have been making wheels until I am eighty years old. I have taught my son the craft, but my son has obtained only the dregs; he could not obtain the deeper technique. That deeper technique resides only within my own heart. What Your Majesty reads now is no different from this — you obtain only the dregs and cannot obtain the truth.' The king bore with these words and a single insight arose within him. He thereupon came to respect that man" — and so forth. This episode is borrowed to speak of "dregs and husks," and the original text should be consulted for confirmation. The meaning is that the scriptures and treatises translated by previous generations yielded only the outer skin and could not yield the true flavor. *Shuǎng* 爽 means "error; discrepancy." Of *shuòzhì* 碩滞, *shuò* 碩 means "great; large." "Kōng yí" 鴻疑 means that [the doubts] cannot be made to dissolve and disperse like melting ice. Regarding the phrase "yōu xuàn lǚ zhāng" 幽絢屢彰 — *xuàn* 絢 is pronounced *xǔxuán* (許懸切) and refers to elegant literary appearance; gathering the five colors of brilliance constitutes *xuàn*. Stringing together words in translation is likewise. The various literary meanings and expressions in the scriptures and treatises translated by the Three-Basket masters of the past are like the beautiful *xuàn* patterns of literary coloring. Yet their detailed and thorough content may for now be omitted. Furthermore, "yōu xuàn" 幽絢 also refers to the name of a record such as the *Gaoseng zhuan* (Biographies of Eminent Monks) and similar works. The various literary meanings and textual colorings of the scriptures and treatises translated by the successive Three-Basket masters are frequently displayed in the *Yōu Xuàn*. Hence it says "the details may be omitted."

**"I reflect that our preceptor, Tripiṭaka Dharma Master Xuanzang, contained within himself the brilliance [of virtue] and rose above the multitude. He illuminated the *fú* tree's radiance upon the Sacred Terrace (*líng tái* 靈臺, the mind). He accumulated virtue and dwelt in the fundamental [teaching]. He caused ripples of discourse to surge forth in the pond of wisdom."**

**[Right Page, Lower Section]**

What follows is the fifth section, which demonstrates that the translations of Tripiṭaka Master Xuanzang are correct and authentic. Regarding "含章拔萃" (*hán zhāng bá cuì*, "containing brilliance and rising above the multitude"): the *Zhōuyì* (*Book of Changes*) says, "Containing the brightness (*zhāng* 章), declaring the brightness." This is said to refer to the illumination manifested above the writings and paintings of noble and humble alike. (*Annotation: The text notes that what follows "謂" is difficult to read through and may be an erroneous character.*) *Cuì* 萃 is pronounced *zuì* (醉) and means "to gather; to assemble." *Zhāng* 章 means "beauty; brightness; brilliance."

Regarding "燭槫景於靈臺" ("illuminating the radiance of the *fú* tree on the Sacred Terrace"): the *Wénzì Jílüè* [*Compendium of Chinese Characters*] states: "*Fú* 槫 [the mythological mulberry tree of the east] is pronounced *fú* (扶). The great mulberry tree beyond the sea. [Pronounced] *fángyú* (防虞切)." The *Shānhǎi Jīng* [*Classic of Mountains and Seas*] states: "There is a valley called Warm Source Valley (*Wēnyuángǔ* 溫源谷) [the annotation says: this is the Hot Valley (*Tánggǔ* 湯谷)]. Above the Hot Valley there is a *fú* tree. When one sun arrives, another sun sets out [the annotation says: they meet and relieve one another]. All [the suns] are borne upon crows [the annotation says: the sun's disc bears a three-legged crow]."

Regarding "靈臺" (*líng tái*, "the Sacred Terrace"): *Zhuāngzǐ* says, "The ten thousand evils must not be admitted into the Sacred Terrace (*líng tái*)," and the commentary says, "The heart/mind is the terrace of the divine spirit (*shén líng*)."

Regarding "涌談漪於智沼" ("causing ripples of discourse to surge in the pond of wisdom"): the *Lúnyǔ* (*Analects*) says, "The wise person takes delight in water." *Yī* 漪 is pronounced *yūyí* (於宜切) and means "overlapping waves; ripples."

**"He galloped the precious tracks of the Three Turnings [of the Dharma Wheel], advancing through the golden sands [of the sacred river]. He clarified the true waves of the Eight Liberations, cleansing them in the jade well from afar."**

*Jìn* 晉 means "to advance; to proceed." Regarding "金砂" (*jīn shā*, "golden sands"): Li Shan states that the Bhadra River (*Baṭṭi Hé* 跋提河) is also known as the "Golden River" (*Jīn Hé* 金河). *Wù* 騖 is pronounced *wángjù* (亡具切) and means "a beast running in full gallop"; also "to race about in disorder." "Three Turnings" (*sān lún* 三輪) refers to the Three Turnings of the Dharma Wheel (*sān zhuǎn fǎlún*). It is also said to refer to the three disciplines: precepts (*jiè*), concentration (*dìng*), and wisdom (*huì*); or also the Three Karma Wheels. Regarding "淸玉井" ("purifying/clarifying the jade well"): in the land of Shu there is a Jade Well (*yùjǐng*). A holy man of the western regions shot an arrow into the ground, and thereby a well was formed. The water is said to be white as jade, hence it is called the Jade Well. It is also said that when the Buddha was still a prince, he and Devadatta (*Tíbódátā* 提婆達多) shot their bows toward the sky, and the earth became a Jade Well. The Tripiṭaka Master [Xuanzang] came to this place in his quest for the Buddhist Dharma — and so forth. *Liáo* 遼 is pronounced *lìtiáo* (力條切) and means "distant; far."

**"He forgot his body and gave himself over to the Dharma; he entrusted himself to destiny and prayed for [the way to] open."**

**[Left Page, Upper Section]**

*Xùn* 殉 is pronounced *cíjùn* (辭峻切) and means "to seek; to follow." When one's body follows a thing, it is called *xùn*. This passage says that the Tripiṭaka Master [Xuanzang], while dwelling in this land [China], made a solemn vow and traveled to the western regions, seeking the Dharma and praying for its penetrating realization.

**"He secretly accorded with the Heavenly True [Nature] and but slightly availed himself of acquired learning and practice."**

Regarding "天真" (*tiān zhēn*, "Heavenly True [Nature]"): this is another name for the natural [inherent nature]. The Tripiṭaka Master had an innate understanding (*sùjiě* 宿解) deriving from his Heavenly True Nature. Now, traveling to the western regions, he sought the Dharma and attained it. He relied only slightly on the aid of study and practice, and through this his mind became still more illuminated. *Wēi* 微 means "hidden; subtle; slight."

---

**"It was not through spreading [supernatural] efficacy and displaying the extraordinary; rather, it was through the solid accumulation of merit and the prolonged extension of auspiciousness."**

*Chī* 摛 is pronounced *chǐlí* (耻離切) and means "to hope for; to spread forth; to unfold." Regarding "匪摛靈而顯異" ("not spreading spiritual power to display the extraordinary"): what the Tripiṭaka Master understood and studied was not the displaying of extraordinary [powers]; rather, all the various matters resided simply in the Dharma itself. *Yùn* 蘊 means "to accumulate." *Yán* 延 means "to set forth; to extend." "延祥" (*yán xiáng*) means "to set forth and spread auspicious [signs]."

**"He fully trod the divine traces [of the Buddha], plumbing and probing the Hidden Treasury [of Teachings]. What the former worthies had not yet beheld, he penetrated entirely to the hub of [his] consciousness. What the sages of old had left behind, he encompassed comprehensively to the uttermost depth of [his] mind."**

"神蹤" (*shén zōng*, "divine traces"): these are the places where the Buddha wandered in the course of transforming sentient beings. The Tripiṭaka Master likewise traveled to and trod these very places, as described at length in the *Xīyù Jì* (*Records of the Western Regions*). "祕府" (*mì fǔ*, "Hidden Treasury") refers to the sacred teachings (*shèngjiào*). "探祕" [Japanese gloss: *kanaberu*, "to delve into"]. *Dí* 覿 is pronounced *dájì* (達寂切) and means "to see; to encounter." *Shū* 樞 is pronounced *chǐyú* (齒楡切) and means "the pivot of a door; a hinge." *Nǎng* 曩 is pronounced *núlǎng* (奴朗切) and means "formerly; in the past."

---

**"He pledged his will to the great work of relieving [beings] (*hóng fǔ* 弘撫), and declared his return to his homeland."**

Regarding "弘撫" (*hóng fǔ*, "broadly benefiting"): seeking the Buddha's Way above is called "弘" (*hóng*, "to broaden [the Dharma]"); transforming sentient beings below is called "撫" (*fǔ*, "to nurture; to console"). Regarding "言旋" (*yán xuán*, "declaring/saying [one's] return"): the commentary (*jiān* 箋) says, "*yán* means 'to say; to state.'" The *Guǎngyǎ* [dictionary] says, "*yán* means 'to follow'; *xuán* means 'to return.'"

[The Japanese reading gloss for *xuán*/*yán* is] *kaerel-ri* 加倍禮利 ("has returned"). *Bāng* 邦 means "country; state."

**"His virtue was chosen by the Emperor's heart; his Way was extended in the heavenly literary compositions [of the imperial pen]."**

Regarding "德簡帝心" ("his virtue was chosen/distinguished in the Emperor's heart"): the *Lúnyǔ* (*Analects*) says, "The emperor's minister was not concealed; his selection resided in the emperor's heart (*jiǎn zài dì xīn* 簡在帝心)." Master Bāo (*Bāo shì* 苞氏) says, "[The tyrant] Jié (*Jié* 桀) occupied the position of the emperor's minister. [Though he had] transgressions and faults that could not be concealed or covered, [his character] resided clearly within the emperor's [discerning] heart."

Regarding "道延天藻" ("his Way was extended in the heavenly literary compositions"): the virtue and Way of the Tripiṭaka Master were set forth and praised by the jade brush (*yùbǐ* 玉筆, the imperial writing brush) of the Son of Heaven. As the *Shèngjiào Xù* (*Preface to the Sacred Teachings*) states: "There is a dharma master Xuanzang, the foremost leader of the Dharma gate. In his youth he harbored pure and keen [understanding]; he early awakened to the mind of the Three Voids (*sān kōng*). As he matured, he accorded with the divine mind; he first encompassed the practice of the Four Tolerances (*sì rěn* 四忍). The wind through the pines and the moon reflected in the water do not suffice to compare with his purity and brilliance. The dew of immortals and the luminous pearl — who could match his clarity and richness?" — and so forth.

**"Thereupon these precious verses appeared in the south [of India] in praise, and the golden tablets flowed eastward. He spread the obscured principles throughout the mysterious fords [of truth], and dispelled the mist of doubt from the richly adorned [realm of] thought."**

Regarding "宝偈南贊" ("the precious verses appearing in praise from the south"): *zàn* 贊 is pronounced *zǐqiě* (子且切) and means "to emerge; to appear; to be seen." This says that these precious verses appeared and were discovered in the south of Central India (*Zhōng Tiānzhú*). Regarding "金牒" ("golden tablets"): just as with the *Yogācārabhūmi* (*Yúqié [lùn]* 瑜伽論) and similar texts in the land of Gandhāra (*Jiàntuóluó guó* 健駄羅國) — fearing damage by water or fire, [the texts] were engraved as writing on golden tablets and stored in a jeweled chamber. Regarding "東流" ("flowing eastward"): this refers to how the Buddhist Dharma now flows eastward [into China]. *Chàng* 暢 means "to spread through; to communicate." Regarding "疑氛" (*yí fēn*, "mist of doubt"): *fēn* 氛 refers to an omen (*xiáng* 祥) — a portent of good or ill fortune that manifests in advance. Some versions have the character *qì* 気 (air; breath), pronounced *qùjì* (去旣切), meaning "the exhalation and inhalation of breath." [The text here argues it] should be the character *fēn* 氛. Regarding "縟思" (*rù sī*, "richly adorned thought"): *rù* 縟 is pronounced *rúshǔ* (如屬切) and means "colorfully adorned; decoratively embellished." This refers to how the great and lesser scriptures and treatises, the literary compositions, and the principles of doctrine that the Tripiṭaka Master resolved and understood in his mind are like *rù* — richly adorned and embellished.
