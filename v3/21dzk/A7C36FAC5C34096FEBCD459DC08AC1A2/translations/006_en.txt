**Upper Section**

(Continued from previous page) ...resided in a high tower, reading books aloud in a loud voice. At that time, beside the tower there was a cartwright. Having stopped his own work, he looked up toward the high tower, heard the king reading aloud, and asked the king: "What are you doing?" The king replied: "I am reading the books of the ancient sages." The cartwright said: "What the king is reading now contains only the dregs (zāopò, 糟粕). The true and profound matters reside solely within the hearts of the ancient sages. Reading brings no benefit. Why engage in such an empty endeavor?" The king was immediately furious and was about to punish him for his offense. The cartwright said: "I have been making carts until the age of eighty. My son learned the craft, but what he acquired was only the dregs — he could not obtain the deep technique. That deep technique resides only within my own heart. What the king is reading now is no different from this. One obtains only the dregs; one cannot obtain the truth." The king bore his words patiently, and suddenly a single insight arose in him; he turned and showed reverence to that man. And so on. This story is borrowed here to speak of "dregs (zāopò)." One should consult the original text. This means that the previously translated sūtras and śāstras obtained only the outer husk and did not obtain the true flavor. "Shuǎng (爽)" means "to deviate/differ." "Shèzhì (碩滯)" — "shè (碩)" means "great/large." "Hóngyí (鴻疑)" means the inability to melt away doubts like ice dissolving. "The obscure and splendid (幽絢) is repeatedly manifested (屢彰)" — "xuàn (絢)" is pronounced "xǔxuán" (許懸切), meaning the beautiful appearance of literary composition. Gathering five-colored brilliance is called "xuàn (絢)." Translating and composing texts is likewise. The various literary meanings of the sūtras and śāstras translated by the former and latter Tripiṭaka masters are like brilliantly woven literary colors. Yet the full details may be briefly set aside for now. Furthermore, "yōu xuàn (幽絢)" also refers to the titles of record-books such as the *Gaoseng zhuan* (高僧傳, Biographies of Eminent Monks). The various literary meanings and stylistic qualities of the sūtras and śāstras translated by former and latter Tripiṭaka masters are repeatedly manifested and shown within the *Youxuan*. Hence it is said: "the details may be omitted."

"Our own preceptor (親教), the Tripiṭaka Master Xuanzang (玄奘), embraced brilliance and surpassed the crowd. He illuminated the splendor of the *fú* tree (榑) upon the Numinous Terrace (靈臺). He accumulated virtue and dwelt at the source. He caused ripples of discourse to surge forth in the pond of wisdom (智沼)."

**Lower Section**

The fifth section below makes clear that the translations of Tripiṭaka Master Xuanzang are correct. "Hánzhāng bácuì (含章拔萃, 'embracing brilliance and surpassing the crowd')" — the *Zhouyi* (周易, Book of Changes) states: "Embracing brilliance (*hán zhāng*), proclaiming brilliance (*gào zhāng*)." [Note: The commentary indicates the text is difficult to read here and may contain a copyist's error.] This refers to the luminous manifestation of (the master's) virtue in high and low compositions alike. "Cuì (萃)" is pronounced "zuì (醉)," meaning "to gather/assemble." "Zhāng (章)" means "beautiful," "bright," and "colorful." "Illuminating the radiance of the *fú* tree upon the Numinous Terrace (燭槫景於靈臺)" — the *Wenzijülüe* (文字集略) states: "*Fú* (槫) is pronounced *fú* (扶). It is the great mulberry tree (大桑) beyond the sea." [pronunciation note follows] The *Shanhaijing* (山海經, Classic of Mountains and Seas) states: "There is a valley called Wenyuan Valley (温源谷) [note: this is the same as Tang Valley (湯谷)]. Above Tang Valley there is the *fú* tree. When one sun arrives, another sun departs [note: meaning they alternate and relieve each other]. Both are carried upon crows [note: within the character for 'sun' there is a three-legged crow]." As for "Numinous Terrace (靈臺)" — the *Zhuangzi* (莊子) states: "The ten thousand evils cannot be admitted into the Numinous Terrace." The commentary says: "The heart/mind is the terrace of the divine spirit (神靈)." "Causing ripples of discourse to surge forth in the pond of wisdom (涌談漪於智沼)" — the *Lunyu* (論語, Analects) states: "The wise person delights in water." "Yī (漪)" is pronounced "yūyí" (於宜切), meaning "overlapping waves/ripples."

"He galloped along the precious tracks of the Three Turnings (三輪), advancing beyond the golden sands. He clarified the true waves of the Eight Liberations (八解), purifying the jade well from afar."

"Jìn (晉)" means "to advance." "Golden sands (金砂)" — Li Shan (李善) states: "The Badi River (跋提河) is also called the Golden River (金河)." "Wù (騖)" is pronounced "wángjù" (亡具切), meaning "a beast running at full speed," and also "running about in disorder." "Three Wheels/Turnings (三輪)" refers to the Three Turnings of the Dharma Wheel. Another interpretation says it refers to the precepts (戒), meditation (定), and wisdom (慧); yet another says it refers to the Three Karmic Wheels (三業輪). "Purifying the Jade Well (清玉井)" — in Shu (蜀) there is a Jade Well. A western sage shot an arrow into the ground, and a well was thereby formed. The water is said to be as white as jade, hence the name "Jade Well." Another account says that when the Buddha was a crown prince, he shot an arrow with Devadatta (提婆達多) toward a spot in the sky, and the earth thereupon became a Jade Well. The Tripiṭaka Master came to this place in his quest for the Buddhist Dharma (and thus this is mentioned). "Liáo (遼)" is pronounced "lìtiáo" (力條切), meaning "distant/far."

"He forgot his own body in devotion to the Dharma, and entrusted himself to fate while praying for comprehension (to prevail)."

**Left Page**

**Upper Section**

"Xùn (殉)" is pronounced "cí jùn" (辭峻切), meaning "to seek" or "to follow." To follow a thing with one's own body is called "xùn (殉)." This speaks of the Tripiṭaka Master, being in this land, making a vow and going to the western lands, seeking the Dharma and praying for full comprehension.

---

"In secret accord with the heavenly truth (天眞), he only slightly resorted to accumulated learning."

"Tiānzhēn (天眞)" is another name for the naturally innate [spiritual capacity]. The Tripiṭaka Master, possessing innate and previously cultivated understanding (天眞宿解), went to the western lands, sought the Dharma, and attained the Dharma. That he "slightly resorted to accumulated learning" was also to further clarify his mind. "Wēi (微)" means "hidden/subtle."

---

"He did not spread his numinous power to display wonders; fundamentally he accumulated merit and extended auspiciousness."

"Chī (摛)" is pronounced "chǐlí" (耻離切), meaning "to seek" or "to spread forth." "Fěi chī líng ér xiǎn yì (匪摛靈而顯異, 'not spreading numinous power to display wonders')" — what the Tripiṭaka Master understood and learned was not about displaying wonders; rather, all the various matters simply resided within the Dharma itself. "Yùn (蘊)" means "to accumulate." "Yán (延)" means "to set forth/extend." "Yán xiáng (延祥)" means to set forth and extend auspiciousness (吉祥).

---

"He fully traversed the divine traces (神蹤) and exhaustively explored the treasury of secrets (祕府). What the former sages had not seen, he fully penetrated to the pivots of meaning (情樞). What the ancient wise ones had left behind, he encompassed to the very depths of the mind (心極)."

"Shénzōng (神蹤)" refers to the places where the Buddha traveled and walked while transforming sentient beings. The Tripiṭaka Master also traveled and traversed these places, as is extensively described in the *Xiyu ji* (西域記, Record of the Western Regions). "Bìfǔ (祕府)" refers to the sacred teachings (聖教). "Tàn bì (探祕)" (Japanese reading: *kanaheru*, "to probe/measure"). "Dí (覿)" is pronounced "dájì" (達寂切), meaning "to see." "Shū (樞)" is pronounced "chǐyú" (齒楡切), meaning the pivot/hinge of a door. "Nǎng (曩)" is pronounced "núlǎng" (奴朗切), meaning "formerly/of old."

---

"He vowed his resolve to propagate and nurture [the Dharma], declaring his (intention to) return to his homeland."

"Hóng fǔ (弘撫)" — seeking the Buddha Way above is called "hóng (弘, to propagate)"; transforming sentient beings below is called "fǔ (撫, to nurture)." "Yán xuán (言旋)" — the *Jian* (箋, commentary) states: "Yán (言) means 'to say/speak.'" The *Guangya* (廣雅) states: "Yán (言) means 'to follow.'" "Xuán (旋)" means "to return."

(Japanese reading: *kaereri*, "returned") "Bāng (邦)" means "a country/state."

"His virtue was discerned by the imperial heart; his Way was set forth in the imperial literary compositions."

"Dé jiǎn dì xīn (德簡帝心, 'virtue discerned by the imperial heart')" — the *Lunyu* (Analects) states: "The emperor's ministers are not concealed. What is discerned (*jiǎn*) lies in the emperor's heart." Master Bao (苞氏) states: "Jie (桀) occupied the position of the emperor's minister. If there were offenses, they could not be hidden. (What one truly is) is discerned by the imperial heart." "Dào yán tiān zǎo (道延天藻, 'the Way set forth in the heavenly literary compositions')" — the virtue of the Tripiṭaka Master was praised and set forth by the imperial brush of the Son of Heaven. As the *Shengjiao Xu* (聖教序, Preface to the Sacred Teachings) states: "There is the Dharma Master Xuanzang. He is the leading figure of the Dharma Gate. In his youth he nurtured fidelity and discernment; he early awakened to the mind of the Three Voids (三空). As he grew, he accorded with the divine spirit; he first encompassed the practice of the Four Forbearances (四忍). The wind through pines and the moon reflected in water are not worthy of comparison with his purity and brilliance. Who can match the clarity and luster of the celestial dew and bright pearl?" And so on.

"Thereupon this precious verse (寶偈) was extolled in the south, and the golden tablets (金牒) flowed eastward. He opened up the obscured principles (翳理) in the mysterious ford (玄津), and swept away the cloudy doubts in the elaborately adorned thoughts (縟思)."

"Bǎo jì nán zàn (寶偈南讃, 'the precious verse extolled in the south')" — "Zàn (贊)" is pronounced "zǐqiě" (子且切), meaning "to emerge/appear." This states that the precious verse (bǎo jì) emerged and became known in the south of Central India (中天竺). "Jīn dié (金牒, 'golden tablets')" — like the *Yogācārabhūmi* (瑜伽) and other texts from the Kingdom of Gandhāra (健陀羅國), fearing damage from water and fire, (the texts were) engraved on golden tablets and stored in a treasure chamber. "Dōng liú (東流)" means the eastward flow of Buddhism at present. "Chàng (暢)" means "to pervade/to penetrate." "Yí qì (疑氣)" — "fēn (氛)" refers to auspicious or inauspicious signs, things that appear in advance of good or ill fortune. Some editions write this as "qì (氣)," pronounced "qùjì" (去旣切), referring to breathing in and out. (Here) it should be read as the character "fēn (氛)." "Rù sī (縟思)" — "rù (縟)" is pronounced "rúzhǔ" (如屬切), meaning "colorful decoration/adornment." This means that the various sūtras, śāstras, literary compositions, and doctrinal principles of both the Hīnayāna and Mahāyāna that the Tripiṭaka Master understood in his mind are like *rù* (beautifully woven elaboration).
