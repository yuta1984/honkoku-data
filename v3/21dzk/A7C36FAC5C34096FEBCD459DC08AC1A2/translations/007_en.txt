**"Outstanding (*yǐng* 穎) and exemplary among the Tripiṭaka masters, he completely (*dān* 殫) surpassed the single supreme one (*yī rén* 一人 [i.e., the Emperor]). He was preeminent (*zhuó* 擢) among the Five Regions of India, and overshadowed (*líng yǎn* 陵揜) all of antiquity."**

Regarding "穎標三藏殫駕一人" ("outstanding and exemplary among the Tripiṭaka [masters], completely surpassing the one supreme person [the Emperor]"), two explanations have been handed down. The first says: *yǐng* 穎 is pronounced *yìbǐng* (役餅切) and refers to the tip of a rice ear, the point (*suì*/*mò*) of a grain stalk. *Dān* 殫 means "to exhaust; to end; to finish." *Jià* 駕 means "to ride; to mount." "One Person" (*yī rén* 一人) refers to the Son of Heaven (the Emperor). It means "arriving at the end [of life] while in the service of the One whom he served [the Emperor]." This refers to the Emperor Wenwu Sheng (*Wénwǔ Shèng*) — that is, Emperor Taizong, whose personal name was Shimin. In the twenty-third year of Zhenguan (649 CE), he lay ill in the great inner palace, suffering grievously from sickness. To escape the heat of the capital, he withdrew to the Mountain Coolness Palace (*Shānliánggōng*) to rest and recuperate, but he gradually declined. On the day of his final passing, he lay on his back resting his head on the knees of the Tripiṭaka Dharma Master [Xuanzang], and peacefully passed away. Hence it is said "he completely surpassed [in service to] the one supreme person." The second explanation says: *yǐng* 穎 is pronounced *jūlǐng* (居領切) and means "bright; brilliant." *Biāo* 標 means "to display; to manifest." "One Person" (*yī rén*) refers to the Tripiṭaka [Master] himself. In manifesting and displaying the Tripiṭaka [Master's greatness], he comprehensively and exclusively exalts and mounts that one person, the Tripiṭaka [Master]. *Zhuó* 擢 is pronounced *dázhuó* (達卓切) and means "to rise above; to draw out; to surpass."

**"Who could match those of Jiāyī (*Paramārtha*) in sharing in glory (*xiāng yù* 驤譽), who monopolized beauty in vain in the domain of phonology (*shēngmíng* 聲明)? Kumārajīva (*Tóngshòu* 童壽) spread his fragrance, but was merely seen to be praised in the *Zhōngguān* (*Mādhyamaka*) — that is all."**

"家依" (*Jiāyī*) refers to Tripiṭaka Master Paramārtha (*Zhēndì Sānzàng* 真諦三藏). *Xiāng* 驤 is pronounced *sīliáng* (思良切) and means "to elevate; to soar; to transcend." "童壽" (*Tóngshòu*, "Child of Longevity") is the reverend name for Dharma Master Kumārajīva (Luóshí 羅什). "見称" (*jiànchēng*) means "that which is praised; what is extolled." "見" (*jiàn*) here means "所" (*suǒ*, "what is ..."), not the "見" of "seeing/hearing." "而已" (*éryi*) should together be read as "耳" (*ěr*, "that is all; nothing more"). Since this is not necessarily a paired phrase, it says "what is praised."

**"These [ten commentaries], originally assembled and divided into groups (*huì jù qún fēn* 彙聚群分) as ten [separate works], were now comprehensively and carefully translated by [the Tripiṭaka Master] and blended (*róu* 糅) into a single work."**

What follows is the sixth section, which briefly explains the name of the *Chéngyúshí lùn* (Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi-śāstra) translated by the Tripiṭaka Master. *Huì* 彙 is pronounced *húguì* (胡貴切) and means "category; class; type." *Róu* 糅 is pronounced *nǚjiù* (女救切) and means "mixed rice; a mixed dish." "群" (*qún*) [Japanese gloss: *tamuro* or *murakaru*, "a group; to gather"] refers to the commentaries of the ten bodhisattvas. Blending them (*róu* 糅) into a single work was entirely

the achievement (*gōng* 功) of the Tripiṭaka [Master].

**"He weighed and deliberated (*shānghùo* 商攉) upon the Chinese and Sanskrit [languages], examined and clarified (*zhēng quán* 徴詮) the light and heavy [distinctions of meaning], shaped and refined (*táozhēn* 陶甄) the differences among the various meanings, and exclusively (*yǒu* 有) accorded with the composition (*zhì* 製) of a single master."**

Regarding "商攉" (*shānghùo*, "to weigh and deliberate"): *shāng* 商 refers to a merchant's activity of pursuing profit through careful deliberation. *Huò* 攉 is pronounced *qùxué* (去學切) and refers to a wooden plank laid across water for the purpose of crossing. Regarding "華梵" (*huá fàn*, "Chinese and Sanskrit"): *huá* 華 refers to the Great Tang [China], while *fàn* 梵 refers to India. *Zhēng* 徴 means "to clarify; to illuminate." *Quán* 詮 means "to reveal; to manifest." Regarding "輕重" (*qīng zhòng*, "light and heavy"): this refers to the distinction of existence and non-existence (*yǒu wú* 有無). Furthermore, "light and heavy" also refers to tonal distinctions in phonology (*shēng* 聲): the meaning of a word may differ depending on whether the sound is light or heavy. *Yǒu* 有 is pronounced *yújiǔ* (于九切) and means "solely; exclusively." "有叶一師之製" (annotation: it appears there may be a missing character below *zhì* 製). Furthermore, "light and heavy" meaning "existence and non-existence" refers to the fact that the Tripiṭaka Dharma Master, at the time of translation, carefully deliberated upon and definitively determined the Sanskrit and Chinese terminology, and also clarified where the ten great bodhisattvas, in their commentaries on the root verses, had inclusions and omissions relative to one another. *Xié* 叶 means "to accord with; to harmonize."

**"'Chéng-Wéishí' (*Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi*) raises the great guiding principle (*hóng gāng* 宏綱) to designate (*jīng* 旌) the general title of the whole work. Furthermore, the word 'lùn' (*śāstra*, 'treatise') lifts up the emblematic mirror (*zǎojìng* 藻鏡) to distinguish (*jiǎn* 簡) the distinctive appellation separating it from the two [other] Baskets (*piṭaka*)."**

*Jīng* 旌 is pronounced *zǐshèng* (子盛切) and means "to indicate; to mark; to display." "藻鏡" (*zǎojìng*, "the decorated mirror"): this refers to the ornamental designs (*wéncǎi* 文采) on the back of a mirror, which resemble aquatic plant (*zǎo* 藻) patterns; hence the name "decorated mirror." "二藏" (*èr zàng*, "two baskets") refers to the Sūtra Piṭaka (*jīng* 経) and Vinaya Piṭaka (*lǜ* 律). The various literary meanings of the Abhidharma (*āpídámó* 阿毗達磨) are like the decorative patterns on the back of a mirror. The illuminating clarity of the learning of wisdom (*huì xué* 慧學) is like the reflective face of a mirror. Hence "the decorated mirror" (*zǎojìng*) is cited as a metaphor for the Abhidharma (*lùn* 論, or Dharma) Basket (*huì zàng* 慧藏).

**"'Chéng' 成 (*siddhi*, 'accomplishment/establishment') is the designation of that which accomplishes (*néngjǐng* 能成), and its function (*gōng* 功) lies in establishment (*chénglì* 成立). The name 'that which is accomplished by Vijñaptimātratā (*wéishí suǒchéng* 唯識所成)' takes clarification and discernment (*jiǎn liǎo* 簡了) as its meaning (*yì* 義). The great enlightened one's (*dàjué* 大覚, the Buddha's) purport that 'only consciousness (*wéi shí* 唯識) exists' is exalted and flourishing, and the meaning of the Middle Way (*zhōngdào* 中道) established by the root verses is clearly manifested."**

"成乃能成之稱以成立爲功" ("'Chéng' is the designation of that which accomplishes, with establishment as its function"): this interprets the character *chéng* 成 exclusively in relation to the commentary (*shì lùn* 釋論). Since the root

treatise (*běn lùn* 本論 = the Triṃśikā, thirty-verse treatise) also "accomplishes" (*chéng*) Vijñaptimātratā, [the character *chéng*] here does not refer solely to the character *chéng* in the root treatise. "唯識所成之名以簡了爲義" ("the name 'accomplished by Vijñaptimātratā' takes clarification and discernment as its meaning"): this interprets the characters "唯識" exclusively in relation to the root treatise. *Zhù* 著 means "brilliantly manifest; clear."

**"'Wéi' 唯 (*only*) means 'to distinguish and separate' (*jiǎnbié* 簡別); it excludes (*zhē* 遮) the [claim of] non-existence of external objects (*wài jìng* 外境). 'Shí' 識 (*consciousness*) means 'that which cognizes' (*nénghǎo* 能了); it explicates (*quán* 詮) the [claim of] existence of the inner mind (*nèi xīn* 内心). The substance (*tǐ* 體) of consciousness (*shí*) is precisely [what is meant by] 'only' (*wéi*): this is a *dharmī-dharma* compound (*chí yè shì* 持業釋, *tatpuruṣa*-type [endocentric]). The nature (*xìng* 性) and characteristics (*xiāng* 相) of consciousness do not depart from the mind. Mental factors (*xīn suǒ* 心所) and the mind-king (*xīn wáng* 心王) take consciousness as their master (*zhǔ* 主). Returning to the mind and dissolving characteristics, the general term is 'Vijñaptimātratā' (*wéishí* 唯識)."**

This passage interprets the earlier statement "the great enlightened one's purport that 'only consciousness exists' is exalted and flourishing." Through the thirty verses (*triṃśikā*), the domain (*jìng* 境), practice (*xíng* 行), and fruition (*guǒ* 果) within Vijñaptimātratā are revealed; the nature and characteristics of consciousness are preserved while external objects beyond the mind are eliminated. Through this, the meaning of "mind-only" (*wéixīn* 唯心) within the sūtras becomes all the more clearly exalted. Regarding "mental factors and the mind-king take consciousness as their master": it is understandable that mental factors (*xīn suǒ* 心所) take consciousness as their master. But how can the mind-king (*xīn wáng* 心王) take consciousness as its master? In explanation: two views have been transmitted. The first says: among mind (*xīn* 心), mentation (*yì* 意), and consciousness (*shí* 識), "mind" serves as the master overall, because the eighth consciousness (ālayavijñāna, *āláiyéshí* 阿頼耶識) is "mind." Thus for the present, when arguing from the standpoint of the object (*jìng*), consciousness (*shí*) is taken as the master. The second says: "mind" (*xīn*) refers only to the substance [of mental phenomena] and is applicable to both the causal stage (*yīn wèi* 因位) and the fruition stage (*guǒ wèi* 果位). "Consciousness" (*shí*) refers to the function (*yòng*) [operating] at the causal stage; it is strong and superior only at the causal stage. The present [text] clarifies the meaning of "master" (*zhǔ* 主) with reference to the stronger and superior aspect of mind. Hence it is named "Vijñaptimātratā" (*wéishí* 唯識, "only consciousness") and not "mind-only" (*wéixīn* 唯心). If so, "only consciousness" does not extend to the fruition stage. [See the *Wéishí Zhāng* (唯識章) for further details.]

**"'Wéi' 唯 (*only*) excludes the [claim that] objects [exist externally] (*jìng yǒu* 境有); those who cling to [the view of] existence (*yǒu* 有) lose the truth (*zhēn* 真). 'Shí' 識 (*consciousness*) distinguishes [the mind from the claim of] emptiness of mind (*xīn kōng* 心空); those who are mired in emptiness (*kōng* 空) deviate from what is real (*shí* 實). Therefore, to obscure this [understanding of] emptiness and existence (*kōng yǒu* 空有) is to be long submerged in the two extremes (*èrbiān* 二辺). To awaken to that existence-and-emptiness (*yǒu kōng* 有空) [in its true sense] is to tread loftily upon the Middle Way (*zhōngdào* 中道)."**

This passage interprets the earlier statement "the meaning of the Middle Way established by the root verses is clearly manifested." Through the commentarial masters' (*shì lùn shī* 釋論師) commentary on and elucidation of the root verses, external objects (*wài jìng* 外境, considered as "existence") are excluded and inner consciousness (*nèi shí* 内識, considered as "empty") [from being clung to in its] emptiness is eliminated. Once the attachment to emptiness and existence is removed, the meaning of the Middle Way becomes all the more clearly illuminated.

**"The root treatise of thirty [verses] is named 'Vijñaptimātratā' (*wéishí* 唯識). Relying upon (*jiè* 藉) this [root treatise] to accomplish (*chéng* 成) that [commentary], it is named 'Chéngyúshí' (*Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi*, 'Accomplishment of Vijñaptimātratā'). The accomplishment (*chéng* 成) of Vijñaptimātratā reveals (*zhāng* 彰) the purport (*lùn zhǐ* 論旨) of the treatise. This is a *samāsa* compound (*sān mó suō* 三摩娑釋) with the *bahuvrīhi*/*tatpuruṣa* type (*yī shì* 依士立名). The *supti* ending (*sū màn duō* 蘇漫多 [= *sup* case-ending in Sanskrit grammar]) with the possessive/genitive construal (*shǔ zhǔ* 属主) serves as the title (*mù* 目, designation)."**

This passage pairs *néngjǐng* (the accomplishing) with *suǒchéng* (the accomplished) and explains the name of the treatise according to the Six Modes of Interpretation (*liù shì* 六釋) and the Eight Case-endings (*bā zhuǎn* 八囀). The Six Modes are as [explained in] the *Zǒng Liàojiǎn Zhāng* (General Discernment Chapter). As for the Eight Case-endings [annotation: it appears the text after "如" 如 is missing in various editions].

**"In the treatise, the host and guest (*bīn zhǔ* 賓主) meet here in full vigor (*yún liè* 云烈), and the banners and drums (*qí gǔ* 旗鼓) are thereupon raised. The obscured pass (*yōu guān* 幽關) is thrown wide open, and the wondrous meaning (*miào yì* 玅義) is herein made manifest (*zé* 賾)."**

Regarding "云烈" (*yún liè*): *yún* 云 means "there; at this place." *Liè* 烈 means "fierce; intense; a blazing fire; violent." The *Yùnjí* (*Rhyme Collection*) says: "*liè* 列 means 'to arrange in a row'; *liè* 烈 means 'a stalwart warrior'" (Japanese reading gloss: *takekushite*, "vigorously/fiercely"). "載揚" (*zǎi yáng*, "banners and drums are raised"): when two armies engage in battle, the banners and drums are urgently raised and sounded. *Zǎi* 載 means "thereupon; then." Regarding "幽關洞開玅義斯賾" ("the obscured pass is thrown open and the wondrous meaning is herein revealed"): this is like the moment when armies engage in battle, break through the fortified gate, and throw it open — all the things within the castle become visible and manifest. That is to say, through [the dialectic of] question and answer, the heterodox is refuted and the orthodox is revealed, and the profound and wondrous meaning of the true nature of all dharmas (*zhūfǎ shí xiàng* 諸法實相) becomes fully manifest.

**"'Using teaching to accomplish teaching' — relying upon teaching to establish principle: this [phrase] means 'accomplishing this treatise (*shì lùn*)', which is a *dharmī-dharma* compound (*chí yè shì* 持業釋). 'Using principle to accomplish principle, and by means of principle establishing teaching': this [phrase] means '[it is] a treatise of [this] accomplishment (*chéng zhī lùn*),' which is a *tatpuruṣa* compound (*yī shì shì* 依士釋)."**

"以教成教" ("using teaching to accomplish teaching"): this means that by means of the condensed teaching of the thirty verses, the teaching of the Buddha's pronouncement — "Only mind [underlies] the three realms" (*sānjiè wéixīn* 三界唯心) — is established and set forth. "資教成理" ("relying upon teaching to establish principle"): this means that through the teaching of the thirty verses, the reasoned principle (*dàolǐ* 道理) of Vijñaptimātratā as expounded [therein] is established. "以理成理" ("using principle to accomplish principle"): this means that through [the analysis of] domain (*jìng* 境), practice (*xíng* 行), and fruition (*guǒ* 果), the reasoned principle of Vijñaptimātratā as expounded by the teaching is established.
