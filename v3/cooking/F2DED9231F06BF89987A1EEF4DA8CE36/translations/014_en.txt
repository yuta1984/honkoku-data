**Menu of One Soup, Two or Three Dishes (Ichijū Ni-san-sai)**

**Arrangement A:**
　Flat dish (hira)　　Soup
　Pickled vegetables　Rice
　Grilled dish　　　　(Note: sliced fish and similar items may also be served as "hiki" dishes)

　Sake cup
　　Clear soup (suimono)
　　Side dish (sakana)
　　　Sake decanter (chōshi)

**Arrangement B:**
　Flat dish　　　Soup
　　　　　　　Pickled vegetables
　Grilled dish　　Rice
　Small fish / sliced fish
　Side dish　　　Deep bowl (tsubo-zara)
　　　　　　　Small cup (猪口, choko)
　Sake cup (also noted as "sake tomo")
　Sake decanter

Sets composed in such combinations are called "makanai" (staff meal / informal meal), "yowatashi," or simply "zen" (a meal tray), and are served promptly at mealtime.

The menu arrangements and combinations described above have been set out in sequence based on transmission texts (denshō), in accordance with current practice.

**[Left Folio]**

These are the formal, public-facing procedures, and the various households of the present day follow these same conventions. Even within a single household, there may be slight differences in terminology. There may also be differences in the order in which courses are arranged and served. The hospitality offered after the formal meal service (zenbu) — such as "za-gawari" (changing seats for a more informal gathering) or "mimi-kiki" (intimate conversation gathering) — is called "yokyō" (supplementary banquet), "kōdan" (post-banquet), or "hai" (a round of cups), and it is the custom of the present age to serve these as additional entertainment. There may or may not be a fixed order for each of these additional courses. Furthermore, while this school does not claim complete mastery of all details, having some awareness of them is itself a form of preparedness; thus, what has been seen and heard at various households and learned from professionals in the culinary arts (hōchō-ke) has been recorded here.

During such a meal, it is essential to compose the combination and serving order so that warm dishes, cold dishes, soups (shirumono), simmered dishes (irimono), grilled dishes (yakimono), and steamed dishes (mushimono) are balanced across the five flavors. Paying careful attention to food compatibilities and combinations is said to be of the utmost importance. It is also noted that such hospitality may, if carried to excess, extend into a late-night meal or an all-night banquet.
