Because Japan is a country suited to Esoteric Buddhism, the Great Master Shōtoku first propagated the Buddhist teachings; thereafter, (En-no) Ozunu and Taichō, through spontaneous spiritual awakening and austerities, gradually spread the Esoteric teachings; and later, the Eight Masters Who Went to Tang vied with one another — like orchids and chrysanthemums competing in beauty — to transmit and uphold it. All of this arose from one and the same fundamental vow. Thus, it is said that the Bodhisattva Hōki, Prince Shōtoku, and the Great Master En-no-Ozunu are of one and the same essence.

**Question:** Is En-no-Ozunu a monk or a layman?

**Answer:** He is of lay appearance. "Upāsaka" is a Sanskrit term; in Chinese it is rendered as *qingxinshi* (清信士, "pure faithful gentleman"). It refers to a lay person who firmly upholds the five precepts throughout one's lifetime. Although the ascetic maintained lay appearance, because he had neither wife nor children, he might also be called a "monk with hair." Today's yamabushi and those who call themselves Shugenja are those who follow in the ascetic's footsteps. According to the founding legend of the ascetic, his uncle was called Gangyō. It is also recorded: the ascetic wore a square cap (sumibōshi) and used the nine-strip robe; he donned a straw raincoat, leaned upon a tin staff (shakujō), and carried a single-pronged vajra (tokkō). Gikaku was the first to wear a hood (tōkin) and the robe of Fudō, and to carry a sword. Gigen wore the robe with a jeweled crown (hōkan) and carried a backpack-basket (oi). Gishin wore a jeweled crown and held prayer beads with a robe. Juen wore a square cap and robe, and also carried a rope…

**The Origin of the Kanekake (Bell-Hanging Rock) of Ōmine**

The Kanekake of Ōmine is a sheer cliff of ten jō, a place so perilous that even one person at a time climbing it is dangerous. Nevertheless, in ancient times, a single bronze bell suddenly appeared, hung upon this rock. The inscription read: "Tōtōmi Province, Sano District, Harada Village, Chōfuku-ji temple, the second day of the sixth month of the seventh year of Tengyō." Upon inquiring into its origins, it is told that long ago there was a yamabushi who lived before the gate of Chōfuku-ji temple in Harada Village of Tōtōmi Province. He was poor and unable to undertake the peak-entry pilgrimage (mine-iri). The abbot of Chōfuku-ji took pity on him and every year, without fail, contributed gold and silver to help him make preparations for the pilgrimage. In time, the abbot retired, and the resources of the new abbot greatly exceeded those of the previous one. However, the new abbot was tightfisted and refused to lend the silver needed for the pilgrimage, falsely claiming: "In this temple, there is no money other than this bell." At those very words, a monk of great strength appeared, seized the bell, flew off through the air, and hung it upon the rocky summit of Śākyamuni Peak (Shakaga-take) of Ōmine. It is said that to this day, there is what is called the Kanekake-ishi (Bell-Hanging Stone). Whether this mighty monk was a transformation of the ascetic himself, or a being of the kind of Zenki and Goki (the fore-demon and rear-demon), is unknown.
