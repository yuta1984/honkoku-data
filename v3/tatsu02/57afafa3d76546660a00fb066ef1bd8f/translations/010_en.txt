(Continued from previous page) …On the night of the twenty-third day of the sixth month, the ascetic invited and led a monk called Shunjin of the Northern Bō (Kita-no-Bō) up Mount Kongō, where he conversed at length with the Bodhisattva Hōki. From there they proceeded to Tōnomi-no-mine (Tonomine), paid reverence at Hase-dera temple, made a pilgrimage to Miwa, and traveled to Kasuga-yama in Nara, where the Myōjin (divine presence) manifested in physical form and engaged in dialogue with them for several days. The deity spoke, saying: "A person in Ōsaka is making an image of the ascetic. Go and have a look." At this, the ascetic said: "Sculptors of this day cannot carve a figure as lean and gaunt as I truly am. Nevertheless, let us go and see." Thereupon he flew through the air to Ōsaka, went to the house of the sculptor, and upon seeing the work, declared: "This sculptor is most unskilled — the figure is too fat and unsightly." When asked about the image, the sculptor replied: "This statue is to be dedicated to Ozasa in Yoshino." From there, the ascetic again flew off to the high mountains.

As for Shunjin: he received transmission of seventeen Sanskrit characters from the ascetic, and the details of how he produced wondrous secret talismans and cured all manner of illness are recorded fully in the first volume of the *Myōō-shū* (Collection of Numinous Responses). Therefore, it is omitted here.

Also, in early June of the beginning of the Genroku era, a strange person ascended Mount Kongō. He appeared to be over sixty years of age, with hair that was half-white. He wore bronze clogs (ashida) fitted with iron thong-straps, leaned upon an iron staff, bore an iron-boned hat upon his head, and came to the ascetic's lodging (gyōja-bō) to beg for food. He was invited in and given a meal, whereupon he produced a bamboo flute and from within it

【Left folio】

drew out iron chopsticks, which he used to eat. The assembled monks found this most extraordinary. Examining his clogs in detail, they found that the wooden teeth were worn down and that the impressions of toe-prints remained where he had trodden — clearly not something that an ordinary old man would use. The abbot asked: "Do you always wear these clogs?" The old man replied: "I always wear these and climb even as far as Ōmine, Katsuragi, and Mount Fuji." All present found this deeply suspicious. Watching him as he departed, he walked with ease, descending in the direction of Chihayaburu (Chihaya). After that, he never came again.

Reflecting on this now: could it be that the ascetic had come around on his rounds? How regrettable it is that one could think so — those present were filled with lasting regret afterward. Truly, there is no way that a man of over sixty could put on bronze clogs, take up an iron staff, and climb mountains in such a manner. This is a wonder of recent times. The ascetic told Dōshō that, though he dwells in Tang China, he crosses to Japan once every three years to make the rounds of the three sacred mountains — and indeed, this must be true.

> *Kokoro dani / makoto no michi ni / kanainaba / inorazu tote mo / kami ya mamorān*
> "If only one's heart is in accord with the True Way, even without prayer, will not the gods protect one?"

> △ *Shōshin ni / kono yo wa hito ni / homerarete / nochi no yo wa mata / hotoke to zo naru*
> "Truly, in this world one is praised by others, and in the next world one becomes a Buddha."

> *Mina hito no / naoki kokoro zo / sono mama ni / kami no kami nite / kami no kami nari*
> "The upright heart of every person — just as it is — is the divine among the divine, the very essence of the gods."

The last poem is an oracle of the Great Deity of Wakasa-hiko, and is said to be the sacred verse of the Four Deities.
