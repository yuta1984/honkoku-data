【Right Page】

　　*Furusato no / asaji no niwa no / tsuyu no ue ni / tokowa kusaba to / yatoru tsuki kana*

(Upon the dew that falls upon the reedy, overgrown garden of my old home — the moon takes its lodging in the grass and leaves, as if making a bed there. The moonlight alone remains unchanged in this ruined dwelling.)

He made a pilgrimage to the shrine at Kashima, and there quietly offered prayers and religious devotion. Meditating upon the ancient history of the god enshrined there, he composed:

　　*Kasugano no / tsuyu ni zo utsuru / azumaji no / michi no hate yori / ideshi tsukikage*

(Reflected in the dew of Kasuga Plain — the light of the moon that rose from beyond the far end of the eastern road. The moonlight binds together Kasuga of the homeland and the far reaches of the eastern provinces through which he has wandered.)

He was then moved to think upon his distant ancestors, and a longing for the capital arose within him.

【Left Page】

　　*Ito semete / koishiki koro to / furusato ni / kasumi no koromo / kaeru karigane*

(At the very time when I long most intensely for my homeland, the wild geese fly homeward wearing robes of mist — their cries echo my own yearning.)

So it is said that he composed, and set out on the road back to Kyoto. Taking only waka poetry as his companion, he composed poems at many places along the way — there are a great many such verses, but how could one possibly learn or record them all? Furthermore, he traveled even to the remote hinterlands far beneath the heavens, and to the distant shores of the western sea, and after seeing all of these, he returned to the capital.
