In the direction from which (the tsunami) comes, willows should be planted at intervals of one ken (approximately 1.8 meters), with all branches cut away every year. When they have grown to a height of over two jō (approximately 6 meters) and a trunk circumference of two shaku (approximately 60 centimeters), one should be able to escape the peril of large drifting objects being pushed against one's home. As the years pass and the trees grow to a trunk circumference of three shaku (approximately 90 centimeters) or more, no matter how large a ship comes floating in, it should absolutely never be knocked over. Among all trees, there is none that surpasses the willow in speed of growth. It is therefore recommended that these be planted in order to avoid such great disasters.

Illustrated Guide to Fire Prevention Strategies　　One volume combining upper and lower volumes with appendix
　This book is a remarkable work for the prevention of fire disasters. By means of the protective devices devised by the venerable author, even the most violent winds and raging fires can be swiftly blocked and stopped, freeing tens of thousands of houses from the great calamity of spreading conflagration — a remarkable art never before discovered by earlier sages. The appendix records methods for preventing disasters such as great earthquakes, great tsunamis, great storms, and waterspouts, with the aim of enabling people everywhere — from cities to harbors and coastlines — to escape the dangers of water, fire, earthquakes, and wind. This is by no means the suspicious empty theorizing of prayers and incantations; its methods and techniques are all presented in illustrated form, easily understood even by women and children, and it may truly be called one great and remarkable work of unparalleled benefit to the nation throughout all ages.

Illustrated Guide to the Technique of Directing Water to Irrigate Hills　　Complete
　This book is likewise a remarkable art never before discovered by earlier sages. With barely any human effort, it sets in motion the stagnant water of low-lying swamps and ponds, causing it to ascend hills of three jō, five jō, or even ten or twenty jō (approximately 9, 15, 30, or 60 meters), thereby irrigating fields and paddies, freeing farmers from the peril of drought damage, and bringing them peace of mind. It truly requires neither treadmill wheels nor water wheels, and eliminates all further worry about water scarcity. This too is a work of the utmost importance for the benefit of the nation.
