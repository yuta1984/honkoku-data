As shown in the figure on the right, the joints of stones stacked two or three tiers high should be bored out in the shape of a balance weight (fundo), and molten lead poured into the holes and allowed to cool and solidify. All other stone walls should likewise be reinforced using this method. When stone lanterns and stone torii gates are similarly fixed to their base stones with notches filled with lead, they will absolutely never topple over in even the most severe earthquake.

○ The method of ground preparation and foundation laying (jigyō chikuso) varies depending on the hardness or softness of the soil at each location, and is therefore not uniform. One should thus make the foundation sturdy in accordance with the soil conditions and local customs of the particular place. Especially in areas where the soil is extremely soft, requiring excavation to a depth of four to five shaku (approx. 1.2–1.5 m),

followed by the insertion of sacrificial foundation timber, the placement of squared stones on top, and the laborious pounding and compacting by a large crew over several days — in short, in boggy or marshy areas (igomi) where such work requires enormous effort and expense — a simpler method for laying foundations is as follows: excavate the ground to a depth of two to three shaku, then mix stone chips and the dregs of oil-lime (shikkui) mortar together and pour the mixture in. Using a round timber of about one hand's-breadth in diameter, patiently pound and compact the mixture, then continue to add more material little by little, pounding it down each time, repeating this process several times. Done this way, the work is remarkably simple and inexpensive, and the resulting structure grows more solid with each passing year, eventually consolidating into a single mass like a stone well-frame (igeta), becoming firm as a great boulder.
