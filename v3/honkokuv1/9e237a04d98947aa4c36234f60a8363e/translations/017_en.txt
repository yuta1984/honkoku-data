The figure on the right shows the interior of the storehouse (dozō) entrance depicted in Figure 5, where cross-shaped diagonal bracing has been installed, and onto which boards of one sun thickness have been further nailed horizontally. The surface of these split boards is to be planed smooth, and they are to be nailed to each pillar with three large nails weighing 300 me each. At the area just below the beam (keta), a space equal to the width of one board is to be left as a removable panel. Through this opening, dried Arakawa sand—free of salt and dried in sunlight on a clear day—is to be poured without gaps into the space between the wall and the boards; the panel is then to be pushed firmly into place and secured. When this is done, even if rice grain is stored inside, there will be no

○ In the conventional construction of a storehouse roof, several gassho-gi (rafters joined at a ridge) are straddled across the ground ridge beam, and on top of these, the ridge beam, purlins, and common rafters are all planed smooth and carefully assembled in a grid pattern. The roof framing to support the tiles involves a great deal of labor. Furthermore, the practice of nailing polished boards horizontally and then applying a kokera-buki (shingle) base layer for a doi-buki (earth-covered) roof is the same everywhere—it merely inflates the craftsmen's labor costs and provides little actual sturdiness. It is truly a wasteful and unnecessary expense. My devised method involves no wasteful expenditure and is economical...
