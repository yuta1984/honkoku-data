When samurai households are impoverished, they are frequently put to shame before townspeople's households. In castle towns of other provinces, regulations on clothing are strictly enforced, and according to one's rank, wearing cotton is prescribed as the rule—those of still lower station go without saying (they have even fewer options).

1. When one tastes the food of Kyoto and Osaka, there is nothing whatsoever that surpasses what is found in Edo. Indeed, just as the proverb "Edo's *kui-daore*" (ruining oneself through gluttony) says, the night trade along Nihonbashi-dōri consists entirely of food vendors, and the livelihoods conducted from the back tenements (*ura-dana*) generate enormous profits. Beyond this, restaurants and teahouses line up side by side, and in every block there are many vendors selling eel, as well as those trading in boar, deer, chicken, and soft-shelled turtle (*suppon*). Some people regard these as mere side dishes (*hosai*)

If the people of this world were to curtail expenditure on the three necessities of clothing, food, and shelter, their expenses would diminish, they would manage to get by, and they ought to make preparedness for emergencies their paramount concern. The splendid city of Edo grows more prosperous with each passing day; there is not the slightest vacant plot of land remaining, and even to the far reaches of the Musashino plain, houses stand so densely packed that one cannot even catch a glimpse of the moon passing between the eaves. Truly this is a blessed reign to be grateful for—yet the one thing above all that must be feared is, needless to say, the frequency of fires.
