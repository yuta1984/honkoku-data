(Continued from previous page) — one falls into panic, and husbands, wives, and children are scattered in all directions; some are engulfed in smoke, while the elderly and young are trampled to death by people and horses. There is nothing in this world as terrifying as a fire. In the case of drowning, one can still recognize the body by clothing and features, but those who perish in flames turn completely black, so that even siblings cannot be told apart. Water is what overcomes fire, yet even in rainy weather, a fire does not immediately go out. Once it becomes a great conflagration, neither water nor human effort can prevail against it.

1. To extinguish falling sparks, one should wet an old palm-fiber (*shuro*) broom and use it to beat them out — this is the teaching of elders.

　　　　*Hibuse no Uta* (Fire-Suppression Poem) — Nichiren Daibosatsu

*Shimo-bashira / kōri no hari ni / yuki no keta / mizu no taruki ni / hi koso kiekere*
(Frost pillars, rafters of ice, beams of snow — in timbers of water, let fire be extinguished!)

　　　　This verse should be copied out and affixed to the ridgepole of one's home.

*Nuruzo nedai / tanomuzo taruki / yanaka-take / nanigoto araba / okose mune no ki*
(The floor joists are soaked; I rely on you, rafters; bamboo in the roof — should anything happen, rouse the ridgepole!)

　　　　The above two verses should be recited every night before going to sleep.

*Yoku kike yo / hotaru hodo naru / tabako no hi / kokoro yuruse ba / hayagane no koe*
(Listen well — a tobacco ember no bigger than a firefly: let your guard down, and you will hear the sound of the alarm bell!)
