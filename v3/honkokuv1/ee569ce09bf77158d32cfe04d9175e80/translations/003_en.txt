Enchanted by the blossoms at Koganei, the author wandered about visiting companions in verse, floating a boat along the peach-blossom-lined banks, spending the day composing poems. That night, lodging in the vicinity of the Tamagawa river, he heard that there was a fire in the direction of great Edo. Climbing the hill before him to look, he saw the vast eastern sky glowing as if painted with vermillion (kōmyōtan). Before long, a raftsman returned from Edo and reported that everything had burned from the shores of Takanawa all the way to the far reaches of Asajigahara. "Just as I feared," he thought — his spirits dampened and his heart sinking — and he hurried home at dawn. The rain fell heavily, making the journey utterly miserable. Since he had been born in the Year of the Boar, the Year of the Tiger was his fourth "tekisetsu" (a direction or year deemed inauspicious), yet at the time he had taken no precautions whatsoever.

Just as the physiognomist had foretold, he himself escaped the disaster, but his house and storehouse were reduced to ashes. Though he had known there were omens, he had assumed he would escape entirely unscathed and had not even been careful about sleeping away from home — this was the error of a foolish man.

Furthermore, there was a certain person who, on the twenty-first day of the third month (Yayoi) of this year — the memorial day of Kōbō Daishi — set out on the road toward Rokugō. When he was about to board a roadside palanquin, the blade of his short sword slipped from its scabbard and fell, which startled him and dampened his will to proceed. He promptly reconsidered and abandoned his plans for the pilgrimage, giving the palanquin bearers money for sake instead...
