【Right folio】

The damage was immense. Suijin-no-Mori, Sekiya, Suda-mura, Terajima-mura, and Ukeji-mura suffered damage. There was much collapse along Hikifune-dōri, Kinoshita-gawa, Yakushi, Yotsugimaga-rikane-no-watashi, Hirai, Shibamata, Kasai, Kameari, and the Nigōhan-ryō area. Collapsed buildings: 3,415; collapsed earthen storehouses: 22.

Regarding the major highways and surrounding areas: along the Tōkaidō, damage extended as far as Hodogaya, with various collapses in Kanagawa, and the effects reached as far as Honmoku, Kanazawa, Kamakura, Enoshima, and Uraga. ○ Along the Nakasendō, damage extended as far as Takasaki in Kōzuke Province; on this road there were places where the ground cracked and water gushed forth, with much collapse between Warabi and Ōmiya. ○ Along the Kōshū-kaidō, damage extended as far as Hachiōji. ○ Along the Nikkō-dōchū, damage extended as far as Utsunomiya, with collapse reaching as far as Sōka. ○ Along the Mito-kaidō, damage extended as far as Tsuchiura. ○ In the Shimōsa direction, the shaking was particularly strong in the vicinity of Sakasai, with much collapse in the Gyōtoku and Funabashi areas. Matsudo and Ichikawa were utterly destroyed.

　　　　Collapsed buildings: 14,241 structures and 1,303 buildings

Jishin Nendaiki — End

【Left folio】

Treading my way through the withered moors, I had traveled down to the East and was lodging in the Eastern Capital (Edo) for some five or seven days — perhaps due to the unsettled warm and cold weather — when the Great Kantō Earthquake struck. It was on the second day of the tenth month of the second year of Ansei (the year of the Wood Hare, 1855), past the Hour of the Boar at night, when the shaking suddenly began. Damage to the city was immense, and fires broke out in various places, filling one with a truly extraordinary sense of dread. People slept in the open streets, waiting for dawn, and it was only on the morning of the third day, at the Hour of the Dragon, when the fires in various locations had finally been brought under control, that relief was at last felt. Afterward, I investigated the damaged and burned areas with the intention of making it into a tale to bring home as a souvenir. Seeing the devastation moved me deeply; hearing about it filled me with astonishment. Upon returning to my hometown, friends from near and far first rejoiced at my safety and asked about what had happened — and there was simply not enough time to tell the whole story in person. Therefore, I have had these detailed accounts engraved (in woodblock print), and have also researched great earthquakes from ancient times, from the Age of the Gods onward, with the intention of making it available for the perusal of my close friends.

　　　　　　Composed by the Master of Bunreidō
