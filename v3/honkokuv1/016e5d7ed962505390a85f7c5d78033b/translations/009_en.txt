As early as the third day of the eighth month of Kanmon the tenth year (1670), wind and rain were exceedingly fierce, shaking both mountains and sea; great waves rolled up toward the heavens, and the houses of the common people in Naniwa (Osaka) were repeatedly destroyed, and it is said that many people perished. (This is now one hundred and eighty-five years ago as of the present year [1854].) Thereafter, on the fourth day of the tenth month of Hōei the fourth year (1707), a great earthquake gave rise to a tsunami, and many people of Naniwa are said to have died. (This is now one hundred and forty-eight years ago as of the present year.)

Upon reflection, the tsunami of Kanmon must surely have overflowed into the streets of the city and collapsed many dwellings. However, during the Jōkyō era (1684–1688), Kawamura Zuiken applied his expertise to the geography of the Aji waterway (*Aji Suidō*), dredged the lower reaches of the channels branching off from the Ōkawa River, and put an end to the affliction of reverse flooding. He named the resulting waterway the "Ajikawa" — so called on account of the reading (*on*) of the name "Aji (安治)." As a result, many new rice paddies were opened up, and the common people felt a great sense of relief and security. The earth and sand excavated at that time was piled up to form a hill, which was built to protect the mouth of the river. This was named "Namiyokeyama" (Wave-Break Hill; also commonly called "Zuiken-yama"). The name must signify the place where high waves are blocked and warded off during floods.

The tsunami of Hōei occurred after the development of the Ajikawa, and while the coastal shores and riverbanks were not entirely free from harm, it can be readily inferred that the damage was far less severe than at the time of the Kanmon tsunami. Today, furthermore, additional fields have been opened beyond the site of Namiyokeyama, and there is a small island along the coast called Mejirushiyama (Landmark Island), so that compared with the days of Hōei, the land now extends ever further out to sea.
