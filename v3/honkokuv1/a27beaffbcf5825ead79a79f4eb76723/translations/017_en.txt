The earthquake was no longer as it had been at first, but the ground rumbled with a low, rolling sound (*doro-doro*) and trembled several times over the course of a short while. It is said that from the initial shock to the morning of the third day, there were over one hundred and twenty tremors in all. And so, on that night, household by household, people had their stable hands (*batei*) light lanterns for them as they passed the night out in the great road.

Come the morning of the third day, the sky had cleared entirely, and the sunlight shone bright and brilliant. Feeling that even so, living out in the great road looked unsightly, people returned one by one into their homes, and at last managed, however modestly, to patch up some of the damage. Even on this day the tremors did not cease; roughly seven to eight, and even up to ten times per *hitotoki* (a unit of approximately two hours). From the hour of the seventh bell (*nanatsu-doki*, around 4 p.m.) that evening as well, people resolved once again to spend the night in the great road. They laid out tatami and set up folding screens, and fashioned rain covers overhead. In the narrower streets, ropes were strung from one side to the other and bamboo poles laid across, with reed mats (*mushiro*) or rain capes (*kappa*) spread over them as cover. Everyone kept watch through the night just as they had the night before.

Moreover, those gripped by the most intense fear, declaring it dangerous to remain within the city, made their way to the fields of Higashiyama, or to the banks of the Kamo River, or set up camp in the western fields, carrying out their cooking utensils and taking refuge there—and the numbers of such people were enormous. This night, it rained briefly toward dawn, but by morning the sky had cleared. The tremors had grown somewhat calmer, yet they still came six or seven times per *hitotoki*.
