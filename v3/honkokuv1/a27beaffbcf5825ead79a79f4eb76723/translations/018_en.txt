Even so, everyone huddled close together near the edges of their doorways and verandas, ready to flee out into the great road should the need arise. From this point, the number of tremors day by day grew fewer, and by around the fourteenth or fifteenth day, they had diminished to between fifteen or sixteen and up to twenty times per day and night.

Now, there was not a single house in Kyoto, great or small, that had escaped damage, and though people sought urgently to make repairs, carpenters and plasterers, to say nothing of hired day laborers and assistants, could not possibly be allocated to every household. No matter how many times people sent for them, workers did not easily come. And even when they did come occasionally, if they came one day they would not return for two; if a job took two days, they would rest for five. And so repairs could not be carried out to any satisfactory degree. People made do by temporarily propping things up with bamboo and timber, or in some cases tying things together with rope. In many places where no workers came at all, there was no choice but to leave things as they were.

Among these, there were storehouses (*kura*) that had at first appeared perfectly sound—ones their owners had boasted of proudly to others—that, having been damaged little by little from the daily tremors, collapsed all at once without warning, and the sound of their falling threw the surrounding neighborhood into alarm. After this, on both the seventeenth and eighteenth days, there were heavy rains.
