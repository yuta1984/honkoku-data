By the following morning, past the Hour of the Rabbit (approximately 10 a.m.), all fires were completely extinguished, and the people were finally able to feel a sense of relief.

The foregoing is a general account of the calamity within the city (go-funai). Beyond the city, to the north, along the Ōshū Kaidō highway, the shaking was strong at Senju post-town, diminishing from there toward Sōka and Koshigaya, and crossing at Kurihashi, with Koga and Utsunomiya marking the limit of its reach.

○ To the west, along the Kōshū Kaidō highway, below Naitō-Shinjuku the shaking was extremely mild.

○ To the south, along the Tōkaidō highway, Shinagawa post-town experienced strong shaking; Kawasaki post-town experienced mild shaking; Kanagawa post-town was extremely strongly affected, with many collapsed buildings. The tremors reached as far as Odawara.

○ To the east, the areas of Gyōtoku and Funabashi were strongly shaken, with many collapsed buildings and deaths. The Narita area was largely spared.

○ Along the Mito Kaidō highway, the areas of Shinjuku-machi and Matsudo also had many collapsed buildings; the area around Kogane was strongly affected, with the reach extending as far as Tsuchiura in Hitachi Province.

○ To the northwest, along the Nakasendō highway, the limit extended from Itabashi through Kōnosu, the area around Kumagaya, and to Honjō post-town, beyond which the shaking was said to be very mild.

Thus, the affected area extended approximately thirty to forty ri (roughly 120–160 km) in all directions, with the city at its very center.

○ At the time of the earthquake, a flash of light like lightning appeared in the eastern sky, and disappeared after a short while. This was likely the subterranean vital energy (yōki) surging up from deep within the earth and bursting forth all at once. On the eighteenth of the same month, rain began falling around midday, and as night fell the wind and rain grew violent; around midnight, the sound of thunder was heard three or four times and lightning flashed. Perhaps this too was the earth's inner energy releasing itself.

○ During that time, the number of those crushed beneath beams or burned to death was beyond counting. Some were dug out from collapsed buildings and emerged unharmed; others perished. In the areas consumed by the spreading fire in particular, many people—still pinned beneath beams and not yet dead—were burned alive in great numbers.

For the conveyance of the dead to burial grounds, people rushed frantically in all directions; bodies were loaded—how many could not be said—onto nagamochi transport chests and carrying frames, while even more were placed on daihachi-guruma (large-wheeled handcarts) and pulled through the streets. Truly, though one might call this an unprecedented calamity and act of heaven, it was a source of tears both to witness and to describe—a scene utterly beyond words.
