It is also said that where arsenic stone (*砒石*) is found, hot springs are likewise present. I have not yet been able to ascertain whether this is true or not. In recent times, Kagawa Taichū of the capital (Kyoto) has firmly based himself upon the theory of Ine Wakamizu, holding that within the earth there are two veins—of water and of fire—and that where these two veins meet and converge, hot springs are formed. This theory is indeed outstanding. However, in the course of setting forth his argument, he measures everything by a single, partial viewpoint and is not sparing in his own self-praise; such excess is by no means rare. How could it be that no one exists who might refute what is incorrect therein and return these matters to the truly proper and correct path?

Hara Kōyō of Heian (Kyoto) once composed a work in one volume entitled *Onsen Shōgen* ("Brief Words on Hot Springs"). His discernment is firm and settled; his argumentation is precise and penetrating. He refutes Taichū's careless assertions and returns the discussion to what is correct and proper. Moreover, he has at once dispelled the doubts that I had long harbored—is this not most gratifying? Furthermore, this work employs the national script (*kana*) for the convenience of rural farmers and village elders in reading it aloud, and may truly be called an act of compassionate, grandmotherly solicitude (*bashin*).
