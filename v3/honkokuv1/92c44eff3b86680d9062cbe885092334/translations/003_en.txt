(Continued from previous page) …[Keichu] asked me to write a preface. I was occupied with both public and private affairs (and had not yet found the leisure to take up my brush). Before I knew it, Keichu had fallen ill and passed away. Recently, Keichu's son Hakkei, acting on his late father's dying instructions, intending to have this work woodblock-printed, once again came to request a few words from me. I had formerly heard from Keichu that Shikōyō also had a work called "Keidai Zuihitsu" (Miscellaneous Notes from the Keidai Study) and several volumes of poetry and prose collections, which were to be carved and transmitted to the world. Alas, how countless are those in this world who discard and neglect the manuscripts left by their forebears, leaving them merely to be devoured by rats and bookworms! Keichu succeeded in bringing his father's writings to light, and Hakkei has further carried on his father's will — how could one not praise and admire this? I myself never had the opportunity to meet Shikōyō in person, yet this book is assuredly a fragment of his broader scholarship. Reading it now, I can discern the depth and breadth of his learning. And seeing his son and his grandson, I can well imagine what manner of cultivated man he must have been. Now, there exists the theory of "water-fire wandering seeds" (水火游子六) concerning subterranean veins, which originally derives from Western learning, and yet the words of Ine Jakusui of our imperial realm accord with this theory as though by chance.
