As his learning grew vastly rich, he was grieved that scholars of later generations each established their own schools of learning, diverging from the teachings of Confucius. Exhausting his refinement and dedicating his full spirit to the task, he composed the work *Hishu Tsumumono* ["Refutation of Non-Zhu Xi matters"]. Although some questioned whether this work might itself be aligned with the school of Fuji (Itō Jinsai), he subsequently composed *Gitō* ["Doubts about Tōgai/Fujiwara"]. That he did not simply comply with the calls for his censure or exile was, in all likelihood, because he could not bring himself to turn against his former teacher. This alone is sufficient to demonstrate that he was a man of upright character.

He ultimately established his own independent school, calling his teaching "the learning of Confucius." After his death, his friend AkutaEnshō composed a tomb inscription, warmly praising his virtue, his broad learning, and his skill in guiding and teaching others, writing: "The reputation of Karatsu for producing men of talent is owed to his efforts."

Now, Tōgai was the foremost Confucian scholar of his age; whatever he praised must have been based on what he had personally tested and verified. Akuta, too, was a Confucian of renown, and his words were not necessarily empty. Moreover, the written works that survive today and are stored in Keichū's household are very numerous indeed. At one time, drawing upon various topics in natural history (*hakubutsu*), he composed one volume of *Onsen Shōgen* ("Minor Discourse on Hot Springs").
