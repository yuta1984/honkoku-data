The water accumulated deep into the valleys, extending for approximately eight or nine ri. Officials were dispatched by the domain lord, and relief measures were carried out. Strict orders were further issued to the villages of Kawanakajima, and in preparation for emergency flood prevention, large embankments were constructed using stone-filled straw bales (ishidawara). The residents of the various households each went to nearby locations — to the west, the mountains of Komatsubara and Okada, and to the east, the mountains of Saijō and Hoshina — and set up shelters in the fields and fields. They sent the elderly ahead, took their wives and children with them, carried rice, grain, miso, and other provisions, left two or three watchmen in each village, and had rafts assembled and kept ready. Due to this upheaval, the relay transport system at the post stations was also disrupted; from Yashiro post station all the way to Sekikawa post station in Echigo Province, the luggage of samurai households and other goods were carried by porters along the road on the eastern bank of the river. Meanwhile, at the Koichi village ferry crossing — the point where the Sai River flows out toward the lowlands — the water had disappeared and a small hill had formed in the middle of the river, which would surge against things during flood surges, causing hardship for the surrounding villages. To address this, thousands of laborers turned out and erected triple-layered large embankments of stone-filled bales to serve as flood barriers. Furthermore, signal fire operators (noroshi) were ordered to send up smoke signals from the mountains above the water-filled valleys; these were received from mountain to mountain, and the news was immediately communicated to the villages in the lowlands. By means of these signals, the watchmen remaining in the villages, as well as travelers passing through, were warned to flee toward higher ground in the mountains. In addition, the lord of Matsushiro provided dozens of rescue boats, which were moored and kept ready at the nearby villages. Moreover, large cauldrons were set up at several locations and food was cooked and distributed daily; thousands of laborers were provided with meals and other generous forms of relief — all of which was a matter of profound gratitude.
