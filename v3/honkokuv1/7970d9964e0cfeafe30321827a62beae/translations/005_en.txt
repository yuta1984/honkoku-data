Everyone fled to the fields and stood there in a daze. From nearby villages, relatives and kin came rushing to help, digging out those still calling out from beneath collapsed houses, assisting one another. They also tried to fight the fires, but were utterly powerless against them, and the flames only grew fiercer. The wind blew in from the direction of Hitsuji-Saru (southwest), and the main hall enshrining the sacred image appeared to be in imminent danger. At that moment, the figures of numerous people appeared on the roof of the main hall and on the roof of the Sanmon gate, rushing about in all directions to prevent the spread of flying embers. The main hall was neither shaken down by the earthquake nor destroyed by fire. When people looked up at the roof at daybreak, not a single person was to be seen among the many who had been there before — truly a mysterious occurrence.

Lord Matsushiro dispatched a large contingent of firefighters to assist, and from the encampment at Rokugawa, forces were also sent down (as described below), and the advancing front of the fire was brought under control. Those who survived that night all lay out in the open fields, exposed to rain and dew, while the earthquakes continued unabated and the fires burned fiercely for two full days and nights — a hardship and sorrow truly beyond words.

Regarding the temple precincts: all the halls within the precinct of the Daihongan Lady Abbess were lost; all twenty-one in (sub-temple residences for senior monks), fifteen bō of chūshū (mid-ranking monks' quarters), and ten妻戸 bō were burned to the ground. The Niōmon gate, the stalls and souvenir shops in the hall garden, teahouses, mat-sided sideshow booths and the like — more than three chō of them — were all burned without exception. The Daikanjin, the administrative temple, survived partially collapsed. The scripture repository (kyōzō), the bell tower hall (shōrōdō), and the Manzen-dō remained.
