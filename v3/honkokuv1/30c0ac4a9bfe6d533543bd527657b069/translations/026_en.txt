(Continued from previous page) ...when [the Sun] descends below the horizon, its light gradually diminishes. This is what is called "chen-hun" (晨昏, the twilight periods of dawn and dusk). The light of dawn and dusk is not equal across the four seasons. Likewise, the times at which starlight becomes visible are naturally unequal. The reason for this is that the depth to which the Sun descends below the horizon along the ecliptic varies—sometimes ten degrees, sometimes fifteen degrees, reaching as much as thirty degrees—and is originally unequal. Moreover, when a star is to the south of the ecliptic, its angular distance [from the Sun] is necessarily several degrees greater; when it is to the north of the ecliptic, its angular distance is necessarily several degrees less. Therefore the limiting value cannot be treated as fixed.

In general, the three planets Saturn, Jupiter, and Mars move more slowly than the Sun and travel behind it; they therefore set in the evening (夕伏) and appear in the morning (晨見). The two planets Venus and Water Star (Mercury) revolve eastward in accordance with the heavens and move more quickly than the Sun, traveling ahead of it; they therefore become invisible in the morning (晨伏) and appear in the evening (夕見). When in retrograde motion, this is reversed, and when they encounter (come into conjunction with) the Sun, they likewise set in the evening and appear in the morning.

The Moon's movement is even faster than the Sun's, [so it too] becomes invisible in the morning and appears in the evening. Regarding the latitudinal motion of Venus (金星), it does not reach eight degrees. When [Venus] is in retrograde and comes into conjunction with the Sun in the two mansions of Shouxing [interlinear note: Shen (辰) Palace/Sign] and Dahuo [interlinear note: Mao (卯) Palace/Sign], and its latitude is moreover over seven degrees to the north, even when in conjunction with the Sun, its light does not disappear (伏). It can be seen at both morning and evening.

Mercury's latitude is only a little over four degrees. If its latitude is directed southward and it comes into conjunction with the Sun in Shouxing (辰 Palace), after this departure [from the Sun], it will certainly not be visible in the evening. If it comes into conjunction with the Sun in Jianglou [interlinear note: Xu (戌) Palace/Sign], after this departure, it will certainly not be visible in the morning. These are the differences in the disappearances and appearances (伏見) of the five planets.

To take Saturn as an example: it moves in retrograde (rightward/westward) and completes one circuit of the heavens in a little less than thirty years. Its evening invisibility (夕伏) and conjunction invisibility (合伏) are each twenty days and forty ke [units of time], during which it travels two degrees and forty fen each. [Interlinear note: For these five planets, one degree is calculated using one hundred fen (分).] It emerges in the east at dawn (晨出); combining its fast and slow phases, it travels for eighty-six days, covering seven degrees and sixty-five fen. It then stations (留) for thirty days. Its morning retrograde (晨退) and evening retrograde (夕退) are each fifty-two days, sixty-four ke, and fifty-eight fen, during which it retrogrades three degrees, sixty-two fen, and fifty-four and a half miao (arc-seconds) each. It then stations again for thirty days. Slow and fast phases combined, it travels eighty-six days, covering seven degrees and sixty-five fen. Then it again undergoes evening invisibility and upon reappearing, one synodic cycle (一周) is complete. Roughly every sixty years [the pattern] repeats, but the exact days and times are not identical. [Interlinear note: After sixty years, the pattern of fast, slow, invisible, and retrograde phases is the same, but the position in the zodiacal signs and the exact times differ. Roughly speaking, it is sufficient to advance [the reckoning] by about three shi (時, double-hours).]
