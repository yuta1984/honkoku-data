The light of the twilight is not equal across the four seasons, and the time [of appearance] of starlight is likewise naturally unequal. The reason for this is that the angle at which the Sun descends below the horizon along the ecliptic varies—sometimes ten degrees, sometimes fifteen degrees, up to as much as thirty degrees—and is inherently unequal. Furthermore, when a star is to the south of the ecliptic, its angular distance [from the Sun] is necessarily greater by several degrees; when it is to the north of the ecliptic, its distance is necessarily less by several degrees. This limit cannot be rigidly fixed.

In general, the three planets Saturn, Jupiter, and Mars move more slowly than the Sun and travel behind the Sun; hence they experience evening invisibility (夕伏, heliacal setting) and morning appearance (晨見, heliacal rising). The two planets Venus and Mercury revolve eastward in accordance with heaven, and move faster than the Sun, traveling ahead of it; hence they experience morning invisibility (晨伏) and evening appearance (夕見, evening visibility). During retrograde motion, the reverse is true. When [Venus and Mercury] meet (conjoin) with the Sun, they also experience evening invisibility and morning appearance.

The Moon moves even faster than the Sun, [and thus] experiences morning invisibility and evening appearance. As for the latitude of Venus, it does not reach eight degrees. When [Venus] undergoes retrograde motion and conjoins with the Sun in the two mansions of Shouxing (寿星, the Virgo mansion / Chen-gong 辰宮) and Dahuo (大火, the Scorpio mansion / Mao-gong 卯宮), and its latitude is furthermore more than seven degrees north, even though [Venus] is in conjunction with the Sun, its light does not become invisible—it can be seen in both morning and evening.

The latitude of Mercury is only about four degrees. If its latitude is directed southward and it conjoins with the Sun in Shouxing (辰宮, the Chen mansion), after separating from [the Sun] thereafter, it will necessarily not be visible in the evening. If it conjoins with the Sun in Jianglou (降婁, 戌宮, the Xu/Aries mansion), after separating thereafter, it will necessarily not be visible in the morning. These are the differences in the invisibility and visibility of the five planets.

Taking Saturn as an example: it makes one complete revolution of heaven in slightly less than thirty years [moving] to the right (westward/retrograde in overall direction). Each of its evening invisibility (夕伏) and conjunction invisibility (合伏) periods lasts twenty days and forty [units of time], during which it travels two degrees and forty minutes. [Interlinear note: For these five planets, one degree is calculated in units of one hundred minutes.] [Saturn] then rises in the east at dawn; with fast and slow [motion] combined, [it travels] for eighty-six days, covering seven degrees and sixty-five minutes. It then remains stationary for thirty days. Morning retrograde and evening retrograde each last fifty-two days, sixty-four [units of time], and fifty-eight minutes, during each of which it retrogrades three degrees, sixty-two minutes, and fifty-four and a half arcseconds. It is again stationary for thirty days. Slow and fast [motion] combined [it travels] for eighty-six days, covering seven degrees and sixty-five minutes. Then it undergoes evening invisibility again, and [the cycle from one] appearance to the next constitutes one complete cycle (synodic period).

Approximately every sixty years [the cycle] coincides, though the exact days and times do not coincide. [Interlinear note: After sixty years, the slow, fast, invisible, and retrograde [phases] coincide, but the mansion [it is in] is not the same, nor are the exact times. Approximately, one should expect it to be about three time-units (時) earlier.]
