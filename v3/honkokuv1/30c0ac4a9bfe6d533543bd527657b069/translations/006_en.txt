…and revolves ceaselessly. As for the roundness of the Earth, there is no point upon it that is not the centre.

[Interlinear note: Master Jie (揭子, i.e., the author Jie Xuan) states: "The emptiness of Heaven is not truly empty. What is called 'empty' means that qi fills and pervades it completely, with no gaps whatsoever. It is like when you draw water with a bottle and stop up one of its holes — the water will not enter, because qi seals the interior. Qi is none other than Heaven itself. Likewise, the solidity of the Earth is not truly solid. Qi enters and exits through it; even though there is soil and rock, the hard portions are entirely on the outer surface. Press inward, and it becomes hollow and soft. Now, within Heaven there is qi; hence it sometimes condenses to form such stars as Chānsāng (攙搶), comets (彗), and guest stars (孛), and it refracts to produce such phenomena as halos (暈), sun-dogs (珥), and coronae (霱珮). Within the Earth there are cavities; hence the tides breathe in and out, transforming into springs and fountainheads; deep mountains and great valleys exhale clouds and mist; when yin is suppressed and yang goes awry, this erupts as earthquakes and tremors. Thus Master You (游子) says: 'The Earth, too, is Heaven.'"]

One need only point to the pole star to distinguish east, west, south, and north, and observe the Sun to determine the seasons of cold and warmth and the alternation of day and night. Therefore, depending on where one lives, the manner in which the Sun shines differs. For those living directly under the equator — within the belt of land twenty-three and a half degrees to the north and south — at the spring and autumn equinoxes the Sun passes directly through the zenith, casting no shadow at midday. After the spring equinox the shadow falls to the south; after the autumn equinox the shadow falls to the north. This zone is called the Warm Zone (Dantai / 煖帯).

Beyond twenty-three and a half degrees north and south, up to the lands at sixty-six and a half degrees, the Sun never passes through the zenith of these regions, yet it is neither especially close nor especially distant. These two northern and southern circles constitute the Temperate Zone (Seitai / 正帯), which is neither excessively cold nor excessively hot.

[Interlinear note: This zone is mild and temperate, and it is a place where sages and worthies are born in abundance. China's location, ranging from eighteen degrees north of the equator to forty-two degrees north, falls precisely within this zone.]

To the north and south, the areas from sixty-six and a half degrees to each respective pole constitute the Cold Zone (Reitai / 冷帯). On some days the Sun circles about these lands and is perpetually visible; on other days it circles about but remains perpetually hidden. These periods of concealment and visibility may last for several months, or may extend to half a year.

[Interlinear note: Master Jie states: "Below the two poles, the celestial wheel revolves horizontally, making half a year into day and half a year into night. The land there is extremely cold, and its people endure the cold."]

This is the general outline of the Five Zones. From this one may deduce that for those who are separated from the equator to the north and south, their climates must be the inverse of one another. For example, when the Sun is in Xīngjì (星紀, the Stellar Record)

[Interlinear note: i.e., the Ox Palace (丑宮, Capricorn)]

it becomes the winter solstice for those facing north, while for those facing south it becomes the summer solstice. All the seasonal nodes follow this same pattern without exception.

Furthermore, one may deduce from this that since the terrestrial globe is where human beings reside, one divides the four directions by means of the zenith. It can also be demarcated into three hundred and sixty degrees to correspond to the motions of the heavens. East and west constitute the lines of longitude (kei / 経), measured by the ecliptic; north and south constitute the lines of latitude (i / 緯), fixed by the meridian. If one wishes to measure north and south, the two poles serve as its endpoints.

[Interlinear note: You Xiong (游熊) states: "Western learning measures the terrestrial globe and finds its circumference to be ninety thousand li. For example, if one travels two hundred and fifty li northward, the pole star rises by one degree as measured. At twenty-two thousand five hundred li, it will have risen by ninety degrees" (i.e., one reaches the pole).]
