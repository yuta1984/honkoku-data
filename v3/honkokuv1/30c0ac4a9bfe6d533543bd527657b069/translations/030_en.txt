**Precession of the Equinoxes (continued)**

[Question, continued:] ...one degree of difference. Sui dynasty's Liu Zhuo (劉焯) took the mean of the two schools' values and set the rate at one degree per seventy-five years. Liu Xuan (劉炫) set it at one degree per forty-five years. Liang dynasty's Yu Kuang (虞鄺) set it at one degree per one hundred and eighty-six years. Tang dynasty monk Yixing (一行) composed the *Dayan Calendar* (大衍暦) and set it at one degree per eighty-three years. The Song dynasty *Daming Calendar* (大明暦) set it at one degree per forty years. The *Tongtian Calendar* (統天暦) set it at one degree per sixty-seven years. The Yuan dynasty *Shoushi Calendar* (授時暦) set it at one degree per sixty-six years. The Ming dynasty *Datong Calendar* (大統暦) set it at one degree per seventy-eight years. During the Wanli reign period, Li Xitai [interlinear note: personal name Mǎdòu (瑪竇); a person from the Western Regions] arrived [in China] and accordingly set the rate at approximately one degree per sixty-eight years and eight months, with the Sun falling short of a full circuit by one fen and fifty seconds each year. Xiong Liangru (熊良孺) set it at one degree per sixty-seven years. Fang Mizhi (方密之) set it at one degree per sixty-some years.

The theories of precession are thus numerous and chaotic — upon what basis can one determine which to rely on? How can one know the true amount of the precession? Does precession have a fixed and constant rule? Or does the difference ever generate further difference, making it impossible to calculate and measure precisely? Please explain this in detail."

[Answer:] It was said: "Precession of the equinoxes arises from the motions of Heaven and the Sun's annual position (*rìchán*, 日躔). Over the course of one year, the Sun travels through the full circuit of the heavens, and before it has reached the remaining fractional position of the stars [i.e., the previous year's winter solstice point], the Sun has already arrived [at the solstice]. This is because the stars have shifted slightly eastward: it *appears* as though the Sun has retreated, but in reality it is the stars that have advanced. Thus each year, [the Sun's solstice position] consistently shifts by a certain number of fen and seconds to the east, passing beyond its original boundary — this is what is called 'the difference' (差).

[Interlinear note: You Yan (游燕) said: "When ancient and modern scholars speak of [the Sun] 'not reaching the remainder,' what this means is that when the Sun treads upon its boundary [the winter solstice point], the stars have already moved past it. Thus what is described as the stars 'passing beyond' is expressed as the Sun 'not reaching.' It is not that the Sun is incapable of reaching the stellar degree."]

Tracing back to calculate the winter solstice of Emperor Yao's year of *jiǎchén* (甲辰): at dusk, the Pleiades (昴, Mao) were culminating [on the meridian], and the Sun's position (*rìchán*) was at the seventh degree of the Xu lunar mansion (虚宿). [Interlinear note: Tang Dao (湯道) traced back further to Emperor Ku's (帝嚳) year of *jiǎzǐ* (甲子), setting the winter solstice Sun at the sixth degree of Xu.]

During the Xia dynasty, in the year *yǐwèi* (乙未) of [King] Bujiang (不降), at the winter solstice, the Sun had retreated to the eleventh degree of the Nü lunar mansion (女宿). [Interlinear note: "Sun retreating" means that as the stars advance, the Sun is regarded as retreating.]

During the Shang (Yin) dynasty, in the year *bǐngyín* (丙寅) of [King] Wuyi (武乙), at the winter solstice, the Sun had retreated to the seventh degree of the Niu lunar mansion (牛宿). [Interlinear note: "Retreat" is precisely what is meant by "difference" (差). The Sun retreats gradually year by year; but by taking this as a standard from which to begin calculating, the computation does not become overly complicated.]

During the Zhou dynasty, in the year *dīnghài* (丁亥) of King Jian (簡王), at the winter solstice, the Sun had retreated to the twenty-third degree of the Dou lunar mansion (斗宿).
