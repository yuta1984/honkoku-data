(Continued from previous page) ...when [the lunar path] is to the north of a given latitude: when it is within the yin calendar [inside the ecliptic], the Moon is seen early; when it is within the yang calendar [outside the ecliptic], it is seen late. Beyond this, there are also various other factors: the differing elevations of the North Pole above the horizon, the effect of twilight (朦朧, dim light), and atmospheric refraction (気差), among others. This is why it is difficult to make the speed of the Moon's appearance uniform.

Nevertheless, the Moon is also a kind of star—indeed, smaller than the other stars. Because it is situated at a low elevation relative to the celestial pole, it appears extremely close when people look up at it. For this reason, the striking differences between the new moon (朔), full moon (望), half moon (弦), and the last day of the month (晦)—as well as between a lunar eclipse and no eclipse—become apparent.

The two planets Venus and Mercury also share the same degree (orbit) as the Sun, so they constantly appear as dark spots (black points) within the solar disk. However, because the celestial sphere in which these stars are located is high, and they are extremely far away when people look up at them, these occurrences are not called "eclipses." If the Moon's disk were to pass beneath these two stars, it would also be able to obscure their light. The reason that such an occultation does not appear as prominent as a solar eclipse is that the celestial sphere of these two stars is high, their light is faint, and their apparent size is small—matters that people tend to overlook.

Therefore, the Moon governs exclusively the tides (潮汐) and has no connection to the weather and seasons (気候). This is inferred from the fact that the Moon is close to the Earth and that the light and shadow it casts illuminate the Earth.

　Question: The Sun travels one degree per day. People observe it day by day in this manner and have never thought of it as advancing or retreating.
