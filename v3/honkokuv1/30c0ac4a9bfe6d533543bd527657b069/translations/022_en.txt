(Continued from previous page) ...when (the white way) lies to the north of its latitudinal degree: if it is within the yin calendar (陰暦, inside the ecliptic), the Moon is seen quickly; if it is within the yang calendar (陽暦, outside the ecliptic), the Moon is seen slowly. Beyond these factors, there are also differences due to the elevation of the celestial north pole above the horizon (北極出地), as well as various discrepancies such as the twilight shadow (朦朧たる影) and atmospheric refraction (気差). For all these reasons, it is difficult to make the timing of the Moon's first appearance uniform.

In any case, the Moon is also a kind of star. Compared to the (other) stars it is even smaller. However, because (the Moon) is low relative to the celestial pole, it is exceedingly close to us who gaze up at it. Therefore, the distinctions of new moon, full moon, quarter moon, and last day of the month (朔・望・弦・晦) appear clearly, as do the notable differences between eclipse and non-eclipse.

The two planets Venus and Mercury also share the same degree (orbit) as the Sun, so there are always dark spots on the solar disk (indicating their transits). However, because these stars are positioned high up and are exceedingly far from the people who gaze upward at them, this is not called an "eclipse." If the lunar disk at times passes beneath these two stars (Venus and Mercury), it can also obscure their light. The reason why (this occultation) does not appear as conspicuously as a solar eclipse is that the heavens of these two stars are high, their light is faint, and their bodies (apparent sizes) are small—and thus people overlook them. Therefore, the Moon governs only the tides and has no involvement with the weather and seasons. This is inferred from the fact that the Moon is close to the Earth, and its reflected light illuminates the Earth, thereby driving (the tides).

　　The Rightward Motion (Westward Movement) of the Sun and Moon

　Question: The Sun travels one degree per day. When people observe it day by day, this appears to be the case, and it does not normally appear to advance or retreat (visibly). The Moon travels more than thirteen degrees per day. When people observe it, it appears to fall behind by one day compared to the previous day. Yet it is said to move swiftly. Why is this?

Answer: Regarding the slow and fast movements of the various heavens (諸天), and the intricate degrees of the Seven Luminaries (七曜, the Sun, Moon, and five planets), there are still points that have not been fully clarified. However, as for the movements of the Sun and Moon, the Confucian scholars of the Song dynasty discussed this in great detail. Zhuzi (朱子, Zhu Xi) says: The movement of Heaven is exceedingly vigorous, and in one day and one night it travels three hundred and sixty-five and one-quarter degrees of the full celestial circuit. [Interlinear note: If one degree is taken as one hundred parts, then twenty-five parts are obtained.] Furthermore, it advances beyond one additional degree. The speed of the Sun's movement is second only to Heaven. In one day and one night, it travels exactly three hundred and sixty-five degrees of the full celestial circuit—precisely. Compared to Heaven's advance of one degree, the Sun thus falls back one degree.
