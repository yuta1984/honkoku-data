In general, when the motion of the Sun and Moon reaches the two crossings (正交 and 中交) and they are at the same degree, an eclipse occurs. When they are at the same degree, there is an eclipse. However, an eclipse must necessarily occur within the eclipse limit (食限). If the crossing falls within the limit, there is an eclipse; if the crossing falls outside the limit, there is no eclipse. The eclipse limit for solar eclipses and that for lunar eclipses also differ. Therefore, in calculating lunar eclipses: if five months have passed (since the last eclipse), another eclipse can occur; if seven months have passed, another eclipse cannot occur. For solar eclipses, even after five months, another eclipse can occur, and even after seven months, another eclipse can also occur. This is because the eclipse limits are each different. As for the eclipse magnitude (食分), it is determined from the angular separation (距度)—that is, the degree of separation between the centers of the two orbital paths. In the case of a lunar eclipse, the closer the center of the Moon (太陰) is to the center of the Earth's shadow, the greater the eclipse magnitude.

[The *Xin Yu* (新語) says: When fire illuminates an object, a black shadow is necessarily cast in the opposite direction. When the Earth is positioned in the middle: if the Sun shines from the east, the black shadow is cast to the west in opposition; if the Sun shines from below, the black shadow is cast upward in opposition. When an object passes through the black shadow, it darkens and cannot be seen. Therefore, when the Sun and Moon face each other directly and the Earth is interposed between them, the shadow is cast and the Moon darkens. That the eclipse has varying degrees of depth is because the Moon's orbit has variations in elevation (浮沈, literally "floating and sinking").]

In the case of a solar eclipse, the calculation is made using the apparent angular separation (視度) between the two centers of the Sun and Moon. The degree of distance is not based on the actual (geometric) distance, but is taken as the standard based on what is reached by the human eye (i.e., the apparent, parallax-affected position). This is precisely why the eclipse magnitude of a lunar eclipse is the same for all people under heaven, while the eclipse magnitude of a solar eclipse differs in every direction—east, west, south, and north—according to the position of the observer.

When the lunar path (白道) curves for half a circuit toward the South Pole, and the (crossing) is at times located at the zenith and within the ecliptic, this is called the "Yin Calendar" (陰暦). When the lunar path curves for half a circuit toward the North Pole, and is then located outside the ecliptic, this is called the "Yang Calendar" (陽暦). Therefore, the eclipse limit for solar eclipses at any given location cannot be fixed uniformly.

For example, taking the capital city (京師, Beijing), where the North Pole (Polaris) rises forty degrees above the horizon (i.e., latitude 40°N): a rough calculation gives eight degrees for the Yang Calendar and twenty-one degrees for the Yin Calendar. This means that when the Sun and Moon conjoin (合朔), if (the conjunction) is within eight degrees of the two crossings in the Yang Calendar, or within twenty-one degrees of the two crossings in the Yin Calendar, a solar eclipse will necessarily be visible at that location. Beyond these limits, it will not be seen. From the northern (example), the south can likewise be calculated; in every case, the presence or absence of an eclipse is determined by the varying distances and magnitudes.

The reason, however, that the eclipse limit degrees differ between the Yin Calendar and the Yang Calendar lies in the apparent disk of the Moon (月輪). The lunar disk is the closest to the Earth (of the celestial bodies), and is also smaller than the Earth. The place from which a person observes it is on the Earth's surface, not at the Earth's center (地心).
