...called "central crossing" (中交). Whenever the Sun and Moon in their motion reach the two crossing points (Dragon's Head and Dragon's Tail) and arrive at the same degree, an eclipse occurs. When they reach the same degree, an eclipse occurs; and an eclipse must necessarily fall within the eclipse limit. If the crossing falls within the limit, an eclipse occurs; if the crossing falls outside the limit, no eclipse occurs. The limit for solar eclipses and the limit for lunar eclipses also differ. Therefore, in calculating lunar eclipses, an eclipse can recur after five months have passed, but cannot recur after seven months have passed. For solar eclipses, an eclipse can recur after five months, and can also recur after seven months. This is because the respective limits each differ.

As for the eclipse magnitude (食分), it is calculated by the distance in degrees (距度)—that is, the degrees by which the centers of the two bodies (両周) are separated from each other. In the case of a lunar eclipse, the closer the center of the Moon (太陰) actually is to the center of the Earth's shadow, the greater the eclipse magnitude becomes. [The *Xinyu* (新語) says: When fire illuminates an object, a dark shadow necessarily forms in the directly opposite direction. When the Earth is positioned at the center, if the Sun illuminates from the east, the dark shadow opposes from the west; if the Sun illuminates from below, the dark shadow opposes from above. When an object passes through the dark shadow, it immediately darkens and becomes invisible. Therefore, when the Sun and Moon directly face each other and the Earth is interposed between them, the shadow is cast and the Moon turns dark. That there are degrees of shallowness and depth in an eclipse is because the Moon's path has variations in height (浮沈).]

In the case of a solar eclipse, the mutual distance between the centers of the Sun and Moon is calculated in terms of apparent degrees (視度). Its nearness or farness does not depend on the actual distance (実度) but is based on the extent reached by the human eye's line of sight as its standard. This is precisely why the eclipse magnitude of a lunar eclipse is the same for all people under heaven, whereas the eclipse magnitude of a solar eclipse varies according to the observer's position, differing in each of the four directions—east, west, south, and north.

For example, during the half-circuit in which the lunar path (白道) is directed toward the South Pole, there are times when it lies between the zenith and the ecliptic; this is called the "Yin Calendar" (陰暦). During the half-circuit in which the lunar path is directed toward the North Pole, it lies outside the ecliptic; this is called the "Yang Calendar" (陽暦). Therefore, the solar eclipse limit for locations below these paths cannot be fixed uniformly.

For example, in the case of the capital (京師, Beijing), where the North Pole rises forty degrees above the horizon (i.e., at latitude 40°N), the approximate calculation gives eight degrees for the Yang Calendar and twenty-one degrees for the Yin Calendar. This tells us that when the conjunction of the Sun and Moon occurs within eight degrees of the two crossings in the Yang Calendar, or within twenty-one degrees of the two crossings in the Yin Calendar, a solar eclipse will necessarily be visible at that location. Beyond these limits, it will not be visible. From the northern (example), one can infer the southern (cases). There is no case in which the verification of an eclipse cannot be determined according to the variations in distance and quantity.

However, the reason there is a difference in the degrees of the eclipse limit between the two calendars (Yin and Yang) lies in the Moon's disk (月輪). The Moon's disk is closest to the Earth, and is also smaller than the Earth. The place from which people observe it is on the Earth's surface, not at the Earth's center.
