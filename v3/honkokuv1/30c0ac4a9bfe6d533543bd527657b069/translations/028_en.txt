**Mercury**
Mercury also travels before and behind the Sun, completing one circuit of the heavens per year. Its morning invisibility (晨伏) and conjunction invisibility (合伏) are each seventeen days and seventy-five ke, during which it travels thirty-four degrees and twenty-five fen each. It emerges in the west at evening; combining its fast and slow phases, it travels for twenty-seven days, covering thirty-one degrees and fifty fen. It stations for two days. Its evening retrograde invisibility (夕退伏) and conjunction retrograde invisibility (合退伏) are each eleven days, eighteen ke, and eighty fen, during which it retrogrades seven degrees, eighty-one fen, and twenty miao each. It stations at dawn (晨留) for two days. Combining slow and fast phases, it travels twenty-seven days, covering thirty-one degrees, and then again undergoes morning invisibility (晨伏), completing one synodic cycle, it is said. Approximately every forty-seven years [the pattern] repeats, but the exact days and times are likewise not identical.

**The Calendrical Epoch of the Seven Luminaries**
The complex interweaving of the fast and slow motions of the Seven Luminaries (the Sun, Moon, and five planets) is such that after four thousand six hundred and seventeen years, on the day of the winter solstice at midnight on the new moon (朔旦) of the zi month (eleventh month) falling on a jiazi (甲子) day, the Sun and Moon come together like a pair of jade discs, and the Five Planets align like a string of pearls. This is the very first day of the calendrical epoch (暦元) of the Seven Luminaries. Tracing backward, this corresponds precisely to the zi month of the year Dingchou (丁丑) in the seventh year of the Yuanfeng era (元封七年) of the Han dynasty.

Reckoning forward, this corresponds again to the zi month with a jiazi new moon day, two thousand eight hundred and forty-nine years after the third year of the Kangxi era (康熙三年), the year Jiachen (甲辰).

**The Four Remainders: Luoji, Qibo, and Pei**

**Question:** Luoji (羅計), Qibo (気孛) — these are originally said to be the "remainders" (余) of Fire, Earth, Wood, and Water [i.e., Mars, Saturn, Jupiter, and Mercury]. According to astronomical calculators, Luohou (羅睺) and Jidu (計都) appear in the calculations of eclipse shadows, while Qibo (気孛) are not always visible, and when they do appear, something extraordinary is at hand. Are these stars that are not normally visible? Or are they purely computational constructs for eclipse calculations?

**Answer:** As for the Four Remainders (四余) — originally they were all designated as stars, with the claim that the lunar lodge positions (宿度) they occupied each carried auspicious or inauspicious significance. What was not understood was that, because the motion of the Moon is difficult to calculate, these were established as the intersection points (交点) of various orbital paths. Upon reflection, these are nothing more than names — they are used purely for the convenience of computing the degrees of motion, and none of them correspond to any physical, real object.

Luohou (羅睺) is precisely the intersection point (node) of the White Road (白道, the Moon's orbital path) — [interlinear note: that is, the path along which the Moon travels]. Jidu (計都) is precisely the midpoint of the White Road — [interlinear note: Calendrical scholars rely on these two points as the starting basis for calculating solar and lunar eclipses].
