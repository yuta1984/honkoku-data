This is the method of calculating the Moon's wandering orbit (游輪) as it enters and exits the inside and outside of the ecliptic. When it emerges from the inside to the outside, this is called the "Yang calendar mouth" (陽暦口); the world calls this Rāhu (羅睺), also known as the "Dragon's Head" (竜首). When it emerges from the outside to the inside, this is called the "Yin calendar mouth" (陰暦口); the world calls this Ketu (計都), also known as the "Dragon's Tail" (竜尾). [You Xi says: Rāhu and Ketu are the names for the points where the ecliptic and celestial equator intersect, crossing inside and outside of one another on the left and right. Later scholars mistakenly regarded these as residual stars of Mars and Saturn.]

Thus, the motion of the white path's (白道) direct crossing (正交), along with the four cardinal points of the yin and yang calendars, are given different names to form eight paths (八道), and together with the ecliptic (黄道) make nine paths (九道). In reality, it is a single path. The Moon's motion traverses all nine paths completely in slightly over eighteen years. [You Yan says: The nine paths are named according to the Moon's proximity or distance from the Sun. When the Moon travels east of the ecliptic, it is called the "Blue Path" (青道); south, the "Vermilion Path" (朱道); north, the "Black Path" (黒道); west, the "White Path" (白道). Each is divided into inner and outer portions to form eight paths, and together with the ecliptic, nine paths. Within nineteen years, the Moon completes its circuit of all nine paths and begins again. Therefore, when calculating solar and lunar eclipses, within the span of nineteen years the predicted degrees and arc-minutes are approximately the same, while the exact times shift somewhat—yet all follow fixed rules.]

Regarding the two points where the white path (白道, lunar orbit) intersects the ecliptic (黄道), the maximum distance between them is five degrees. Current measurements show that outside of new moon (朔) and full moon (望), the mutual separation always exceeds five degrees. At the upper and lower quarters (二弦), it is five degrees, seventeen arc-minutes, and thirty arc-seconds. From this we can infer that the angle at which the two paths intersect is not fixed and immovable.

In rough terms, the range of variation in this breadth follows a regular cycle, always with fifteen days as its limit.

**Solar Eclipses**

**Question:** The Sun is the master of all yang forces and should never suffer any diminishment. How then can there be a solar eclipse?

**Answer:** The Sun is the source of all yang forces; the stars and Moon all borrow their light from it. The Sun's path of travel lies above the Moon's celestial sphere (月天), while the Moon's path lies below the Sun's celestial sphere (日天). On the day of the new moon (朔日), the Sun and Moon meet, and their two orbital spheres come into mutual contact. [The "two spheres" refer to the Sun's orbital sphere and the Moon's orbital sphere.] When they share the same longitude (north-south) and the same latitude (east-west), the Moon receives the Sun's light from above, while the Moon's body blocks the Sun's light from below. This is why when the Sun and Moon coincide, the Sun is obscured and cannot be seen. Yet, although the Sun and Moon overlap in this way, they are separated vertically by several million li; when they coincide, they merely brush past one another. To a person standing on the surface of the Earth and looking upward, it appears that the Moon is covering the Sun. Thus, the Sun seems as if it has no light—yet in reality it has not actually lost its light at all. It is simply that the person cannot see its light.
