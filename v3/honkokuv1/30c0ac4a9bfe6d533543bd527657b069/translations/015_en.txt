This is a calculation of the Moon's touring circle (游輪, orbital path) as it enters and exits the inner and outer regions of the ecliptic. The point where (the Moon) exits from inside to outside is called the "Yang Calendar Gate" (陽暦口), which the world calls *Rāhu* (羅睺) and also names the "Dragon's Head" (竜首). The point where it enters from outside to inside [note: the transcription originally read 出, "exits," but the marginal correction #1 clarifies it should read 入, "enters"] is called the "Yin Calendar Gate" (陰暦口), which the world calls *Ketu* (計都) and also names the "Dragon's Tail" (竜尾). [Interlinear note: Youxi (游熙) states: Rāhu and Ketu are the names for the points where the ecliptic and the equator cross each other on left and right, inner and outer sides. Later people regarded them as residual stars of Mars and Saturn.]

That is, the motion of the Moon's true ascending node (正交) along the lunar path (白道), together with the four cardinal points (四正) and the two yin-yang calendrical gates, are each given separate names to form eight paths, and combined with the ecliptic make nine. In reality, it is but one path. The Moon's motion traverses all nine paths in a little more than eighteen years. [Interlinear note: Youyan (游燕) states: The nine paths are named according to the Moon's proximity or distance from the Sun. The Moon moving east of the ecliptic is called the "Blue Path" (青道); moving south, the "Red Path" (朱道); moving north, the "Black Path" (黒道); moving west, the "White Path" (白道). Each is further divided into inner and outer, making eight paths; combined with the ecliptic, they make nine. Within nineteen years, the Moon completes all nine paths and begins again. This is why, in computing solar and lunar eclipses, one need only look within the cycle of nineteen years: the degrees and minutes are nearly the same, and the time marks change only slightly, but all follow fixed rules.]

Regarding the two nodes where the lunar path (白道) crosses the ecliptic: when discussing the maximum angular distance (between the two paths), it is five degrees. But when measured today, outside of new moon (朔) and full moon (望), the mutual distances all exceed five degrees. At the first and last quarters (上弦・下弦), this distance is five degrees, seventeen minutes, and thirty seconds. From this one can infer that the angle at which the two paths intersect is not fixed and immovable.

In sum, concerning the variations in the breadth of this angle, fifteen days is always taken as the limiting period.

　　Solar Eclipse

　Question: The Sun is the ruler of all Yang (solar forces) and should never suffer any diminishment. How can it possibly be eclipsed?

Answer: The Sun is the ultimate source of all Yang; stars and the Moon all borrow their light from it. The Sun's orbital path lies above the Moon's heaven (orbital sphere), and the Moon's orbital path lies below the Sun's heaven. On the day of the new moon (朔), the Sun and Moon meet each other, and their two orbital spheres are tangent to one another. [Interlinear note: "The two spheres" refers to the orbital sphere of the Sun and the orbital sphere of the Moon.] When they share the same longitude (north-south) and the same latitude (east-west), the Moon receives the Sun's light from above, while the body of the Moon blocks the Sun's light below. This is why, when the Sun and Moon align with each other, (the Sun) is blocked and becomes invisible.

Nevertheless, although the Sun and the Moon appear to overlap, they are in fact separated vertically by several million *li*, and when they meet, they merely pass each other with a slight shifting of position. If a person standing on the surface of the Earth looks up at this, they will see the Moon covering the Sun. In this way, the Sun appears as if it has no light. Yet in truth, it has not actually lost its light at all — it is only that people cannot see its light.
