...How could the year constantly have a discrepancy? One merely observes that the various stars have shifted by approximately one fen [a small angular measure] when one gazes upward. This cannot be called "annual precession" (*suì chā*). It can only be called "stellar shift" (*xīng chā*). But even "stellar shift" is not quite right. One should only say that "the fixed stars complete a circuit of heaven, moving one degree per one hundred and ten years" — and moreover, they may be slower at some times and faster at others, their numbers not being constant. Those who measure this do no more than measure how far the fixed stars have moved from the solstice point, and how long they take to complete one circuit of heaven; this has nothing to do with the year (*suì*). The year has been constant and unchanging throughout all antiquity.

Yet a person, exhausting the full energy of a lifetime, does not exceed a few tens of years, and thus can only observe the fixed stars move by one degree or half a degree in their progression. How then could one, relying upon their speed and retardation and the degrees before and after, extend one's investigation to reach as far as tens of thousands of years? This passage discusses the principle of the discrepancy, but has not yet clarified the actual numerical value of that discrepancy.

I [the author] hold that the principles and numbers of heaven and earth have a subtlety that cannot be fathomed; they reside precisely within the intervals of seconds, *hū*, *wēi*, and *máng* [exceedingly minute units of measure]. The phenomena of the sun, moon, and stars, and the indicators of yin and yang, cold and heat, at times fail to align, and one cannot seek to understand them by means of a single fixed rule. The measurement of annual precession (*suì chā*): from antiquity down to the present, the degree, fen, and seconds of the star position at which the sun stands at the winter solstice differs year by year. [Interlinear annotation: The same is true for each seasonal node; the winter solstice is simply taken as the primary reference.] Moreover, the rates are each different [between observers and eras]. [Interlinear annotation: The slower instances show a shift of one degree per hundred-odd years; the faster instances show a shift of one degree per forty or fifty years. Such are the examples.] At the time of Emperor Yao, the winter solstice [position of the sun] was in the lunar mansion *Xū* (Emptiness), but now it has shifted eastward to the lunar mansion *Jī* (Winnowing Basket). This movement has a principle grounded in reality. Understand the method of precession; knowing this, one can grasp heaven's [workings] with the utmost precision.

[Interlinear annotation: Youyan says: In the measurement of actual observation and the precession method, the sun is taken as the primary reference and the motion of the stars as the basis. There are two kinds of solar year. The first: [the sun] departs from a certain seasonal node at a certain point, completes one circuit of heaven, and returns to the original node and original point. This is called the "Solar Seasonal-Node Year" (*tàiyáng jiéqì suì*). The second: the sun comes into conjunction with a certain fixed star, completes one circuit of heaven, and again comes into conjunction with the same star. This is called the "Solar Sidereal Year" (*tàiyáng héngxīng suì*). Fixed stars have their own intrinsic motion (*běn xíng*), moving from west to east. For example, suppose that at this year's winter solstice the sun is in conjunction with a certain star; at next year's winter solstice, that star has already advanced and lies only a small number of fen and seconds past the winter solstice point. When the sun reaches the winter solstice, it has already completed the full measure of the seasonal-node year, yet has still not reached the full measure of the stellar year. Accumulating over several tens of years, [the star] still does not reach [a difference of] one degree; over more than twenty-five thousand years, it still does not complete one full circuit. Calendrical scholars invariably use the seasonal-node limit [as their standard]; taking the amount by which [the sun] falls short of the full measure of the sidereal year as the "annual precession" (*suì chā*). Those who understand how to distinguish between the full measure of the seasonal-node year and the full measure of the sidereal year will grasp this.]

From the Han and Tang dynasties onward, scholars have only used year-divisions (*nián fēn*) to assess the coarseness or precision [of their calculations]. [Interlinear annotation: If one knows the methods of fen-measurement (*fēn cè*) and comprehensive measurement (*tǒng cè*), then it is not necessary to resolve doubts by deliberating over the quantity of years.] They have never understood the reason underlying the discrepancy.
