This is because the number of marks per day is not equal — this is what is called "spring motion is fullness (盈), autumn motion is contraction (縮)."

[Interlinear note: Yü-tzu (兪子) says: "In recent years, through precise measurement, it became known that the Sun and the primum mobile (宗動) do not share the same center. Therefore it became known that the seasonal nodes (節気) have fullness and contraction. Originally, one simply divided (the year) evenly into fifteen days, two hours, and four marks to make one seasonal node, without knowing that spring has fullness and autumn has contraction."]

Therefore, in fixing the boundary of fullness and contraction, it does not lie at the point of the two solstices.

[Interlinear note: The old calculations placed it here.]

Rather, it lies six degrees after the two solstices. These two points are designated the "end of fullness and beginning of contraction." Now (following the marginal corrections): contraction (縮) corresponds to the highest point (最高之点, i.e., apogee), and fullness (盈) corresponds to the lowest point (最卑之点, i.e., perigee). By fixing these two points, it at last becomes clear that the Sun's motion traces a single circle (規) that does not share the same center (as the Earth).

When its motion is slow, it is at the highest point (apogee); when its motion is fast, it is at the opposition of the highest point (最高之衝).

[Interlinear note: That is, the lowest point (最卑之点).]

This highest-point motion (最高本行) is analogous to the Moon's following its lunar apogee (月孛, yuè-bó). If the center of the Sun were the same as the two celestial poles and the primum mobile (宗動天), the revolution of the Sun's motion would always remain on the circle of the celestial equator, with absolutely no angular separation, and how could there be any difference in its eastward and westward motion? We thus understand: if the pneumas (気) were not intermingled and varied, there would be no means to accomplish transformation (化). If the seasons had no cold and heat, there would be no means to accord with order (序). If things were not interwoven in complexity (錯綜), there would be no means to produce pattern (文).

**The Moon (太陰)**

*Question:* The motion of the Moon does not follow the single line of the ecliptic, nor does it tread the seasonal nodes of the ecliptic. In sum, it swiftly completes one circuit on its own, and then it paces along to conjoin with the Sun, forming a new moon (合朔). Yet the light of the Sun is indispensable (切須) to the world, while the Moon, though it possesses a pure radiance (清光), does not shine constantly. Why is it that its motion is, on the contrary, more difficult (to calculate) than that of the Sun?

*Answer:* When the Sun rises, it spreads its light over all things of form and color (色象), and illuminates the scenery of mountains and rivers. When the Sun sets, the various luminaries (諸曜) receive its light, while the surface of the Earth becomes dark and obscure (幽暗). The body of the Moon is nearest to the Earth; it receives the illumination of the Sun and reflects it downward, causing that which is dark and obscure in the night to at times receive a pure radiance (清輝). Therefore, the Moon stands as the counterpart (敵) of the Sun. Yet its motion is intricately irregular and not uniform, making astronomical calculation (推歩) and arithmetic reckoning (籌算) ever more difficult. All of this is on account of the eclipses (交食).
