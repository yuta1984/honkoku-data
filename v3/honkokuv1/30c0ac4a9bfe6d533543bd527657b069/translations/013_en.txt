[Continuing from previous page:] …five degrees. But the time elapsed amounts to forty-six days, thirty-eight marks (刻), and ten minutes. This was originally called "shared degrees" (共度). This is because the number of marks per day is unequal — what is called "the spring motion is fullness (盈) and the autumn motion is contraction (縮)."

[Interlinear note: Yü-tzu (兪子) says: "It is through precise measurements made in recent years that we have come to know that the Sun and the primum mobile (宗動) do not share the same center. We therefore know that the solar terms have fullness and contraction. Originally, one merely divided equally, making fifteen days, two hours, and four marks into one solar term, without knowing that spring has fullness and autumn has contraction."]

Therefore, the boundary (界) of fullness and contraction is not located at the points of the two solstices

[Interlinear note: The old calculations placed it here],

but rather at six degrees after each of the two solstices. These two points are defined as the "end of fullness and beginning of contraction" (盈末縮初). [Marginal note #1: The character 盈 in "今盈" should read 縮.] Now, contraction (縮) corresponds to the point of highest distance (最高之点, apogee), [Marginal note #2: The character 縮 in "縮為" should read 盈.] and fullness (盈) corresponds to the point of lowest distance (最卑之点, perigee). By fixing these two points, one can finally clarify that the motion of the Sun traces a single circle (規) whose center does not coincide with (the Earth's center). Where its motion is slowest is at the point of highest distance (apogee), and where its motion is fastest is at the point in opposition to the highest (i.e., the perigee).

[Interlinear note: That is, the point of lowest distance (最卑之点).]

This highest point (apogee) and its own motion are analogous to how the Moon follows its lunar apogee (月孛). If the center of the Sun were the same as the two poles and the primum mobile (宗動天), then the revolution of the Sun's motion would always remain on the circle of the celestial equator, with absolutely no angular distance (距度). How could there then be any difference in east-west motion? From this we understand: if the qi (気) were not irregular (参差), there would be no way to bring about transformation (化); if the seasons had no cold and heat, there would be no way to accord with proper sequence (序); if things were not intricately interwoven (錯綜), there would be no way to generate pattern and beauty (文).

**The Moon (太陰)**

*Question:* The motion of the Moon does not follow the single line of the ecliptic, nor does it tread the solar terms of the ecliptic. In essence, it completes one circuit swiftly on its own and then steps forward to meet the Sun, forming a conjunction (合朔, new moon). Yet while the Sun's light is indispensable to the world, the Moon, though it possesses a clear light, does not illuminate constantly. Why, then, is its motion even more difficult to calculate than that of the Sun?

*Answer:* When the Sun rises, it spreads its light over all forms and appearances (色象), and illuminates the landscape of mountains and rivers. When the Sun sets, the various luminaries (諸曜) receive its light, while the surface of the earth falls into darkness. The body of the Moon is closest to the earth; it receives the illumination of the Sun and reflects it downward, causing those in the darkness of night to sometimes receive its clear radiance. Therefore, it stands as a counterpart (敵) to the Sun, and its motion is complex and irregular (参錯), never uniform, making its calculation and reckoning (推歩籌算) doubly difficult.
