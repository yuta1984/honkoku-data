[Continuing from the previous page:] …five degrees; but the time elapsed is forty-six days, thirty-eight marks, and ten minutes. This was originally called "shared degrees" (共度, kyōdo). This is because the number of marks (刻) per day is unequal—what is called "spring motion is in excess (盈) and autumn motion is in deficit (縮)."

[Interlinear note: Yuzi (兪子) said: "In recent years, through precise observation, it has become known that the Sun and the Primum Mobile (宗動天) do not share the same center. Thus it is known that the solar terms have excess and deficit (盈縮). Originally one simply divided evenly into fifteen days, two hours, and four marks to make one solar term, without knowing that spring has excess and autumn has deficit."]

Therefore, when the boundary of excess and deficit is fixed, it does not lie at the points of the two solstices [note: the original calculation placed it there], but rather six degrees after the two solstices. These two points are designated the "end of excess and beginning of deficit" (盈末縮初). Now, [deficit (縮)] corresponds to the apogee (最高之点), and [excess (盈)] corresponds to the perigee (最卑之点). [Marginal note #1: "In 'now excess (盈),' 盈 should read 縮 (deficit)." Marginal note #2: "In 'deficit (縮) for,' 縮 should read 盈 (excess)."] By fixing these two points, it is thereby made clear that the Sun's motion traces a single circle (規) whose center does not coincide [with that of the Primum Mobile]. The motion is slowest at the apogee and swiftest at the point opposite the apogee—that is, the perigee (最卑之点).

This proper motion of the apogee is analogous to the Moon following its apogee point (月孛, the lunar apogee). If the center of the Sun were the same as the two poles and the Primum Mobile, then the revolution of the Sun's motion would always remain within the equatorial circle, there would be absolutely no angular distance (距度), and how could there be any difference in its eastward and westward motion? From this we understand: if the vital pneuma (気) were not irregular, there would be no means to accomplish transformation (化); if there were no cold and heat in the seasons, there would be no means to conform to order (序); if things did not interlace in complexity (錯綜), there would be no means to generate pattern and beauty (文).

**The Moon (太陰)**

**Question:** The Moon's motion does not follow the single line of the ecliptic, nor does it trace the seasonal nodes of the ecliptic. In short, it swiftly completes one revolution on its own and then steps forward to meet the Sun, forming a conjunction (合朔). Yet the light of the Sun is indispensable to the world, while the Moon, though it possesses a clear light, does not shine constantly. Why is its motion, on the contrary, more difficult [to reckon] than that of the Sun?

**Answer:** When the Sun rises, it spreads its light over all visible forms and colors, and reveals shadows upon mountains and rivers. When the Sun sets, the various luminaries receive its light, while the surface of the earth becomes dark and obscure. The body of the Moon is the closest of all to the Earth; it receives the illumination of the Sun and reflects it downward, causing those who dwell in the darkness of night to sometimes receive its pure and luminous radiance. Thus it stands as the counterpart to the Sun, and its motion is intricate and irregular, never constant; the calculation and reckoning of its course is doubly and triply difficult.
