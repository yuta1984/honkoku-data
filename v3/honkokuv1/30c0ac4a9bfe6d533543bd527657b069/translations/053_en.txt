**[Question]** Saturn and Jupiter are more than twenty times the size of the Earth, and Mars is about half the size of the Earth. That they are visible is entirely reasonable. Venus is one thirty-sixth the size of the Earth, yet if one calculates based on the Earth's diameter of 90,000 li, it still amounts to approximately 3,000 li in size. Its heaven (orbit) lies above those of Mercury and the Moon, so that it too should be visible — and indeed it is. However, Mercury is more than ten thousand times smaller than the Earth, and by calculation its size does not even reach ten li. Its position also lies above the heavenly sphere of the Moon. There ought to be no reason for it to be visible. Then how is it that its movement and degrees are measured — how do we come to know it?

**[Answer]** Venus and Mercury both travel in attendance upon the Sun, and their light is submerged beneath the Sun's brilliance and thus invisible. In the countries of the far West (Europe), in recent years, distinguished scholars of degree-calculation (astronomy) have constructed far-seeing mirrors (望遠鏡; telescopes).
[Marginal note: Yóu Yān states: These mirrors have a tube made of leather, approximately one chi (foot) or more in length, consisting of four or five tubes of varying sizes nested together. The inner small-tube mirror, when pointed at an object, makes large things appear small. The outer large-tube mirror, when pointed at an object, makes small things appear large. At a distance of approximately one hundred li, provided there are no clouds or haze intervening, one can see the beard, eyebrows, and smiling expressions of a person. It was during the Wanli reign period that this instrument first entered China. Subsequently there also arrived the Jiǎn Tiān Yí (simplified celestial instrument) and measurement sundials, praised as instruments of incomparable ingenuity. The measuring of the heights and extents of the heavens, and the determination of the degree-positions (depth and elevation) of the stars — all of this is accomplished by means of these instruments.]

Upon observing Jupiter (through this telescope), four small stars were found at its sides, revolving around it with great speed. At the sides of Saturn, two small stars were found, as though attached to its ears. When observing Venus (Tài Bái), one sees that at times it darkens (new phase), at times its light is full (full phase), and at times it shows upper and lower quarters (half phases).

As for Mercury: if one wishes to see it, one must look horizontally toward the level of the tops of tall mulberry trees and elms. By cutting along the floating vapors (atmosphere) above the ground, the small can be reflected and made to appear large — just as wearing a mirror (lens) over the eye enlarges letters when reading. It is also possible for distant things to be reflected and made to appear near — just as placing a coin in a bowl of water makes it appear closer (due to refraction).

Furthermore, Mercury has a proper heaven (ben tian; deferent), and also a heaven of an attached wheel (fù lún; epicycle). This wheel (epicycle) is also quite large. Were it not large, Mercury could not at times come before the Sun and at other times lie behind the Sun. Traveling on the epicycle, there is an apogee (the arc of the wheel far from the Earth) and a perigee (the arc of the wheel near the Earth). Moreover, the center of its proper heaven does not correspond to the center of the Earth (i.e., it is eccentric). Because it does not revolve concentrically, there are already places where it is distant from the Earth's center, and also places where it is near the Earth's center.

Therefore, Mercury can be seen at certain times. That is: by looking horizontally through the floating atmospheric vapors, the small is rendered large by refraction; moreover, the arc of its orbital wheel (epicycle) already lies close to the Earth; and in addition, the motion of its proper heaven also brings it near the Earth.
