A (lunar) eclipse must occur at the time of the full moon (望). The location where the eclipse occurs is invariably fixed at the Dragon's Head and Dragon's Tail. [You Yan says: These are precisely the points where the ecliptic (黄道) and the lunar path (白道) intersect.] [At the intersection, the longitudes and latitudes coincide—hence an eclipse occurs.] Beyond thirteen and one-third degrees from these nodes, the Moon's path no longer intersects (with the Sun's or the Earth's shadow), and the Earth's shadow cannot obstruct (the Moon). The Dragon's Head and Dragon's Tail are the two boundary points of the Sun's path (i.e., the two nodes where the ecliptic and lunar path cross), and they are the places through which a lunar eclipse passes. However, at the time of an eclipse, the Earth's shadow shields upward from beneath the observer's feet; therefore, all people under heaven see it alike. [You Xi says: One always sees a lunar eclipse at night, and the observer stands within the Earth's shadow. Therefore all people under heaven see it the same way.] In general, those who observe from the surface of the Earth will also see the size and speed (of the eclipse) the same way. Only if their position along the meridian (子午線) differs will the time also differ. In broad terms, every nineteen years the orbital positions (宮度) recur the same, yet the timing and depth of the eclipse will not be identical. As for why the eclipse does not reach the enclosures and lunar mansions of Saturn and Jupiter, it is because the (Earth's) shadow becomes oblique and tapers to a point, so that it does not penetrate through to those distances. When the Sun completes one full circuit of the heavens, its shadow likewise completes one circuit. When the Moon arrives at the position of that shadow, an eclipse immediately occurs. Yet the position (on the ecliptic) where the eclipse takes place has no fixed location (i.e., it varies each time).

**Eclipses (Combined Discussion)**

**Question:** A solar eclipse is the Moon's occultation (of the Sun), and a lunar eclipse is the shielding of the Earth's shadow. However, occultation and shielding both result in an absence of light—so should not all eclipses be the same? Moreover, it is said that a lunar eclipse looks the same (from everywhere), while a solar eclipse does not look the same, owing to the fact that the Earth's shadow is circular. Please explain this in detail.

**Answer:** In the movement of the Sun and Moon, after twenty-nine days and a fraction, (the Sun and Moon) reach the same degree (longitude) in the east-west direction—this is called "conjunction at new moon" (合朔, gǒushuò). When the Moon blocks sunlight from below and does so without deviation or obliquity, this is called a solar eclipse. [Yú Dàgāng says: At conjunction (合朔), all first-day-of-the-month (初一) configurations are conjunctions. The reason a conjunction does not always produce an eclipse is that the Moon travels along nine paths (九道), and its degrees deviate obliquely from the Sun's degrees, so it passes along the edge (of the Sun). Hence there can be a conjunction without an eclipse.] When the Moon is one hundred and eighty degrees from the Sun and is in direct opposition (衝) to it, this is called the full moon (望). If at the time of opposition the Moon enters the Earth's shadow, and the Earth, being in the middle, blocks sunlight so that it cannot illuminate the body of the Moon, the Moon loses its light. This is called a lunar eclipse. Eclipses always occur at the places where the two paths—the ecliptic (黄道) and the lunar path (白道)—intersect, which are called the "true nodes" (正交)——
