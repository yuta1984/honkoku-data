An eclipse must occur at the time of the full moon (望). The location at which the eclipse occurs is fixed at the Dragon's Head and Dragon's Tail. [You Yan says: These are precisely the points where the two paths—the ecliptic (黄道) and the lunar path (白道)—intersect.] [Because the degrees of longitude and latitude coincide, an eclipse occurs.] This must be within thirteen and one-third degrees; beyond this, the Moon's path does not intersect (with the Earth's shadow), and the Earth's shadow cannot obstruct it. The Dragon's Head and Dragon's Tail are the two boundary points of the Sun's path (the ecliptic), and are the places through which a lunar eclipse passes. However, at the time of an eclipse, the Earth's shadow rises upward from beneath the observer, shielding what is above; therefore all people under heaven see it the same way. [You Xi says: One always sees a lunar eclipse at night, and people stand within the Earth's shadow, and so all people under heaven see it the same.] In general, for those observing from the surface of the Earth, the size and speed (of the eclipse) are also the same. Only those residing at different meridians (子午線) will see it at different times. Roughly every nineteen years, the degree of the lunar mansion (宮度) through which the Moon passes is the same, yet the timing and depth of the eclipse are also not the same. The reason that the enclosing stars (垣宿) of Saturn and Jupiter are not reached by an eclipse is that the shadow (of the Earth) is oblique and pointed, and does not penetrate to them. When the Sun completes one circuit of the heavens, its shadow also completes one circuit of the heavens. When the Moon encounters the place of that shadow, it is immediately eclipsed. However, the location at which the eclipse occurs has no fixed place (it changes according to the Moon's orbit).

**Conjunction Eclipses (交食: Solar and Lunar Eclipses)**

**Question:** A solar eclipse is the Moon's occultation, and a lunar eclipse is the shielding of the Earth's shadow. Yet both the occultation and the shielding equally result in an absence of light. Then should not all eclipses be the same? Furthermore, it is said that lunar eclipses appear the same (to all observers), while solar eclipses do not—and that this is due to the Earth's shadow being circular in shape. Please explain this in detail.

**Answer:** In the motion of the Sun and Moon, after twenty-nine days and some fraction, when they reach the same degree from east to west, this is called "conjunction" (合朔, the new moon). When the Moon's dark body interposes below the sunlight and is not oblique or slanted (i.e., directly in front), this is called a solar eclipse (日食). [Yú Dàgāng says: At conjunction (合朔), that is, at the first day of each month, all come into conjunction. When they conjoin but no eclipse occurs, it is because the Moon travels along the nine paths (九道), and there is an oblique deviation from the Sun's degree, so the Moon passes along the edge. Therefore, at conjunction there may be no eclipse.] When the Moon is one hundred and eighty degrees from the Sun, directly opposing it in opposition (衝), this is called the full moon (望). If at the time of opposition the Moon necessarily enters the Earth's shadow—with the Earth positioned in between—the sunlight is blocked by the Earth and cannot shine upon the body of the Moon. The Moon loses its light, and this is called a lunar eclipse (月食). The eclipse always occurs at the place where the two paths—the ecliptic and the lunar path—intersect, which is called the "true crossing" (正交)...
