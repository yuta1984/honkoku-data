Next comes the Heaven of the Prime Mover (Sōdō-ten / Primum Mobile), which carries and rotates all the lower heavens beneath it. The motion of this heaven depends on the two poles — South and North — revolving from east to west in a leftward rotation, completing one full circuit in twelve double-hours (one full day). Each of the individual heavens is governed and set in motion by this one movement.

[Interlinear note: According to the measurements of Western scholars (Jesuit missionaries), there are additionally two further layers of precession — a north-south precession and an east-west precession — completing one full circuit from west to east in approximately forty-nine thousand years.]

Next is the Heaven of the Fixed Stars, situated above the Seven Luminaries. This is the heaven's own intrinsic motion (hondō). Along the celestial equator, it moves from south to north and from north to south. This is called the "motion of approach and recession" (ikkin-ichikyo no dō). It completes one revolution over seven thousand years. Its direct motion from east to west completes one full revolution in approximately twenty-five thousand and some years.

[Interlinear note: This is the calculation for the direct motion of precession (saisa seikō).]

Next come the heavens of Saturn [Interlinear note: Saturn completes one full circuit of the heavens in approximately 29 years, 155 days, and 25 units of time (刻)], Jupiter [Interlinear note: Jupiter completes one circuit in approximately 11 years, 313 days, and 70 units of time], and Mars [Interlinear note: Mars completes one circuit in approximately 1 year, 321 days, and 93 units of time].

Next is the Heaven of the Sun, which illuminates the world and from which all the ten thousand phenomena draw their light. It therefore occupies the middle position among the Seven Luminaries.

[Interlinear note: The Sun completes one circuit in approximately 365 days, 23 units of time, and 5 parts.]

Next are the heavens of Venus and Mercury, both of which follow the Heaven of the Sun. The Heaven of the Moon (Taiin) is the closest to the Earth.

[Interlinear note: The Moon completes one circuit in approximately 27 days and 31 units of time.]

All these heavens — of the fixed stars and the Seven Luminaries — are reckoned according to their own rightward rotation (right-to-left, i.e., west to east). The single heaven of leftward rotation (i.e., the Primum Mobile) takes the pole of the Still Heaven (Seiten-kyoku) as its axis and the celestial equator (赤道) as its "celestial girdle" (ten'yō). The heavens of rightward rotation take the ecliptic pole (kōdō-kyoku) as their axis, each deviating north and south by twenty-three degrees and thirty-one minutes, taking the ecliptic (黄道) as their celestial girdle.

The centre of the equator, the centre of the Still Heaven, the centre of the Primum Mobile, and the centre of the Earth — all are identically one single point. The two poles (of the equatorial system) lie along the true north-south meridian (正子午), presiding over the common motion (kōun) of the Seven Luminaries and the ranked constellations through one full revolution per day, to which all are connected and made to revolve (tenken). The path of the equator and the celestial prime equator (ten'gen sekidō) coincide to form a single line. Though motion and stillness differ, the poles never depart from the true meridian throughout all ages.
