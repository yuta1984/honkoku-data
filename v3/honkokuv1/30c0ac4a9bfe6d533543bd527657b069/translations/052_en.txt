**[Question]** We have now learned that there are methods for calculating the distances of the heavens of the Seven Luminaries and the Lunar Mansions (fixed stars) from the Earth. But as for the diameters arranged by the six grades (of stellar magnitude), how large or small are they?

**[Answer]** According to the measurements of the Húngài Túxiàn (astronomical charts and instruments), the bodies of the fixed stars (jīng xīng) are divided into six grades. The first grade (upper grade) has a full diameter
[Marginal note: The full circumference and diameter of the Earth: approximately 90,000 li in circumference; 28,660 li and 36 zhàng in diameter.]
sixty-eight times greater than the full diameter of the Earth. The very largest among them are yet a further twenty times greater, and the next smaller ones are reduced by a similar proportion. The second grade is twenty-eight times greater than the Earth. The third grade is eleven times greater than the Earth. The fourth grade is four and a half times greater than the Earth. The fifth grade is approximately the same size as the Earth, or slightly larger. The sixth grade has only one-third the volume of the Earth.

Beyond the sixth grade, there are yet more that are so faint and minute as to be difficult to see — they are beyond what the eye can measure and beyond what numbers can calculate.

Among the bodies of the Seven Luminaries, only the Sun is the greatest. Its diameter exceeds the Earth's by one hundred sixty-five and three-eighths times.
[Marginal note: Yóu Yān states: This is calculated by taking each degree of the celestial circumference as sixty arc-minutes. The Sun is approximately thirty arc-minutes in apparent size. If the Earth's scale were converted to solar terms, it would amount to merely ten-odd arc-seconds. The same applies to other stars. Furthermore, taking the Sun and Moon each in their own proper heaven (běn tiān), with half a degree constituting one full diameter, Fāng Sùbó calculated on the basis of half a degree that the Sun's diameter is 138,248.5 li. If one calculates on the basis of the figure of over one hundred times, then the Sun's diameter would be 4,884,035 li and more. In its own heaven, it should be based on seventeen degrees and 118,306 li. This is a place where the original text (chū hán) is in error, and thus Sùbó has clarified it.]

Saturn (Tián Xīng, "the Filling Star") is twenty-two times larger than the Earth. Jupiter (Suì Xīng, "the Year Star") is approximately the same as Saturn. Mars (Yíng Huò, "the Fitful Glitterer") is yet less than Jupiter. The Earth is thirty-six and one twenty-seventh times larger than Venus (Tài Bái, "the Great White"). And Mercury (Chén Xīng, "the Hour Star"), being in the lowest position, is yet more diminutive and tiny. The Earth is thirty-eight and one-third times larger than the Moon. It follows then that the Sun is six thousand five hundred thirty-eight and one-fifth times larger than the Moon.

I would observe: what the human eye perceives — what is near, though small, appears large; what is far, though large, appears small. These (figures for stellar size) have been measured through the application of theoretical reasoning; if one were to seek firm empirical proof, there would remain some degree of conjecture and forced inference.
