[Interlinear note: When it (the pole star) is precisely at the zenith, if one proceeds further, it once again gradually descends from the zenith. There is no principle by which the North Pole could pass south of (the zenith), or the South Pole north of (the zenith). This is because the Earth is spherical.]

If one wishes to measure east and west, one must first fix upon a single location to serve as the starting point of the boundary.

[Interlinear note: For example, if one is situated in a certain place, that very place is taken as the starting point.]

Only then can measurement be carried out. Without a method of measurement, the seasons of cold and warmth would have no fixed standard, and east and west would be impossible to distinguish.

**On the Ecliptic (黄道) and the Celestial Equator (赤道)**

*Question:* The body of Heaven has no bounds and is difficult to measure with instruments. The sun, moon, and five planets have visible forms and can be calculated by observation. But the two paths — the ecliptic and the celestial equator — have absolutely no visible form or shape. To what, then, do they point, and how are they named?

*Answer:* The celestial equator (赤道, *sekidō*) is that which divides the celestial sphere equally into south and north. Following the boundary that lies precisely midway between the south and north celestial poles, one demarcates a single fine path, which is called the celestial equator.

[Interlinear note: Zhu Xi says: "The celestial sphere resembles a single round box. The celestial equator is the single line at the place where the two halves of the box meet and are joined together."]

It extends across the middle of the sky and has remained unchanged since time immemorial. All who calculate the movements of the heavens rely upon it entirely as their standard and criterion.

Li Zhenzhi (李振之) states: "This path (the celestial equator) is arranged into three hundred and sixty degrees, divided into twelve chronological divisions (*chen* / 辰), and demarcated into ninety-six *ke* (刻, quarter-hours).

[Interlinear note: Reckoning eight *ke* to one *shi* (時, double hour); the fractional remainder is not included in the listing.]

Its functions are seven in number: First, to measure the revolution of the heavens' daily motion, completing one circuit per day; second, to determine the length and brevity of the divisions of day and night; third, to equalize the extent of the ecliptic's excursions north and south of it; fourth, to delimit the gnomon shadows at the spring and autumn equinoxes; fifth, to distinguish between north and south along the celestial path; sixth, to establish the calculation of northern and southern latitudes; and seventh, to record the sphericity of the terrestrial globe throughout the world."

As for the ecliptic (黄道, *kōdō*): it is established in accordance with the orbital path that the Sun traverses in the course of one year, and serves to record the paths along which the moon and five planets travel. In general, the orbital trace that the Sun follows as it traverses the heavens, completing one circuit in one year, revolves to form a circle. This is named the ecliptic.

This path winds obliquely across the celestial equator, as though two rings were overlaid upon one another. Half extends south of the celestial equator; the southernmost boundary, at approximately twenty-three and a half degrees of latitude, constitutes the winter solstice (point). Half extends north of the celestial equator; the northernmost boundary, at approximately twenty-three and a half degrees of latitude, constitutes the summer solstice (point). The places where the two paths (ecliptic and celestial equator) intersect constitute the spring and autumn equinox (points). Dividing these four points equally, one obtains four quadrants, each quadrant being ninety degrees. These are precisely the two equinoxes (*nibu* / 二分)...
