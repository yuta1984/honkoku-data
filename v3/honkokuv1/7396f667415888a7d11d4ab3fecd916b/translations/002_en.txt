**Preface to the Ansei Fūmon-ki**

Is it not truly said that "when one disaster strikes, a second disaster follows"? Last winter brought a great earthquake, and this autumn a great storm (typhoon) came, tilting houses and felling trees — in this, both calamities are much alike. Yet where they differ somewhat is that the rain soaked and saturated the earthen walls and clay storehouses. As a result, floodwaters overflowed, sweeping away people's property and drowning many souls — surely these terrible events came to pass because of the unseasonable disorder of the seasons.

Upon reflection, it is said in old writings that when governance goes astray and virtue is hidden, great winds break open houses and snap trees. Yet such ancient words do not necessarily ring true in the present case. Natural disasters belong to the order of nature itself, and it is also said that in a time of great peace, the wind does not so much as rustle a branch, and the rain does not break a clod of earth. Nevertheless, even the sage-king Yao suffered nine years of flooding, and the sage-king Tang endured seven years of drought. That disasters of flood and drought occur in this way is a natural calamity (*tengetsu*) unto itself, unrelated to whether the times are good or bad.
