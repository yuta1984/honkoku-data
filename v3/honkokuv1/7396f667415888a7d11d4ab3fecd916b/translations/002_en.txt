**Preface to the Ansei Fūmon-ki**

Is it not truly fitting that it is said, "When one disaster strikes, a second disaster follows"? Last winter, there was a great earthquake, and this autumn, there came a great wind that tilted houses and felled trees — in this, it was much the same as the earthquake. Yet it differed somewhat in that the rain soaked the plaster walls and flooded the earthen storehouses. Because of this, floodwaters overflowed, sweeping away people's valuables and drowning many souls. It must be that this calamity came to pass because of the unseasonable weather.

Reflecting upon this: it is said that when governance goes astray and virtue goes into hiding, great winds break open houses and snap trees. Yet even that old saying does not hold true in the present case. Natural disasters arise of themselves, and it is said that in an age of great peace, the wind does not so much as rustle a branch, nor does the rain break a clod of earth — yet the sage-king Yao suffered nine years of flooding, and the sage-king Tang endured seven years of drought. That such disasters of flood and drought occur is proof that they are natural calamities (*tengaku*) independent of whether the times are good or ill. Even the reign of a sage cannot escape them.
