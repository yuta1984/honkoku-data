There are things that cannot be escaped, and yet there are also things that somehow are escaped. As the popular saying goes, "The floating world of today is turned upside down." Therefore, perhaps one may say: "A calamity of one's own making can still be avoided, but a calamity wrought by Heaven cannot be escaped."

And so (by this storm), boats ran aground on dry land, people floated adrift on the water, hills crumbled into hollows, and ditches were filled up into mounds. Houses that had formed the shape of the character 人 (*hito*, "person") were shattered into scattered strokes. If Wang Jing-gong (Wang Anshi) could have seen this, what on earth would he have said?

Now, this great maritime gale (*guifū*) is said to have struck this side of Hakone, and it blew everything quite topsy-turvy — it is, in the terms of the *Book of Changes*, the hexagram of *Xùn* (Wind/Gentle, 巽). In general, the northwest suffered only minor damage, with few people injured, while the southeast was devastated, with many lives lost. Looking at the state of things, the guardian shrines stand desolate and ruined, sure to entreat their parishioners to help with repairs; the saving Buddhist temples will no doubt appeal to their affiliated faithful for funds to rebuild.

At such a time, even the pine trees of "Aioi" — celebrated for their thousand years of longevity — have been snapped at the trunk. After a hundred years, or fifty years at least, the autumn wind strikes with sudden astonishing force —

**[Left folio]**

— and even the willow that never bends, its flowerless branches are rent apart. If (these trees) had hearts, they would surely lament this sorrowful world come to its end.

Or again: great fish are pushed out and come to dwell in shallow waters; mole crickets and ants are swept in and fall prey to fish large enough to swallow a boat — and in the end all become things without feeling or life. Moreover, the water of the Tate River overflows sideways, and the farmers raise a great clamor, not about to let their painstakingly grown rice paddies and vegetable fields — the fruit of grain-by-grain hardship — be ruined by the flood. The peasants around Rakan-ji temple, baring their right shoulders, stripping both shoulders bare, working naked and indifferent to appearances, pour forth their precious sweat once again — truly living up to the name of beings whose fate is bound to the flow of water.

In the overflowing waters, it is said that the courtesans (*yūjo*) selling their affections in the temporary brothel-quarter (*karitaku*) of Fukagawa found themselves submerged up to the neck. At Imado, where the kilns' smoke no longer rises, even the "Saigyō" ceramic figurines (popular souvenir wares) were swept away, their whereabouts unknown, and so they lament.

One who cites the line "the great swan flies off into the darkness" must be a poet acquainted with the Chinese verse of Zhang Jiuling; one who murmurs "Wheresoever shall I find a place to rest my weary self?" must be a *waka* poet in the vein of the Middle Counselor Fujiwara no Tametori. And one who intones "trembling and quaking" (*sensen kyōkyō*) must be a person of refined taste who has read the *Mao Shi* (the *Book of Odes*).
