The sub-temples (of Reiganjii) and the grounds of Jōshinji were also greatly damaged. The area around temple town (*teramachi*), already known for its unpleasant odors, was now made worse by the accumulation of the drowned carcasses of dogs and cats, their flesh rotting, and as the water receded and the days passed, the stench became piercing to the nostrils. Sarue and Umibe Daiku-chō were entirely devastated. Various places around Saga-chō, on the far side of Eitaibashi Bridge, were damaged. In the licensed quarter's temporary residences (*karitaku*) around Naka-chō, the establishments known as "Sa no Tsuchiya" and "Dai no Jiya" were largely destroyed, while others had their walls blown out, their roofs broken, and their buildings tilted—all suffering severe damage. Within the grounds of the Hachimangū shrine, the temporary structures housing the special exhibition of the Enoshima Benzaiten (from Sagami Province), as well as the booths for dedicated votive offerings and shows, all toppled over and were washed away. The sacred shrine cabinet (*ozushi*) of Benzaiten was transferred for safekeeping to the main hall of the Hachimangū. One bridge within the shrine grounds collapsed and was swept away. In Shinchi and Hamaguri-chō, many houses collapsed. In the area of Kiba, Dobashi, and Irifune-chō, houses were swept away by the storm surge and crushed by the wind, suffering total devastation. At the Susaki Benzaiten shrine precincts, the tea stalls (*kake-jaya*) were all blown to pieces, and a great pine tree was toppled. The bridge before the gate collapsed and was carried away; houses along the riverbank were entirely destroyed, with damage throughout.

◯ Around this time, someone—it is not known who—composed a piece in the style of a *kudoki* (a narrative lament-song), setting down an account of the storm's path in verse. Among its lines were the following:

**[Left page]**

〽 The deep, deep Fukagawa, a flat expanse, the people swept away by wind and water— how agonizing the town [*kurushisa ga machi*], waiting for someone to help [*matsu ga machi*]; yet in Morishita-chō, every flourishing branch and bough has been blown and broken off. The houses have crumbled—that is Ōshima-chō. In the middle of the sea-town [*umi no naka-machi*], the Dobashi Bridge has fallen; the front watchtower flies up, the rear watchtower too. Even the Benzaiten of the flower quarter has come here on her travels to take temporary residence—a jeweled public display, what a futile, wretched end. Though they call it a village of drifting homes, deeper than children's pools are the depths of suffering [*kukai no fuchi*]: the water is six *shaku* deep upon the estate grounds, submerging one up to the neck, driving one's body under; warehouses, dwellings, timber from the lumberyards—all parted from with tears to the tolling of the Hachiman bell. That sound, too, marks the impermanence of the newly established Tsukidashi Shinchi. What remains are Susaki and Nagahori-chō; the town of long life, Kamehisa-chō; the town of accumulated years, Mannen-chō; westward toward the Pure Land of Amida, Nishinaga-chō; what is called Ikuyo Bridge is now Ariake Bridge— the lamp flame gutters out, the oil runs dry.
