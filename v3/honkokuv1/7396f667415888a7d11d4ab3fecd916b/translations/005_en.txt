## [Right Page] The Wind Deity (Fūjin)

Among all things that exist between Heaven and Earth, there are many that the naked eye cannot perceive — but among these, it is the wind whose very form and shape remains truly unknowable. And yet it is said that the wind has its own deity. A commentary on the *Jinshu* (*Book of Jin*) states: "Fei Lian (飛廉) is the Earl of Wind (風伯); the Winnowing Basket Star (箕星) is the Master of Wind (風師)." That the Winnowing Basket Star, among the northern lunar mansions, governs the wind is something known to many. As for the appearance of the deity Fei Lian, it is described thus: "His body resembles a deer, his head is like that of a sparrow, he has horns, a serpent's tail, and the dappled markings of a leopard." For this reason, a figure of him is depicted here — though what he truly looks like in reality, one can only wonder.

Furthermore, in the northern and cold regions of the country, there is said to be a creature called *kamahetachi* (鎌閉太知). When one is caught in a whirlwind (*tsujikaze*), one invariably receives a wound — and yet the form of this creature is never seen. Since the *raijū* (thunder beast) has reportedly fallen to earth and been captured by people, this creature must be something akin to it. Accordingly, through the artistic imagination of painters, one sees depictions of a weasel-like creature with fangs. It is my conjecture that the word *kamahetachi* was misheard as *kamaitachi* (鎌鼬), and through oral transmission this misrendering produced such illustrations — serving mainly to astonish the eyes of children.

### Preface (Kanba)

As the old saying goes: when the wind is slight, even a single barrier is enough to stop it from passing through. But when it rises and blows in full force, it uproots trees, topples houses, and renders the blue of the sky, the yellow of the earth, and even the undimmed light of the sun and moon all hazy and indistinct — to the point where one cannot tell one color or shape from another. Yet for all this, the ten thousand things cannot be nourished without wind. Indeed, the very breath and respiration of human beings — who dwell huddled within the space of Heaven and Earth and move about borne upon the *qi* (vital energy) — are nothing other than wind. Thus, when *qi* stirs, the wind grows fierce. And it is likewise that the wind afflicts and torments the human body — when colds (*fūja*) and strokes (*chūfū*) become severe, they numb the hands and feet, reducing one to helplessness, and may strike one down suddenly, even to the point of death. The so-called "karma wind" (*gōfū*) spoken of in Buddhism is said to shatter even metal and stone with a single blow. These must surely be counted among wind's harms.
