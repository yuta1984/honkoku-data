They rushed about in a panic, wandering north and south, a truly pitiful sight. At that very moment, Matsushichi too was startled and frightened together with his wife and children, trembling as they deliberated whether to flee from the front or escape from the back — all the while shaking with fear. His wife proposed that they climb up to the roof ridge to weather it out, but Matsushichi said it was dangerous and they could not reach a decision; while they were at this impasse arguing, the water had already pushed its way up to the edge of the veranda. At that, Matsushichi strapped his four-year-old son tightly to his back and fled out through the back door. He noticed a great old camphor tree (楠) standing nearby, ran over to it, placed his feet on the knots and burls of the trunk, grabbed hold of the thick overhanging branches, and with great difficulty hauled himself up. Trembling and shaking violently, he could do nothing but call out the names of the gods and buddhas over and over. He felt scarcely alive. Even the child strapped to his back, apparently terrified by the ferocious sound of the rushing water, clung tightly to his father's back without letting out so much as a single cry — his unexpected silence must surely have been the result of being paralyzed with fear at the overwhelming horror of it all.

And so it was, at around what seemed to be the Hour of the Ox (八ツ時, approximately 2 a.m.), there came the sound of a commotion and people approaching the base of the camphor tree. Matsushichi, astride the tree up above, thought to himself: surely someone, seeing the flooding caused by this great storm surge, must have rowed a rescue boat over from somewhere. He would ask to be taken aboard and transfer onto it — and so, peering down through the darkness of the night, he found it was not so. Rather, it appeared that a great many people who had climbed up onto the ridges of their rooftops were floating past, being carried along by the current.

So then, if every house was in such a state, his own home must surely have been swept away as well, and his wife and the others must by now have vanished like bubbles on the water — the anguish in his heart was beyond ordinary measure. Alone, he brooded on and on, with not even a moment for his tears to dry, weeping through the remainder of that night. But when the wind ceased and the rain cleared, it was not long before dawn broke, and gradually the floodwaters began to recede. Resolving that now he would climb down and go to see what had become of his wife — whether she lived or had died — he descended with great difficulty from the tree.

When he made his way out toward the street, he unexpectedly encountered his wife, along with some of the neighbors. At that moment, they looked at each other's faces, and tears came first — for a little while, not a word could be spoken. Presently, Matsushichi turned to his wife and said, "I, for such-and-such reasons, managed to survive together with the boy — but how did you manage to get through it?" To this, his wife replied, turning to her husband: "So you were saved by the camphor tree, were you?
