**[Right page]**

In an attempt to rescue the principal Buddhist image, it was taken out from its miniature shrine, and the people who had been sheltering inside provided an escort as the image was moved to the reception hall. Not long after this, the main hall collapsed — a truly miraculous turn of events. A certain acquaintance of mine, three days after the storm had passed, happened by on an errand, having heard rumors that the main hall of the monzeki temple had collapsed. Upon visiting to see for himself, he found countless tens of thousands of people, young and old, men and women, engaged in removing the roof tiles from the collapsed main hall. Some were dressed in kataginu (sleeveless formal wear) or tsunokakushi (a type of headwear), carrying tiles on their heads from the main hall to a storage area about one chō (approximately 109 meters) away, passing them hand to hand in a relay, with the lines of people stretching across more than ten rows. Since the tiles were handled one by one, neither the old nor the young labored excessively in carrying them. Others were sorting and bundling with rope the scattered roof planks, bamboo, and timber that had been piled up. The crowd was beyond counting — like a swarm of ants. Furthermore, believers made offerings labeled "go-myōgakin" (votive donations), with name tags affixed, and many donations of ten gold coins or more could be seen. Moreover, the amount donated anonymously was said to be ten times that of the posted donations — truly, the prosperity of this sect can well be imagined.

**[Left page]**

◯ In Teppōzu, damage to houses in Minatochō was extensive. In Funamatsu-chō, people were swept away about one and a half chō by the storm surge, and many were unaccounted for. In Tsukudajima, the entire West Quarter (Nishi-machi itchō) was devastated; the chigi (crossed finials) of the Sumiyoshi Myōjin shrine were blown away and lost. The Eitai Bridge was pushed and swept away seven to eight ken (approximately 13–15 meters) eastward from its center by the storm, knocked down by large vessels; furthermore, one section of the bridge pilings on the western side was broken through, halting traffic and making ferry boats necessary as a substitute crossing. Along the Ōkawabata, the residences of Suganuma, Akimoto, and Sano suffered considerable damage. From in front of the Andō residence to the Ōhashi bridge, twelve to thirteen large tea-transport boats (ōchafune) were driven ashore; a ship of 300–400 koku capacity was smashed, with only its stern blown up, while the mast was carried all the way to Hakozaki. In addition, three bridge pilings were damaged, and wrecked boats were scattered at various points along the main road.

◯ In Hamachō, the residences of the Sakai, Mizuno, and Manabe clans, as well as many smaller estates, were damaged. This area was also struck by the storm surge, which rose two shaku, four to five sun (approximately 73–76 cm) above floor level. The fire-watch tower at Hetsutsuigashi had its roof blown off, with no trace of it to be found. Matsushimachō was entirely damaged and severely affected, while Ningyōchō-dōri suffered no particularly notable damage.
