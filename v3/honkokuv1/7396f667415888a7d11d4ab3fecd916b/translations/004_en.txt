There may also be monks — not quite a Tōren — who say, "Life is in peril, but let us wait for a break in the weather and then venture out." And there may well be some reckless fellow, quite unlike Shūko, who carries off another man's wife as though she were his own. The blind stumble upon (the damage) with their own hands, the deaf catch wind of it with someone else's ears, and all is thrown into the most tremendous uproar, with everything turned upside down. To list it all out: driftwood floating on the water, roof planks scattered across dry land — too many to count. The sorrow of parting from loved ones (*aibetsuriku*) — the anguish suffered by monks, laypeople, men, and women alike — is, in broad strokes, as described above; and so I set down this outline at the beginning of the volume.

On the sixth day of the tenth month of Ansei 3 (1856), the year of Hinoto-Tatsu (*Heishin*), beginning on a rainy evening when the rain fell heavily, I wetted my brush; and on a morning when a strong earthquake shook the ground, trembling and quaking, I completed this draft.
