(Wind constitutes the very substance of *qi*.) Since the grasses, trees, flowers, and fruits follow the seasons without fail, and since the growth of all the hundred things depends equally upon wind and *qi*, the respiration of the human body must be one and the same as the breath of Heaven and Earth. To reason from this: that disasters and extraordinary changes occur between Heaven and Earth is no different from the fact that illness and pain befall human beings — there is no need to treat it as something remarkable. That said, peace and tranquility are the norm, while disasters and sicknesses are, on the contrary, rare. Not to be surprised by what is commonplace, yet to find remarkable what is rare — such is the universal disposition of people's hearts, and it is from this that wondrous tales and strange accounts arise.

At this time, in the reign of our Imperial Court, in the autumn, on the twenty-fifth day of the eighth month of the third year of Ansei, the year of Hinoe-Tatsu (1856), a misty drizzle had been soaking the ground from early morning. But toward dusk, the wind shifted to the southeast (*tasumi*, the direction of the *Xun* trigram), blowing up in a desolate, gathering gust. That night, from around half past the Hour of the Dog (approximately 8:30 p.m.), the rain grew ever more fierce, pouring down as though flushing a cart axle. By the time the Hour of the Boar (around 10:00 p.m.) had passed, the wind and rain were overwhelmingly violent; black clouds swirled and descended through the air, lightning flashed in all four directions out of the darkness, and thunderclaps boomed and reverberated without cease. Trees were sent flying, sand and stones were swept up, warehouses and rooms swayed like small boats tossed on crashing waves, and palaces and towers shook like pampas grass swaying across a withered field. Wooden shutters flung themselves open on their own, and latticed screens —

— were torn away together with the *shōji* sliding panels, leaving only their traces on the lintels and threshold boards. Walls were hollowed out, returning to their original mud and earth; pillars snapped at every joint, splintering into fragments; rooftops were blown off and fluttered in the void; roof tiles rained down like fallen leaves. Earthen walls crumbled and filled in the moats, and wooden fences naturally formed bridges spanning the rushing floodwaters. It was as though Heaven and Earth themselves were being overturned — people trembled and shook with terror, feeling no sensation of being alive at all.

Moreover, from the direction of the open sea along the coast came a rumbling like the blowing of a hundred thousand conch shells; the surging waves seemed to drench the heavens, and the many ships appeared as if racing through the clouds. And it was at precisely that moment that a great wave of over five *jō* in height surged up and swallowed the fishing villages whole. Alas — such is fate! That such calamity and misfortune should be visited upon the land in this enlightened reign!

In particular, along the Tōkaidō, from Tōtōmi Province's Kakegawa through Nissaka, Kanaya, and as far as the Ōigawa River, while things were not as catastrophically severe, the toppling of dead trees and destruction of old houses was of a character unlike that of an ordinary great windstorm. As for the Ōigawa River — since it has always been the case that the water rises with a south wind and recedes with a north wind, I leave that too for people to consider carefully. One *ri* downstream from the Ōigawa, there is a place called "Iro" (name unclear in the original), where, it is said, a great many drowned bodies washed ashore.
