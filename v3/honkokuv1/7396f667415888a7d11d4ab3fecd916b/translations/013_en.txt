According to a man named Izumiya of Shinchi-machi in Nishi-Uraga: during the previous tsunami, the sea had been roaring incessantly, and even the color of the sun was ominous, so everyone went down to the water's edge to see what was happening. But as nothing particularly alarming occurred, they returned inside, and he had settled back to writing in his ledger. Then, feeling the water lapping quietly against his sleeve, he thought, "How cold," and rose to his feet, brush still in hand — only to find that water had already risen inside his house. He could do nothing but stand there in a daze. When the water finally receded, everything he had within reach had been swept away, leaving only himself behind. Such was the strangeness of it. Afterwards, he heard that some houses had been carried whole into the sea and sunk. Another person, upon hearing the cry of "tsunami!", had hoisted his master's armor chest onto his back and fled up the mountain — but the tide was already lapping at his feet, and thinking "I cannot survive this," he opened the lid of the chest, took out only the money inside, and, now unencumbered, ran up the slope, barely managing to save his life. Afterwards, it was found that his master's helmet had been caught and left hanging high up in the branches of a pine tree — a story that, even just to hear, made one's flesh creep. it was not so violent as that, yet being in the middle of the night, it caused people all the more distress. Fortunately, there were no drowning deaths among the people. This was presumably not a tsunami, but rather the result of a great tide being driven up by southerly winds.

◯ In the area of Kanazawa and Honmoku, ownerless boats were blown in, and in the process damaged the moored vessels that had been tied up there. This was likely due to the great size of the boats that were driven in.

◯ Along the main highway, in the areas of Hakone, Odawara, Ōiso, and Hiratsuka — all places close to the sea — great numbers of roads, bridges, and houses were found to be damaged. In Fujisawa, Totsuka, and Hodogaya, there was for the most part no great devastation to be seen; yet wind has its own particular paths and currents, and unexpectedly large houses were found to be damaged, and trees that appeared too great to fall had toppled — so one cannot make any blanket statement about the damage.

◯ On the road from Odawara, passing through Kaza-matsuri and then from Tatsuta heading not toward Yumoto but directly toward Tō-no-sawa, there had been a large bridge; this had been swept away by mountain floodwaters and carried out as far as the riverbank.
