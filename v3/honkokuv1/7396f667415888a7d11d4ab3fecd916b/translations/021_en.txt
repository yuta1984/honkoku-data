Enormous quantities of timber came floating down the waterways. These were likely materials that had been stacked along the riverside embankments around Hatchobori and Sanjikkenbori, and had been swept up and carried along by the storm surge. From that point to Nihonbashi along the Ryonaka-dori, and further along Honcho-dori, Jikkendana (east and west), and from Imagawa Bridge into inner Kanda, there were some collapsed buildings, but conditions were largely comparable to normal times, and so a detailed account is omitted here.

Ansei Rumors Collection, Volume 2

◯ The following is recorded:

◯ In Tsukiji, from the area of the Ohama Detached Palace (御浜御殿) to Samui Bridge, many residences—both samurai estates and townspeople's homes—were blown down by wind and rain, inundated by the tide, and a considerable number of people lost their homes. Among these, the main hall (hondo) of Nishi Honganji temple collapsed and crumbled. Nevertheless, not a single person was injured, and most remarkably, the principal image of Amida Buddha was safely removed from its shrine cabinet (zushi).

　The main hall of the Monzeki (the head temple's precinct) had been renovated with great care and was a magnificent and imposing sacred site. Its columns and foundation stones were known by all to be of exceptional sturdiness. Even during the great earthquake on the night of the 2nd day of the 10th month of the Year of the Hare (Ansei 2, 1855), only a few roof tiles had fallen and there had been no significant damage. However, during the great storm of the 8th month of this year (Ansei 3), the temple's worshippers—young and old, still shaken by the memory of the earthquake—abandoned their tilting homes and fled the dwellings being swept away by floodwaters, taking refuge in this hall. They felt as though they had "encountered the Buddha in hell," trusting in Amida's vow of salvation.
