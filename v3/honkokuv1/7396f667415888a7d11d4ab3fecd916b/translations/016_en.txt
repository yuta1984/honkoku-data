◯In Susuki Shinden, a coastal area from Kanagawa, there is a remarkable story. There was a wealthy farmer by the name of Tarōbee. After the earthquake in Edo in recent years, having been terribly frightened, and having no shortage of money, he immediately rebuilt his house: he removed the thick ridge beams and replaced them with thinner ones, took down the roof tiles and re-roofed with thatch. Everyone was astonished by the swiftness of his precautions. However, in this recent great storm, his roof was blown clean off in one piece; while other houses did not suffer so greatly, it is said that Tarōbee suffered more than anyone else. Furthermore, nearby there lived a man called Zensuke, who had been in poverty since his grandfather's time and could not afford proper construction. His storehouse and outbuildings were built on simple earthen-embedded posts, and the roof he had tied together himself with rope, barely keeping out rain, dew, and frost

and snow. Now, in this great storm, storehouses throughout the village were blown down, or had their roofs torn off, and on top of that, a storm surge came pushing in, dragging away the collapsed storehouses—so that many people lost their stored miso, pickled radish, and other light household goods and were left in great distress. Among all of this, Zensuke's storehouse, having been built on posts sunk into the ground, did not lose a single item, and the roof, which had been hand-patched and rope-bound from the start, suffered only minor damage, and he passed through safely. Reflecting on this, one recalls the saying that the thousand lanterns of the wealthy patron of Mount Kōya may at times grow dim, but the single lantern of the poor never goes out and burns bright. Even poverty has its virtue, and being wealthy can on the contrary bring about greater hardship. I have recorded this just as it happened, as a lesson for those who come after.
