However, around that time, when I went outside, I came upon three or four old women who appeared to be simple country folk, gathered together and talking about this very matter. One of the old women said, "This talk of a great earthquake tomorrow — who started saying such a thing? It doesn't seem credible at all." Another old woman replied, "I hear it was said by someone well versed in astronomy. We should not take it lightly." The first old woman laughed coldly and said, "Even if one is well versed in astronomy, what grounds does one have for knowing such a thing? If they truly could know the future, then why did they not say so at the time of the first earthquake, and thereby prevent so many people from coming to harm? This is not worth taking seriously." With that rebuke, the conversation was brought to a close. I listened to all of this from nearby, and though this old woman appeared to be an ignorant and simple country person, I thought that compared to that gentleman of letters, her judgment and discernment were far superior — and I left that place with a smile.

Thus it is that a person's judgment and discernment can largely be gauged from even a single word or half a phrase. One would do well to reflect carefully on the matter, as in the "Discourse on the Tiger and the Donkey" that I wrote some years ago.

　　On reflection, this is not limited to the present case. Whether it be a great thunderstorm or a great fire — whenever people are seized with terror, there are always those who cry out noisily: "On such-and-such a day, at such-and-such a time, there will again be a great thunderstorm, or a great fire. This is a divine oracle. Furthermore, it is what the shrine medium at such-and-such a shrine has divined." Yet such predictions have never once come to pass. Nevertheless, those who believe these delusions insist: "It is because of this divine oracle that a renowned monk at such-and-such a place offered prayers and drove away the thunder. And the fire was averted because the people were vigilant and watchful — that is how they escaped this calamity." Many people extol such things. While arguing with such people and wasting words in debate is ultimately a futile exercise, if one were to venture a word on the matter: thunder is produced by the clash of yin and yang forces — how could prayer possibly ward it off? However, there is the episode where, before Sugawara no Michizane, having become the deity of thunder, was about to make the heavens roar over the capital, word reached the monk Hōshōbō (Son'i) on Mount Hiei, saying: "Even if there is an imperial command, do not offer prayers." But Son'i replied: "To the very shores of this land, there is none who is not a subject of the sovereign. If an imperial command is issued, how could I possibly refrain from praying?"
