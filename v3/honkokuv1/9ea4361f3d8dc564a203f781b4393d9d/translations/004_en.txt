The manservant had gone out even earlier than she, and so passed through while the storehouse had not yet fallen, and was unharmed. There are many such cases, and people generally say of each that it was simply "what fate decreed at the time." But upon careful reflection, this is not so. As a child of her parents, to abandon them in their confusion, and upon seeing the hinged door standing open, to run off alone to escape danger — was this not, first and foremost, a sign of insufficient filial piety? This is the exact counterpart — the reverse side of the coin — of the bride of Senju recorded at the beginning of this volume. Had this daughter remained by her parents' side, they would all have escaped unharmed together; yet in her haste to flee, she brought about her own ruin. Those who are subjects and children should reflect well upon this.

　　A supplementary note: here is a remarkable thing. In Kami-Hirai, near the outskirts of Edo, there stands the Shōten-gū shrine, and many people from the city, young and old, make their way there in spring and autumn — it is a place well known to all. In this earthquake, the ground cracked open for a length of more than two *chō* (approximately 220 meters), to a width of about two *ken* (roughly 3.6 meters), and to an unknown depth. Those whose homes happened to stand upon this ground were half buried within it. As for clothing, furnishings, and the like that fell in, there is no prospect of their ever being recovered. Furthermore, the Nihon-zutsumi embankment at Shin-Yoshiwara and the embankment along the Sumida River also cracked — though not to any great extent. Beyond these, lands reclaimed by human labor, or embankments that had been built up, though the ground had hardened over the years, were almost without exception damaged to some degree. Perhaps this Kami-Hirai too was in former times the site of a filled-in river or the like. However, upon later inquiry, as many people knew nothing of this, perhaps it was not as extensive as described. The distance is not particularly great, but as I have not gone to see for myself, I cannot say whether it is true or false.

　　Nevertheless, considering ancient precedent in light of this: in the *Nihongi* (*Chronicles of Japan*), in the seventh year of Emperor Tenmu's reign, in the province of Tsukushi (Kyushu), the earth cracked open to a width of two *jō* (approximately 6 meters) and a length of three thousand *jō* (approximately 9 kilometers), and many dwellings collapsed. At this time, a certain farmhouse stood upon a hill, and that hill crumbled and shifted its position — yet the house itself remained intact. Those inside were unaware of what had happened, and in the morning light they saw it and were greatly astonished.
