The pomegranate instantly turned into flames, threatening to set the cedar door ablaze, but the monk Hosshobo (Zon'i) formed a mudra and subdued the fire. This event is recorded in the *Genko Shakusho* and is well known to people. However, the truth or falsity of this account is difficult to determine. Given this example, one cannot say that eminent monks are entirely unable to avert thunder through prayer. Nevertheless, many scholars of old have put forward various arguments and debated this matter at length. Similarly, the claim that people's vigilant watchfulness prevented a fire can be said to offer only a surface-level explanation. Even without such oracles, since losing one's home to fire brings loss of property, and in the worst case, loss of life, there is no one who would neglect to guard against fire hazards. And yet, when great fires do occasionally break out, perhaps there is indeed such a thing as heaven-ordained fate. One may consider what the Ming dynasty scholar Xie Zhaozhe wrote in his entry on the Shangyuan lantern festival: "Fire disasters have their own ordained fate; the pleasures of common folk in watching festivities are a sign of peaceful times," and so on.

　　　　　○On the Disturbance of Earth's Veins Before and After an Earthquake

It has already been recorded earlier that when the energy of the great earth becomes disordered, and the yin energy rises to the top and traps the yang energy below, and when the yang energy, unable to remain suppressed, attempts to break free, the earth naturally swells up, much like a rice cake being toasted over a fire. As the ground moves in this way, the earth's veins naturally become disordered. Consequently, the water in wells may sometimes rise and sometimes fall, deviating from its normal level.

Now, before the great earthquake of the second day of the tenth month of last year (1854), there was a water teahouse called "Fukudaya" in front of the Asakusa Okura (the shogunate's rice storehouse). A person arrived there by palanquin, and as the palanquin bearer wandered about the garden, he noticed a slightly sunken spot in the ground. Without thinking much of it, he prodded it with his walking stick, whereupon clear water immediately began to gush forth abundantly. The proprietor of the teahouse saw this and was greatly astonished. He went over and dug around the spot, and the spring water gushed out all the more. People marveled at this as something most unusual, and crowds came to see it, making the place as lively as a marketplace. The proprietor removed the bottom of a barrel, placed it over the spring as a makeshift well, and drew water from it to brew tea — and its flavor was exceedingly fine. Everyone who saw and heard of this said it was simply a mysterious occurrence...
