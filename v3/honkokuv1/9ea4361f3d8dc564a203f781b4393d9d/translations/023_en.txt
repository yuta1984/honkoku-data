**[Further Observations on the Direction of Earthquakes]**

As illustrated in the diagram to the right, one may well ask whether a great earthquake capable of shaking an area of two hundred *ri* in all directions has ever occurred in Japanese or Chinese history, ancient or modern. And yet even such a great earthquake is of this scale. How much more so, then, for lesser tremors. As stated previously, since an earthquake originates at a certain point and resonates outward in all directions, there is in fact no such thing as shaking "coming from a particular direction." However, the area near the center is strongly affected, and the intensity gradually diminishes toward the periphery.

It is my belief that the recent Edo earthquake had its center within Edo itself. Within Edo, the area from Asakusa through Honjo and Fukagawa served as the epicentral zone, while the upland areas (*yamanote*) such as Ichigaya, Ushigome, and Ōkubo represented the outer periphery. Edo is a place where earthquakes have been rare, and approximately one hundred and sixty years had passed since the great earthquake of the sixteenth year of Genroku (1703); therefore, no one remained who had any memory of it, and few even knew of it by oral tradition. Because it is a prosperous city, fires have occurred repeatedly since ancient times, and so people had taken great care in their preparations against fire — but they had paid no attention to earthquakes. For this reason, it is said that many lives were lost in this natural disaster. From this point forward, those who are prudent would do well to choose carefully both the construction of their homes and the location of their residences.
