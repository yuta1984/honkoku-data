**[Eyewitness Account of the Earthquake]**

(Continuing from the previous account) It is entirely understandable, for when this great tree swayed and bent down, its branches touched the ground, and when it swung back upright, they reached halfway to the sky. Therefore, had one been struck carelessly by its trunk, one's body would have been crushed to pieces — the danger was beyond description. Yet by great fortune, one escaped unharmed. Those who had fallen directly to the ground could not rise again at all. At that very moment, the earth split open greatly and spewed forth mud and sand, so that everyone was covered from head to toe in mud, and one could not distinguish one person's face from another. When the shaking finally ceased, though one tried to stand up with great effort, one could only stagger dizzily and was unable to walk, like a person who is severely drunk. Then, approximately half an hour later, an aftershock came, though it was somewhat milder compared to the initial quake. Thereafter, the shaking continued innumerable times, moment by moment. Considering all this, the Edo earthquake, fierce as it was, would amount to perhaps seven parts out of ten if the Sunpu earthquake were taken as ten parts. It was a most terrifying thing, the witness said.

**[On the Direction of Earthquakes]**

When an earthquake occurs, some say the shaking came from the east, while others say it came from the west. The same applies to north and south. Wondering where the source of such shaking might be, it is not like thunder that emanates from mountains and reverberates through the villages. An ancient text states: "The circumference of the earth is ninety thousand *ri*." Since this is a Chinese measurement using six *chō* per *ri*, converting to the Japanese *ri* of thirty-six *chō*, the circumference becomes fifteen thousand *ri*. Accordingly, the distance from the earth's center to its surface is two thousand five hundred *ri*. Now, even the most violent earthquakes in Japanese and Chinese history, both ancient and modern, have their vibrations extend generally no more than two hundred *ri* in all directions. As for the recent Edo earthquake, it may have reached as far as one hundred *ri* in all directions. In this vast globe, such a minor movement of the earth does not come from the east, west, north, or south — rather, there is an original source of the tremor, from which it resonates outward in all directions, with the areas experiencing only slight vibrations being the periphery.
