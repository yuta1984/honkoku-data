Since he was only trapped from around the thighs, rescuing him should be simple enough—and with that, seven or eight people immediately gathered and took hold of the man from front and back. The man himself, desperate to free himself as quickly as possible, stretched out his hands and clung to the people around him. The rescuers exerted every ounce of their strength, and with the force that only a large group can muster, they pulled him out in one swift motion. The body did indeed come free without resistance—however, since the corner of the wood from the threshold and beam had been biting deep into the flesh of his thighs, and he had been wrenched out by sheer force, the flesh from his thighs down to the tops of his feet was torn away and left behind in the wreckage, leaving only bare white bone. Blood poured forth like a waterfall, and his groaning and suffering were beyond all ordinary description. His wife and children had been present and, thinking him saved, rushed over joyfully—only to be confronted with this dreadful sight, and were struck anew with shock and horror, utterly at a loss as to what could be done. The people who had rescued him were equally dumbfounded. With nothing else to be done, they first laid out a tatami mat on the spot and helped the injured man onto it, then raised a clamor for a doctor and medicine—but as everywhere was in an uproar, medicine could not be bought and no doctor would come. And even had a doctor come, what means of treatment could there have been?

As time passed, the injured man, who at first had perhaps been numbed, gradually became aware of the full agony of his wounds and began to wail, scream, and weep in anguish—a scene that called to mind, if one were to describe it, the illustrations of hell in Buddhist didactic paintings, specifically the tortures of being chopped and ground, so vivid was the resemblance. He raised his blood-soaked hands and repeatedly stroked his own face and head, until there was not a spot left unstained with blood; his eyes, nose, and even his mouth could no longer be distinguished, and one could only wonder whether the figure before them was a demon or a human being. Any woman or child who happened to catch a glimpse of him could not help but tremble with terror. Thus, on the following day, he died in a paroxysm of agony and delirium.

This is precisely what was said at the outset: it is because of a habitual lack of mental composure that such terrible mistakes come to be made. As for the man himself, there is no point in debating questions of right or wrong. But among the seven or eight people who came to rescue him, those who failed to perceive so obvious a danger were surely not all fools—rather, their own bodies were still seized with terror from the great earthquake, their composure not yet restored, and in their frantic haste, the wisdom and judgment needed to consider the consequences was hidden from them...
