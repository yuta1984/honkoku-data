The hanging lamp swayed and went out, so I immediately kicked open the door; the household's mother had already grabbed a small child and gone out into the garden. I intended to get Junzaburō and Yukichi out to safety, but I fell over twice myself; the single maidservant attempted to get both Junzaburō and Yukichi out together, but she too had fallen, so I assisted her, and we finally managed to get all three of them outside. The remaining two maidservants had been lodging in another room and had vanished completely, as if swallowed up — everyone assumed they must be dead by now and was weeping. From outside, we called out loudly again and again, "Come out! Come out!" but they did not emerge; since we could hear their crying voices, I plunged back inside, but it was pitch dark and I could only follow the sound without being able to locate them. At last I seized one by the hand and threw her out through the entrance, then did the same for the second, and finally felt some relief. But then voices were heard again from inside — calling out "Who is it? Who is it?", I realized that Junzaburō, who had been the first one I had gotten out, must have gone back in half-asleep, thinking he was going to find the old woman; so I plunged inside once more, and in the darkness finally got him out again. We spread wooden doors across the garden as a floor and laid everyone down on them; then at last I went back inside to retrieve my daishō (long and short swords). That night the earth shook greatly some eighty times or more. It seems the shaking continued at considerable length, but amid the

commotion described above, I was entirely unaware of it. Hearing afterward, I was told that Katsuzō and others had come out to the front and looked out at the scene early on, and that the ridge of the roof was swaying back and forth like great waves — a terrifying sight. The ground cracked open slightly here and there. Contrary to expectations, few houses were completely destroyed. The earthen warehouse walls crumbled considerably. However, Nakanojō appears to have suffered the lightest damage of anywhere. For the past two days and two nights up to today, I have been living in a makeshift shelter set up in the garden. Going forward, the tremors seem to have been growing more distant, and I expect they will stop within two or three days — there is no need to worry. Even though things have become considerably calmer overall, there are still seven or eight large tremors and fifty to seventy small ones. I expect to hear further accounts as news arrives. The story of Zenkoji is an extraordinary catastrophe, said to be unprecedented. Even along the river, groups of a hundred or fifty old women pilgrims were all crushed and then burned to death — it seems a dreadful time we have encountered, yet there is much to learn from it for the future. I have heard that in ancient times hanging lamps were widely used — it would appear that this was a period when people had learned their lesson from earthquakes.
