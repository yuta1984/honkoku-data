Inside their homes (or what remained of them), the people had made no arrangements to shelter themselves from the rain and dew, and day after day they wandered in despair, utterly dazed. Upon catching sight of me (the magistrate), they fell into a panic, weeping incessantly and unable to stop their tears; some collapsed in anguish and, when questioned, were too overcome to answer, lying prostrate on the ground. The common villagers — old and young, men and women alike — wailed and cried aloud, while the injured lay in great numbers, suffering in agony. Words cannot suffice to convey the scene; it was a picture of the utmost pitifulness, and one could only grieve deeply. Every village was in the same condition. Those who had stores of food found them buried beneath the ruins of their collapsed houses, and moreover drenched in the mud water, making it impossible to retrieve them easily. As for the lowest ranks of the common people who had no food provisions whatsoever, their situation was even more dire. The water supply, which had come from irrigation channels, was now mixed with mud, and people were suffering from both hunger and thirst. As it was naturally impossible for the villages to provide mutual assistance on a general basis, I arranged as much relief and food aid as I could on that very day — yet with over one hundred villages affected, it was utterly beyond my personal means to reach the entirety of the area and its distant corners. Even those with some means of their own had been struck by the disaster of collapsed houses, and so any particularly resourceful measures were

**[Left folio]**

likewise impossible to arrange. With no alternative, I released the stored grain (kakoi-koku) from the village granaries (gōzō), dispatched my clerks (tedai) to make arrangements and circulate among the villages, and in this way the people are being helped to endure. For the villages nearest to the magistrate's office (jin'ya), temporary shelters have been erected within the precincts of temples and shrines in Nakano Village and Matsukawa Village, and those in the most extreme distress are being provided with relief there.

Furthermore, as I progressively compiled reports on the deaths of persons and livestock and the injuries sustained in each village, the findings were as follows: 578 men and women killed; 1,460 injured — among whom there are many who have become permanently disabled (katawa) and are unable to continue farming or earning a livelihood. Two cattle found dead; 156 horses found dead. Beyond this, there were those who had come to worship at Zenkōji Temple and were lodging there on the night of the 24th day of the 3rd month when fire broke out due to the earthquake; more than 200 men and women burned to death, with great numbers perishing in this way.

For the villages that suffered disaster, the population has decreased by 2.7% (two-bu seven-rin). Of the total assessed yield (kokudaka) of over 58,300 koku under my administration, the undamaged villages account for no more than 30% (three-bu) of the total assessed yield, while over 70% (seven-bu) belongs to the disaster-stricken villages — a truly lamentable state of affairs.
