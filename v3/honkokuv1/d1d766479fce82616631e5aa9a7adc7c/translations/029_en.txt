As it was the middle of the night, he had no idea what was happening, and called out loudly, shouting his colleague's name, but there was no answer. After calling out repeatedly, he finally heard a faint responding voice, and (Nishimura Kakunai) crawled out from beneath the door (rain shutters). The two men met face-to-face and rejoiced at each other's safety. The earthquake continued without letup. The house they had been living in was a separate detached building (*betsuka-date*), roofed with tiles. The main house was considerably further away and, it is said, was a large thatched-roof (*ashi-buki*) building. This main house had been on the verge of collapsing, but there was a large pine tree behind it, and the house fell against it sideways and was stopped from fully collapsing. The row-house (*nagaya*) had completely collapsed. The retainers of Lord Hida-no-Kami who resided in the row-house had gone outside and were uninjured. Unlike any ordinary earthquake, it was as though a great cannon had been fired all at once; from the mountain collapse, clouds of dust rose up, and the fires breaking out in various places were reflected in the clouds — the spectacle was so terrifying as to be beyond description. All was confusion, and people were beside themselves. Eventually making their way to the ruins of the collapsed house, and amidst the continuing tremors, they removed the roof tiles and retrieved the official documents (*goshōmon*) and large and small official boxes (*goyō-bako*). They thought themselves fortunate that the place had not caught fire, but upon looking later, they found that a fire had been kept in the brazier (*hibachi*) with a large earthenware pot suspended above it; however, that

large pot had cracked, extinguishing the fire in the brazier, and the paper lantern (*andō*) had also tipped over and gone out — both, they said, were strokes of good fortune. The following day, around noon, rice balls (*nigirimeshi*) were brought from a place four or five *ri* away, and they each ate one or two, though they had not even been aware of how hungry they were. As for Zenkōji Temple-town, a pre-existing census register (*ninbetsu*) was consulted, and the surviving residents were checked — only some two hundred people remained in the entire town. When the travelers lodging there that night were also counted, the total number of deaths came to approximately thirty thousand persons. However, this figure includes some who survived and immediately returned to their home provinces, and there may be local residents who had fled and would later return; so this figure represents the number of people confirmed missing or dead at that point in time. The area in which the shaking was violent extended roughly fifteen *ri* east to west and eight to nine *ri* north to south; there must have been great numbers of collapsed houses and ground fissures throughout. The details of the mountain collapses and the damage in the more remote interior areas were unknown, and (the speaker) acknowledged that what he was saying about those areas was essentially conjecture. During the shaking, a terrible stench arose that defied description. Ground fissures were fewer in the rice paddies and more numerous in the dry fields (*hatake*); from the gaps and cracks that opened up, mud and sand were belched forth.
