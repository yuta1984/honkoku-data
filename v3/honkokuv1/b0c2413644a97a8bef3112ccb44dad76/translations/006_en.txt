How the hundred craftsmen flourish and prosper! Farmers do not miss the proper seasons, and the five grains ripen in abundance. Truly, this silently accords with the inherited methods of the age of Tang and Yu (the sage-kings Yao and Shun). We, living in this age of great peace, with each person fulfilling their duty and each household diligently pursuing their livelihood, find that their hearts become broad and serene, and they naturally become one body with Heaven, Earth, and the ten thousand things. How fortunate this is! How fortunate this is!

In Kiyo (Nagasaki), there was a man known as the reclusive scholar (*shoshi*) Nishikawa Joken. From youth he was fond of the study of astronomy; he widely investigated a great many books, and sought out Chinese people far and wide in order to measure and test astronomical instruments and other devices, thereby observing the degrees and numbers of the sun, moon, stars, and constellations. He examined the advance and retreat of the *qi ying* (the excess of the solar year) and *shuo xu* (the deficit of the lunar month), and, accumulating years of effort, brought his work to fruition. He then took what he had gained in his mind and ultimately wrote it up in various books to instruct others. His reputation and fame spread and overflowed in all four directions. In a previous year, he came briefly to Kōto (Edo), and thereafter returned to his hometown, where he ended his days at the allotted span of heaven. Ah, what a remarkable man he was!

His son, Masayasu (Seikyu), inherited his father's work and is likewise accomplished in his learning. In recent years, he traveled east [to Edo] and regularly came and went among the community of scholars (*shinrin*), teaching astronomy (*tengaku*). It is said that none who heard him failed to be won over by his words. He once composed the *Tengaku Meimoku-shō* (A Digest of Astronomical Terminology) and presented it to his disciples who were studying under him. [continued on the next page]
