　　Second Preface　　　　　　　　　　　Written by Lú Zhōng Rén, Yú Fēng Zhī

Zhuangzi said: "What lies beyond the Six Directions (*liùhé*) — let it be acknowledged as existing, but do not theorize about it. What lies within the Six Directions — theorize about it, but do not presume to judge it." Zhuangzi delighted in writing in a playful, paradoxical, and enigmatic style (*páixié guǐjué*). It is entirely fitting that, regarding the true forms and fixed numbers of the universe, he should have set them aside and not spoken of them.

Zouzi (Zou Yan) said: "What the Confucians call 'China' (*Zhōngguó*) represents but one eighty-first of the world. It occupies only one of these parts. China is called *Chìxiàn Shénzhōu* (赤県神州, the 'Red County Divine Continent'). Beyond it, there are nine others like *Chìxiàn Shénzhōu*, and then there is the Great Ocean (*Dà Yíng Hǎi*) encircling them, reaching to the ends of heaven and earth." Zou Yan spoke of the vastness of the earth, noting that humans, birds, and beasts cannot pass between these regions, and that as for the sun, moon, and stars of Heaven — he could not speak to those. Yet if the earth is as vast as this, the vastness of Heaven can be inferred by the mind.

Qi Bo said: "The earth exists within Heaven; the great atmosphere holds it aloft." Qi Bo assisted the Yellow Emperor (*Huángdì*) in regulating the calendar, prior to the time when Yao and Shun aligned governance with the *xuán jī* (璿璣, the celestial instrument / armillary sphere). Might not these words of his be the very origin of those who discourse on Heavenly learning (*tiānxué*)?

However, the rulers of ancient times, when they observed the stars and determined the seasonal energies (*qì*), confined themselves entirely to the domain of the "suspended images" (*xiàn xiàng*, the observable celestial phenomena). They illuminated the principles and numbers (*lǐ shù*) of heaven and earth, and, taking up the "balance of the central five" (*zhōng wǔ zhī héng*), aligned it with the four extremities (*sì jí*). Moreover, the written characters of high antiquity were simple and concise, and the intent of their words was broad and comprehensive. They feared that the reckless might wander into the vast and incoherent (*guāngyáng*), and that the unrestrained might drift into the wild and remote. Therefore, they concealed in mystery the subtle beginnings (*duānjī*) of these matters, so as to provide a foundation for empirical observation (*zhì cè*). Over the span of a thousand years, the degree to which the illuminated mind (*shénmíng*) can penetrate these things depends upon the individual. As for the limits of what the ears and eyes can reach — it is best simply to hold to one's established norms (*cháng*).

I, formerly, had the occasion to hear Master Jie Zixuan of Guangchang discourse on Heaven, and I was deeply impressed by the profundity and breadth of his thought. Zixuan strongly recommended You Ziliu of Tanyang. Ziliu composed the *Tianjing Huowen* (天經或問, *Questions and Answers on the Classics of Heaven*). His arguments and those of Zixuan mutually illuminate and develop one another.
