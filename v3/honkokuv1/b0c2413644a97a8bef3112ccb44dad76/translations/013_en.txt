(...continued from the previous page) When the delusion of transmigration (*zhuǎnshì*, metempsychosis) is refuted and dispelled, one becomes truly capable of penetrating the nature of the obscure and the manifest (the worlds of the dead and the living), and of understanding the doctrine of life and death. That this work has already been called a "classic" (*jīng*) is therefore entirely fitting. The saying goes: "One who comprehends Heaven, Earth, and Man is called a Confucian." I would say the same of Master Ziliu.

Alas! Scholars of deviant learning and heterodox persuasions fabricate apocryphal prognostication texts (*chénwěi*) and give free rein to their far-fetched and bizarre interpretations. The official astronomers (*táiguān*) and calendar specialists (*chóurén*) cling to the master-transmitted doctrines of their omen-observation schools and are incapable of illuminating with divine clarity the reasons underlying them. And then there are the scholars who wear round caps and curved shoes — men who merely pick up and repeat the dregs and leavings of their predecessors, while congratulating themselves on having grasped the essential meaning of astronomy. They, too, are like Fengyi (馮夷, the River Earl) boasting of his own greatness before Ruohai (海若, the Spirit of the Sea). They are not worth speaking of — not worth speaking of at all.

Someone might say: "This book too is simply the self-composed words of a single school of thought, provided merely for the benefit of discussion and selection by later generations under Heaven." How petty and trifling (*miǎohū*) such words are!

Having myself observed Master Ziliu, I believe that he has, generation after generation, inherited the legacy of his ancestor, Master Zhishan (廌山先生), and his deep immersion in the family tradition of learning is long-standing. It is for this reason that he is content in poverty and steadfast in conduct, that he exhausts the investigation of principle (*qiónglǐ*) and fully realizes his nature (*jìnxìng*), and, going further, he has pushed through to deduce the compendium (*xuǎn*) of the unity of Heaven and Man — arriving at it through his own independent achievement. If there exists such a person in the world, then that his work will be transmitted to all under Heaven and to later generations admits of no doubt.

I have already spoken my honest words; now I additionally present him with a poem, which reads:
　Directly splitting open the secrets of primordial chaos (*hóngméng*) — why should one say Heaven is remote and imperceptible?
　The Way has from the beginning transcended material measurement (*zhìcè*); Principle has already penetrated the subtle workings of transformation (*huàjī*).
　The *Línghàn* [Zhang Heng's astronomical treatise] has served as a guide and spur; the *Zhèngméng* [Zhang Zai's philosophical work] has relied upon its elucidation.
　The astronomical observations of past and present have been in error; I know where this lost learning (*juéxué*) will return.

Accordingly, I have written this explanatory preface at the head of the text, in order to straightforwardly inform students of Heavenly learning (*tiānxué*).

　Second Preface (*Yòu Xù*) — Composed by Zhang Zilie Ergong of Leishan (𦬊山張自烈爾公氏)

The investigation of things (*géwù*) is the primary gateway of the *Great Learning* (*Dàxué*). If a person is able to apply himself to the path of the investigation of principle and human nature, then what he comes to know will rightly be no trifling matter, not to be regarded lightly.

In the *jiǎchén* year (甲辰), I entered Fujian (*Mǐn*) on account of publishing the *Zìhuì Biàn* (字彙辨). Passing along the way through Shulin (書林, a book trade district), I met Ziliu. I recalled that in earlier times, when I was residing in Jiangning (江寧, Nanjing), I had already heard of Ziliu's name and knew that he was versed in the study of Heaven (*tiānxué*) and skilled at astronomical observation (*cèyàn*).
