Along both banks of the Sai River there are two villages called Hirai and Nagai. Rocks that had collapsed from both sides of the mountain in the aforementioned Yamanaka township blocked the Sai River; houses and standing trees were left atop the collapsed earth, presenting the appearance of a great embankment having been constructed, spanning a width of approximately eight chō [roughly 870 meters], as if a natural mountain had suddenly appeared. Not a single drop of water was flowing downstream, while upstream the river had become like a lake, and several villages were submerged. Water accumulated over a width of six or seven chō [roughly 650–760 m] or as much as fourteen or fifteen chō [roughly 1.5 km], and over a length of five or six ri [roughly 20–24 km]. Though more than ten days have passed since the 24th to today, the water has still not drained away. The site of the collapse stands approximately eighty to ninety ken [roughly 145–163 m] higher than the former normal water level, and as water continues to accumulate, the water surface is still more than thirty ken [roughly 55 m] above the top of the collapsed mass. It is said that because the weather has been clear and dry of late, the water level of the Sai River has been decreasing, and the water pooled in the wide area behind the dam is rising by about two or three shaku [roughly 60–90 cm] per day and night relative to the height of the mountain. Should heavy rain occur on top of this, the situation would be exceptional, but otherwise it is thought that within twenty or thirty days at the outside the dam will not break and the water will not rush down. Under these circumstances, however, the villages downstream have fallen into a panic, fearing that if the dam breaks all at once their villages will be annihilated; some residents have taken food and fled up into the mountains, while others have abandoned their villages entirely and fled elsewhere. In response to these reports, inspection officials were dispatched to observe the conditions upstream, and it was determined that in the current state the dam does not appear likely to break easily; and even if water were to leak through, it would likely flow out in a thin stream, like the spout of a kettle [chōshi-guchi], rather than as a catastrophic flood.

Item: Approximately two-tenths of the houses in the castle town of Matsushiro have collapsed, though the degree of damage varies by location. Across the domain as a whole, the number of collapsed houses and deaths is enormous; according to reports received so far, there are more than two thousand dead. The figures for the Yamanaka township have not yet been ascertained, but according to rumors among members of that domain, the deaths there may amount to approximately ten thousand persons.

Item: Zenkōji Temple was in the midst of its periodic kaichō [public display of the hidden sacred image] and memorial services, and an enormous number of pilgrims from all provinces had gathered there. When the earthquake struck suddenly that night, those attempting to flee did not even have time to take two steps before being crushed; of ten persons sharing the same lodging, six or seven perished, and in some cases entire households were killed to the last person, reportedly creating mountains of corpses. On top of this, fires broke out in three or four places and, driven by fierce winds, burned through the area, destroying approximately nine-tenths of the townhouses. Only the main hall (hondō), the main gate (sanmon), and the head temple residence (honbō) survived; everything else was consumed in the conflagration—a catastrophe without precedent in former times. From subsequent reports, of the eight thousand persons registered in the temple's domain, more than three thousand perished, and an unknown number of additional thousands died along the highway routes. Furthermore, approximately two thousand pilgrims are reported to have died.

Item: The castle town of the Iiyama domain and the village of Inariyama in the Ueda domain suffered the same fate as Zenkōji—earthquake followed by fire—and not only local residents but also the many traveling pilgrims lodging there perished in great numbers.
