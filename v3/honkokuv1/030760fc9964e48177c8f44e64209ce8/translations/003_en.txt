Along both banks of the Saigawa River, there are two villages called Hirai and Nagai. Rocks that collapsed from both sides of the mountains of that same district blocked the Saigawa River; the houses and trees alike now sit atop the collapsed earth, presenting the appearance of a great earthen embankment roughly eight chō in width. It is as though a natural mountain has appeared, with not a drop of water flowing downstream. Upstream has become like a lake, with several villages submerged. The water has accumulated over a width of six or seven chō, or in some places fourteen or fifteen chō, and stretching five or six ri in length. Though more than ten days have passed since the 24th, the water has still not drained. The location of the collapse is approximately eighty to ninety ken higher than the previous normal water level, and as the water continues to accumulate, the water surface is still more than thirty ken below the top of the collapse. Perhaps because clear weather has continued, the flow of the Saigawa has diminished, and the water pooled in that broad area is said to be rising at a rate of two to three shaku per day and night relative to the mountain's height. If heavy rain were to fall on top of this, the situation would be exceptional; otherwise, it is said that there is little chance of the water flowing away in fewer than twenty or thirty days. However, since word has spread that the villages downstream fear a sudden breach would annihilate the villages — with people carrying food and fleeing up into the mountains, or entire villages fleeing elsewhere in a panic — inspectors were dispatched to ascertain the conditions upstream. Their finding was that at present there appears to be no imminent risk of a sudden breach, and that even if water were to seep through, it would likely flow down gradually, as through the spout of a kettle.

Item: It is said that approximately two-tenths of the houses in Matsushiro castle town have been destroyed, though the extent of damage varies by location. Across the entire domain, there are enormous numbers of destroyed houses and deaths; reports received so far account for more than 2,000 persons. The figures for the aforementioned Yamanaka district have yet to be determined, but rumor among retainers of the same domain holds that the total deaths may amount to approximately 10,000 persons.

Item: Zenkōji Temple was in the midst of its periodic public display (kaikō) and memorial services, and there were enormous numbers of pilgrims from throughout the country. When the earthquake struck suddenly that night, those who tried to flee could not even take two steps before being crushed; in households of ten persons, six or seven died, and in some cases entire households perished without exception, piling up what was described as mountains of the dead. Furthermore, fires broke out in three or four locations and, fanned by fierce winds, burned through the area; nine-tenths of the town's merchant houses were destroyed. Only the main hall, the main gate, and the main lodging temple survived; everything else burned in the conflagration. It was described as an unprecedented catastrophe. As further reports come in, it appears that of the 8,000 persons registered in the temple's domain, more than 3,000 died; additionally, how many thousands perished along the roads cannot yet be determined; and approximately 2,000 pilgrims are estimated to have died.

Item: In the castle town of Iiyama domain and the village of Inariyama in Ueda domain, just as at Zenkōji, earthquake and fire combined, and not only local residents but also lodging pilgrims died in great numbers.
