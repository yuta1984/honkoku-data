Even if one were to govern the entire realm, what could possibly be difficult to accomplish? This is precisely why, in all things, such a person is reassuring — and it is the great guiding principle of the world, which everyone ought to follow.

Regarding the verse on "elder and younger brother" (*ani ototo*): the reader should note that this is a grammatical construction. The character for "respect/reverence" (*uyamahi*) is to be read as "respecting one's elder brother," attached to the character for "younger brother" (*ototo*); and the character for "nurturing/raising" (*hagukumu*) is to be read as "nurturing one's younger brother," attached to the character for "elder brother" (*ani*). This is a matter of literary grammar (*bunpō*). The meaning of the annotation can be understood from the preceding poem.

[Left folio]

[Upper section]

"Without negligence" (*tōkan naki*) means a spirit of care and attentiveness. With ordinary people, when they become close and familiar with someone, their sense of reverence invariably tends to fade — this is a common occurrence. Yan Pingzhong (*Anpeichū*) was praised by Confucius himself with the words: "He was skilled at associating with people, and the longer the acquaintance, the more he showed respect." This also connects to what is said in the *Quli* (*Kyokurei*): "The wise person, even when familiar, maintains reverence (*nare te kei su*)." In the *Tsurezuregusa*, there is the story of a monk from Ninnaji Temple who, in his eagerness to enjoy sake to the fullest, put a three-legged cauldron (*ashikanae*) on his head, only to find that he could not remove it, and it became a prolonged affliction — such instances are perhaps a result of straying into crooked paths (*sadō*). This should hold equally true in friendships: it ought to be the proper way.
