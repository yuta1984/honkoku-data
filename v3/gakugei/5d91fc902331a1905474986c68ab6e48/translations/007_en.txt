[Right folio]

[Upper section]

The seven emotions — joy, anger, sorrow, pleasure, love, hatred, and desire (*kidoairaku ai'yoku*) — are a path from which even sages and wise men cannot be separated. Therefore, it cannot be said that one will never feel anger or sullenness. To be angry at things worthy of anger, and to rejoice at things worthy of rejoicing, is the natural way of the sage. However, the cause of anger lies with that other party, and the cause of joy likewise lies with that other party. Why should one keep such things lodged in one's own heart? This is precisely the state of mind that Yan Hui (*Gangai*) exemplified when he was said never to transfer his anger onto others. The phrase "do not scatter so recklessly" (*sanomi chirashi so*) likely refers to this spirit of mind. The word *sanomi* carries the sense of "excessively" or "forcibly."

[Lower section]

In this world,
　　if one raises one's temper
morning and evening,
　　like the autumn leaves
　　　of Mount Tatsuta —
though not even leaves —
　　do not scatter them
　　　so recklessly.

[Left folio]

[Upper section]

The expression "atsukaisō" (*atsukahi kusa*) is akin to saying "matters that are troublesome and require careful handling." As long as one mingles with others in this world, one cannot manage without knowing how to deal with such difficult affairs. There are those who pay not even a drop of attention to their own affairs, yet put on airs of wisdom while meddling in the business of others — such people, who do not understand how troublesome this can be, will find that misfortune comes upon them suddenly. It is like the saying "when the lips are gone, one feels the cold," or like a neighbor's quarrel becoming a noise in one's own ears. If something bad befalls another person, one should swiftly step in and take care of that matter.

[Lower section]

One who knows
　　not even a dewdrop's worth
　　　of the world's
　difficult affairs —
　　treating them
　　　as lightly as grass —
is truly
　　a laughingstock
　　　indeed.
