　　　　Postscript

- Beyond these fifty poems, there are surely many other celebrated poems that serve as moral teachings, but in order to make them easy for children to recite and convenient for memorization by heart, I have set down my brush (and included no more).

- Although there are cases where multiple poems by the same author appear, following the model of the *Hyakunin Isshu* (*One Hundred Poems by One Hundred Poets*), which young children are already well acquainted with, I have titled this collection *Gojūnin Isshu* (*One Poem Each by Fifty People*). Where the poet is known, their name has been noted beside the poem. Where the poet is unknown, this should be investigated in due course.

- Even though the poems included are those whose moral reasoning is sound, accessible to children's ears, and easy to remember as lessons, there is a deliberate and considered reason for not including any *kyōka* (comic or satirical verse).

- Although there is no small amount of trepidation in placing the words of sages and wise men alongside the poems as paired titles, this was done out of a desire to lead young beginners back to the very source of the Six Classics.

- The reason the poem of the Kan Deity (Lord Sugawara no Michizane) is placed first is that young children hold this deity in the highest reverence. As for the remaining poems, no particular effort was made to establish an order among them.

　*Kokoro dani*
　*makoto no michi ni*
　*kanainaba*
　*inorazutotemo kami ya*
　*mamoramu*
