【Right Page】
《Kan Shōkō (Lord Sugawara, i.e., Sugawara no Michizane)》　*"Dare not look down upon others."*

　O garden snail —
　　do not be trodden upon
　　　by the calf!

　Even though you have your horns,
　　do not place
　　　your trust in them alone.

(O little snail in the garden — take care not to be trampled by the calf! Even though you have your horns, do not rely upon them as your sole protection.)

(The snail's tiny horns may seem like a kind of defense, but they are no match for the heedless weight of a calf. The poem carries a double lesson: do not be so overconfident in your own small advantages that you fail to perceive greater dangers; and equally, do not underestimate or look down upon others, however small they may appear. The classical injunction — *"dare not be contemptuous of others"* — cuts both ways.)

【Left Page】
《□□□□ (Author/source illegible)》　*"The truly great hermit does not shun the marketplace or the court."*

　Even should it be
　　that you cannot truly enter
　　　Yoshino —
　　　　that mountain
　　　　　of the longing heart —

　even so,
　　a hidden dwelling
　　　lies in the depths within.

(Even if you cannot withdraw in body to the famed depths of Yoshino Mountain, you need not despair — for a hidden dwelling exists in the depths of your own heart.)

(The classical Chinese phrase declares that the *truly* great recluse does not flee to the mountains; he carries his solitude within him, even while dwelling in the midst of the busy marketplace or the bustling court. This poem renders that idea into waka form: one need not literally seclude oneself in Yoshino's remote valleys — that beloved symbol of poetic retreat and spiritual withdrawal. True refuge is interior. The "hidden dwelling in the depths" is the quiet, untroubled place within one's own mind, available to those of cultivated spirit wherever they may be.)
