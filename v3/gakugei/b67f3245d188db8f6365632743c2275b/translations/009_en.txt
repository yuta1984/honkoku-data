[Right folio]

*(Poem)*
Pitiable indeed
　　are those who slander
　　　their lord and parents,
　　　　never once thinking
　　　　　that the fault
　　　　　　lies within
　　　　　　　themselves.

*(Commentary)*
When one who serves as a subject strives, without distinction between public and private, to excel in loyal service — rousing their spirit day and night, and dedicating themselves to their duty — there is no lord who would fail to look upon them with favor. Likewise, when one who is a child sets aside their own selfish desires, cherishes and reveres their father and mother, never goes against their parents' wishes, tends to them attentively, and serves them faithfully, there are no parents who would not rejoice. And yet, rather than striving in loyal and filial devotion, such people act willfully and indulge their own whims — and when their lord's manner or their parents' treatment falls short of their expectations, they turn instead to resentment and slander. This is truly a pitiable thing. If the lord's treatment is poor, or if the parents' provision is unsatisfactory, the cause ultimately lies within oneself. In all things, one must put oneself in another's place and must not blame others. As it is written in the *Analects of Confucius*: "The gentleman blames himself; the petty man blames others."

[Left folio]

*(Poem)*
How vain it is
　　to pray to the gods
　　　and buddhas
　　　　to grant their aid,
　　　　　while living
　　　　　　as one
　　　　　　　unfilial
　　　　　　　　to their parents.

*(Commentary)*
Those who devote themselves fully to filial piety toward their parents will receive the protection of the gods and buddhas even without prayer. For, to a child, one's parents are the very Way of Heaven. They are, moreover, the very gods and buddhas themselves. The Way of Heaven and the gods and buddhas are the father and mother of all living beings, and the Way of Heaven and the gods and buddhas are one and the same principle. Therefore, to be unfilial toward one's parents is to go against the will of the gods and buddhas, while to be filial toward one's parents is to accord with their will. If one truly wishes to accord with the will of the gods and buddhas, one must first serve one's parents well. And yet there are those who treat their parents as though they were of no account — neither loving nor revering them — and who nonetheless make their way to shrines and temples, bow their heads, and cast away their treasures in offering. What benefit can this possibly bring? It is said, "The gods do not receive improper ritual," and again, "To perform rites of sacrifice for a spirit that is not one's own is mere flattery."
