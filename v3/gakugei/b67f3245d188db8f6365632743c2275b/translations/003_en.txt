Preface to "The Pond of Pure Water"

The songs of woodcutters and the flutes of herdsmen are not rustic or vulgar things. The various proverbs circulating among the people, if one pauses to reflect upon them, serve simply to encourage virtue and admonish vice — this is their principle. The standard for such things lies entirely within oneself. Even the skillful paintings of Sōsei, though they may seem like something for herdboys to laugh at, scatter their seeds across a thousand years to come. How then could one look down upon or belittle the words of woodcutters and laborers?

[Left folio]
These one hundred poems, published as moral instruction, appear to be a work of long standing, and are said to have been composed by the Lord of Saimyōji (Hōjō Tokiyori) himself. Indeed, the spirit of the poems is unpretentious and unadorned, expressing things honestly and plainly, and there are no small number of accessible words suitable both for the upbringing of children and for the moral guidance of those of humble station.
