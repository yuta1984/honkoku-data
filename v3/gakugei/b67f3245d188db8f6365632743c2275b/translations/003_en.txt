Preface: The Pond of Pure Water

The songs of woodcutters and the flutes of shepherds are by no means rustic or vulgar. Even the proverbs of the common people, if one takes them to heart, immediately offer models for encouraging virtue and chastening vice. The principles thereof all reside within oneself. If even the masterful paintings of Sūsei serve to scatter seeds — things that may become objects of laughter among herdsmen — across a thousand years to come, then how could one presume to look down upon or belittle the words of woodcutters and charcoal-burners?

[Left folio]
This collection of one hundred instructional poems appears to be a work that has been in print for many long years, and is handed down as the personal composition of the Lord of Saimyōji. Indeed, the substance of the poems is unadorned and straightforward in its expression, and there is no shortage of accessible language suited both to the moral instruction of children and to the guidance of those of humble station.
