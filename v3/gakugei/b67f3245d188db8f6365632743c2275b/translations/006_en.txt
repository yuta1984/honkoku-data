[Right folio]

*(Poem)*
Never look down
　　upon a person
　　　　for their station
　　when they speak —
whatever it may be,
　　take what is good
　and make it
　　　　your own virtue.

*(Commentary)*
People in the world tend to dismiss what those of low rank and humble station say, even when they say something worthwhile, brushing it aside with "what does it matter?" — yet they lavish praise on the words of the wealthy and powerful, calling even trifling things "perfectly reasonable." This is foolishness indeed. As tradition tells us, even Yao and Shun — who stood upon the throne as emperors and possessed the virtue of sages — listened attentively to the words of woodcutters and grass-gatherers, setting aside what was wrong and adopting what was good. How much more, then, should ordinary people do the same? Whatever the source, if words accord with principle and conform to righteousness, one ought to follow and make use of them. As the proverb says, "Taught by one's own child, one crosses the shallow ford safely" — and indeed, is that not just so?

[Left folio]

*(Poem)*
When someone speaks
　　only out of a wish
　that you
　　may do well —
a word of counsel —
how foolish it is
　　　　if such words
　do not
　　　　enter your ears.

*(Commentary)*
In this world, a word of differing opinion or remonstrance is something that is offered only between those who are truly close at heart — it is never said lightly. And yet, to take such words as something bad, to rise up in anger, and to utterly refuse to hear them — this is truly foolish and lamentable. It is said: "As timber follows the marking line and becomes straight, so a person who heeds remonstrance becomes a sage." From ancient times, there are clear examples of those who heeded counsel and preserved their domains, and of those who rejected it and perished.
