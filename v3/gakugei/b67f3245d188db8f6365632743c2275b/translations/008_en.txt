[Right folio]

*(Poem)*
Without compassion,
　　without knowing
　　　gratitude,
the heart of one
　　who acts
　　　against all decency
　　　　　is beneath
　　　　　　even a dog.

*(Commentary)*
What it means to be human is, above all, to hold a heart of compassion and loving care — to revere those above, to win the affection of those below, to uphold righteousness, and to know gratitude. By contrast, one who is miserly and extravagant, who treats their lord and parents with contempt and disdain, and who exploits and torments those in their charge — such a person sinks lower even than the beasts, and it is a pitiable thing indeed. A dog faithfully recognizes its own master, grows close to them, wags its tail in joy, bears no grudge even when struck, and keeps watch through the night against thieves and wrongdoers. In knowing its master's kindness, loving its master, and faithfully carrying out its duties, the dog upholds a form of righteousness. Is it not shameful, then, that a person — a human being — should conduct themselves worse even than this? One ought to know one's own shame.

[Left folio]

*(Poem)*
Do not
　　slander as wicked
　　　the lord of the domain
in which
　　you find
　　　your place,
having received his favor.

*(Commentary)*
It is said: "One who dwells in a domain does not slander even its senior officials." By "senior officials" is meant the elder councillors (*karō*) and the various magistrates (*bugyō*). These serve as the lord's deputies and as overseers of the people; it is therefore right and proper to follow and honor them. How much more so, then, must this apply to the lord himself. Those who dwell in the countryside are called "subjects of the grass and thicket" (*sōmō no shin*); even if they receive no stipend or rice allowance, they are nonetheless subjects by virtue of receiving the benefits of their domain. Those who do not understand this principle reason as follows: "I till the fields myself, I ply my trade, I pursue my craft — I owe nothing to anyone." This is utterly foolish. It is true that farmers cultivate their fields, merchants conduct their trade, and artisans practice their crafts — each making their living through their respective labors. Yet if there were no administration and no laws laid down by the domain lord and his magistrates, there would inevitably arise among the people violent men who would seize the fields by force, rob others of their wealth, and oppress the people — leaving no time or peace for farming, commerce, or the crafts. Under such conditions, even if one had a wife, children, and family, they could not gather and live together in one place; even if one possessed wealth, there would be nowhere safe to keep it and no way to make use of it — it would become a burden rather than a blessing. In such a world, human society could not endure even a single day, and the human race itself would in time be extinguished. Reflecting on this, one sees that the sustaining of the human way depends entirely on the proper ordering of the relationship between ruler and subject, and between those above and those below. How immeasurable, then, is this debt of grace! Even if those in authority sometimes conduct themselves improperly, one must not slander or resent them. One should reflect deeply on this and understand it well.
