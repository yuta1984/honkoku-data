Confucius gave credence to children's songs, and Ban Gu took street gossip as a source. Popular accounts are not to be dismissed out of hand — and all the more so when there is tangible, real-world evidence to support them. The saying that even common, humble folk may possess remarkable insight must refer to precisely such cases as these.

△ A silk merchant from Kōshū province by the name of Zōsuke had set out from Kumagaya post-station on the Nakasendō highway on the second day of the tenth month of that year, making haste toward Edo. For some reason, however, progress along the road did not go smoothly that day, and by the time he reached Urawa post-station, night had already fallen. Nevertheless, as his commercial affairs demanded it, he pressed on through Warabi post-station and Itabashi, and when he reached the hollow known as Keiseigatsubo, it was already the Hour of the Pig (around 10 p.m.). Still he hurried on — when suddenly, from the northeast stretching toward the south, something bearing a bluish light within a mass of black vapor swept past with a roaring sound like a violent gale. No sooner had it vanished from sight than a tremendous rumbling shook the earth. Overcome with terror, he fell to the ground, and in that very moment the great earthquake struck, bringing the houses and storehouses of the surrounding area crashing down — it was as though his very viscera were being torn asunder. He said it felt like a dream, and he lost all awareness of what came before and after. Could this black vapor have been a precursor to the earthquake?

There were also people in Asakusa who witnessed something of the same nature passing through the sky. Whether it was the same phenomenon is unknown.
