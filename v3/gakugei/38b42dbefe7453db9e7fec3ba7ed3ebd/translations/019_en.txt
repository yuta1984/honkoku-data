△ In Nezu Shichikenchō there lived a man called Heikichi, who made his living as a rag-and-refuse dealer. His wife was named Katsu; his daughter Haru was seventeen years old that year and was an adopted child; and his younger daughter Kura, aged sixteen, was their natural-born child. Haru was both virtuous in character and pleasant in appearance, and was deeply devoted to her parents. Yet the couple treated her with contempt — as stepparents are wont to do — without recognizing the foolishness of their own natural child. Somehow it came to light what the couple had been secretly plotting, and the landlord, Jibei, summoned them both and said: "This daughter Haru is a deeply filial and dutiful child. As for the younger, Kura, since she is your own flesh and blood, perhaps you might do as you wish with her — but I understand you have already taken an advance payment for her. I will send word to have this matter settled and the money returned." Left with no choice, and seething with resentment, they nonetheless gave five *ryō* in gold to the elder daughter Haru to take with her. It was at precisely that moment that the earthquake struck. Heikichi's house was among the very first to collapse, and all three of the family — parents and child — were buried beneath the ruins. A fire then broke out in a neighboring house, and they all perished in the flames. Elder sister Haru, being at the landlord's house, escaped unharmed. This was surely the admonishment of the Heavenly Kings upon the couple's wickedness — their habitual cruelty toward a stepchild, and their intention, beyond that, to sell her into prostitution. One should fear such retribution; one should take heed.

△ Lord Matsudaira Izumo-no-Kami, of Ikenohata, judging that all manner of people must be suffering as a result of this earthquake and its fires, distributed to every household from Kayachō Second Block to Nezu Shichikenchō a gift of three *to* of polished rice and one *ryō* of gold apiece. A lord of great domain extends his relief even to the destitute of other domains; and similarly, among the townspeople too, those with the means to help have made donations, as recorded in this volume. It is said that the seed of good works yields ten-thousandfold — and this is by virtue of the divine protection of Brahmā, Indra, and the Heavenly Deities.

For the most part, [this area] is as if collapsed and burned to the ground alike. △ From Shinmachi to Shinjū, there was extensive damage; storehouses and earthen *kura* collapsed and are as if reduced to nothing. △ In the vicinity of Minowa, the shaking was extremely powerful, and the area is much as if collapsed and burned away. △ From Hotarusawa through Nippori, Sendagi, and as far as Tabata, there was extensive damage, with some collapsed structures. △ In the Somei and Sugamo areas, there was damage but few complete collapses. △ At the Ōji Gongen Shrine and its associated Inari shrine, the main sanctuaries suffered no harm, but there was damage within the precincts. Damage also extended to the Unaginawate and Motomachi areas. △ At the Yushima Tenjin Shrine, the roof of the main sanctuary was damaged, along with other structures. △ At the Tsumagoi Inari Shrine, there was damage within the precincts but no collapse. △ At the Kanda Myōjin Shrine, there was damage but nothing of particular severity. △ In the same area, both the upper residence (*kami-yashiki*) of Lord Takebe and the upper residence of Lord Naitō Bungo suffered collapses of their front tenement rows, with additional damage elsewhere. In the same area, the districts of Odaidokoro-chō, Dōbō-chō, and Kanazawa-chō all saw extensive damage to both samurai residences and townspeople's houses, with some collapsed structures. △ Along Yushima Street as far as Fifth Block, the structures on the front side suffered damage but nothing of particular gravity. In the Sakurababā area of the same district, some samurai residences collapsed. In the northern reaches — Kinsukedō and Harukichō — there was extensive damage and collapsed structures.
