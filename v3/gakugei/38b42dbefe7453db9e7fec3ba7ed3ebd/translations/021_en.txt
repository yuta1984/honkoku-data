△ In Shiroganechō, Ushigome, there lived a man by the name of Asano Yūji. He had formed a deep attachment to a courtesan called Kisegawa of the Daikoku-ya establishment in the Yoshiwara. Though some time had passed since they had last met, Kisegawa had been persistently sending letters urging him to come, and appeared to have urgent matters she wished to discuss in person. However, as Yūji was a devout everyday worshipper of the Bishamonten at Zenkokuji Temple in the same area, he went there first to pay his respects. On his way home, a lone traveling monk asked him, "Which way is Zenkokuji Temple?" and Yūji carefully gave him directions. The monk then told him: "You currently show signs of imminent calamity. Overall, you are inclined toward the shadow, which is most inauspicious." Yūji was deeply alarmed and asked how he might escape this fate. The monk replied: "As you appear to be a devout person who reveres the gods and Buddhas, I will share this with you. Even if you must go out for unavoidable reasons, return home before the late hours of the night. To spend the night away from home would be all the more inauspicious." Yūji gave a polite bow and returned home. While attending to this and that, various errands came up and he had occasion to go to Asakusa, and thinking it a convenient opportunity, he visited Kisegawa's quarters, where they held a drinking party and talked of things that had accumulated between them. Before long it had already reached the Hour of the Pig (around 10 p.m.). Suddenly remembering what the monk had told him, he took his leave, but Kisegawa urgently pressed him to stay. Nevertheless, he finally departed, and had come as far as Umamichi when the earthquake struck, giving him a tremendous fright. Looking toward the Yoshiwara, he saw fires breaking out in various spots within moments, and the crashing sounds of things collapsing were terrible to hear. He hurriedly turned back, rushed to the Daikoku-ya, and rescued Kisegawa and five others. Had he spent the night there, he would surely have met a violent death; that he was saved so miraculously must be attributed entirely to the virtue of his faithful devotion.

△ The Master of Dontei relates the following account. At a one-evening gathering at the residence of Mr. Enomoto, I once heard the following: A shop boy (*detchi*) employed at a pawnbroker's in Komagome Hakusan-shita, on the evening of the second day of the tenth month of that Year of the Hare, had gone upstairs to close and bolt the second-floor wooden shutters, when, after a short while, as he descended the ladder, he muttered to himself: "Tonight there will surely be the sorrow of an earthquake, and a strong one at that." The head clerk heard this but paid it no heed, and scolded the boy harshly: "Don't say such ominous things! Light the lamp and get down quickly!" And so things were left as they were. But sure enough, that night's disaster damaged the house and the storehouse. The members of the household barely escaped with their lives and all emerged unharmed. They spent seven days sleeping outdoors, after which they repaired the tilted house, and eventually everyone moved back into their original homes and settled in with relief. It was then that people recalled what the shop boy had said, and reported the matter in full detail to the master of the house. The master found it remarkable and called the shop boy to question him. The boy answered and said: "My father is from Shinshū province and often told the following story: On the evening of the day when a great earthquake struck during the period of the Zenkōji Temple's open display of its sacred image, white clouds trailed across the western sky like wisps of haze, and in the eastern sky appeared a cloud shaped like a Japanese mountain yam (*tsukuneimo*). That night a great earthquake struck the province. Then after a short while, similar clouds appeared again in the east and west. Thinking this must surely be a major aftershock, those who had seen the clouds carried their household goods out to open fields and hid themselves among bamboo groves — and indeed, that very night once again a great earth-[quake struck]…"
