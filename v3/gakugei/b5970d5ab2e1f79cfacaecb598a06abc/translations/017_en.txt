(Kihei said:) "Though I am on my way home and cannot even be sure of the state of my own house, I cannot abandon you in this moment of crisis. For now, let me take you all to my place, and we shall deal with whatever comes next." Hearing this, everyone was greatly relieved. The three women — Kakusen'in and the others — were carried to Kihei's home and given care, and all were at last able to breathe a little easier.

Throughout this time, the maidservant Kane, not minding even her own pain, continued to nurse the injured while also worrying about all manner of things and lending her hand even to the miscellaneous household chores at Kihei's home — and this spirit of devoted loyalty continued right up until her mistress was taken in by relatives. So moved were the authorities by this extraordinary faithfulness that she was awarded a prize of fifteen silver coins (*gin jūgo-mai*); and Sukeemon, Kihei, and Kichigoro were also summoned and commended for their conduct. Such sincere loyalty and genuine compassion are a model for all posterity, and it is for this reason that I record them here.

△ In Gosukiya-chō, Shitaya, there was a rice-pounder (*tsukikomaya*) named Tohee, who had a manservant called Yosuke. At the time of the earthquake, the master's house was severely damaged, and while the other servants ran about in a frenzy of panic at the outbreak of fire in the neighborhood, this Yosuke quietly gathered every last one of his personal belongings — down to a single sheet of tissue paper (*hanagami*) — bundled them all up without a word to his master or his fellow servants, and dashed away back to his home province of Shimotsuke, to the village of Akutsu. What kind of spirit was this? Even if he feared the earthquake and the fire, at the very least he should have stayed that one night to help his master's household, and then explained the situation to everyone the following day, bidden his farewell properly, and departed — that would not have been too late. When one compares the conduct of the woman Kane with that of this Yosuke — one who saved her master, and one who abandoned theirs — it may truly be said that the difference between them is as stark as the contrast between black and white.

△ *Chikuyōsha's* Account of the Golden Jar

It is perhaps because there are mysterious and wondrous things in this world that the protection of the gods and Buddhas, and the responsiveness of Heaven, exist — that calamity and good fortune come and go, and that the encouragement of virtue and the punishment of wickedness are naturally built into the order of things. And yet, if one were to become so steeped in the rationalist ways of the Western barbarians (*ban'i*) as to dismiss all things wondrous as unworthy of amazement, then the blessings of the gods and Buddhas could surely be expected to cease as well. It is for this reason that I set down here the mysterious things I have witnessed and heard.

△ There was a daughter of a person of my acquaintance who, fourteen or fifteen days before this earthquake, had gone into domestic service at a wealthy household in the Shitaya area. One night, she dreamed of her late father. In this dream, her father appeared to her and told her: "You must not remain in this place." Because she had this dream on two successive nights, she seized a moment of leisure from her duties to visit her mother and told her of the dream, proposing that she should ask for her release from service. But her mother would not permit it. "You dream such things," she scolded, "simply because you dislike that household. You have only just moved there; to ask for your release now would be most improper, and besides, the expense of moving in and out is no small matter." And so, scolding her daughter, she sent her back.

The very next day, the great earthquake struck. The daughter escaped without injury — which was itself a blessing — but her clothing and all her household goods and utensils were lost entirely to the flames. So said the old mother in her telling of the tale. Is this not, too, one of the world's wonders?
