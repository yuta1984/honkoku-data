△The area around Fukagawa Teramachi had an exceptionally large number of collapsed buildings. Three storehouses belonging to a certain merchant named Iseya collapsed in the shaking, and the soil and roof tiles piled up like mountains. They intended to clear everything away quickly, but because the chaos of this disaster was unprecedented, there were no laborers available, and the debris was left for five days. However, since even passage along the road became difficult, they eventually hired workers to remove the soil and roof tiles. In doing so, they dug out a man from within the earth. Everyone was greatly shocked and pulled him out. After a short while, the man opened his eyes, looked around, and said, "Where am I?" Everyone was even more astonished. They then asked about his condition while buried underground, and he replied, "I remember the earth of the storehouse falling and pressing down on me, but I have no memory of what happened after that." A similar incident occurred at Hasegawa-chō in Honjo, and that person also survived unharmed. Altogether, the number of those who perished in fires or met violent deaths in this great calamity is unknown, numbering in the tens of thousands. These are the consequences of karma from previous lives, and there is no escaping them. Yet, as described above, there were those who remained buried in the earth for days and survived safely. This too is the result of good karmic causes, and no matter what mortal peril one enters, such a person will not lose their life. We have evidence of this before our very eyes. A certain pawnbroker in Kitajima-chō, Hatchōbori, had his house and storehouse completely reduced to rubble, with some people injured. Yet both neighboring properties were entirely safe, without a single roof tile falling. What karmic difference could account for such variation in the earthquake's effects? One should infer that the residents on both sides were people of virtuous conduct.

△In the midst of the fires that broke out following the earthquake, the proprietor of a brothel called something-or-other on Edo-chō in Yoshiwara, thinking quickly to escape the fire, placed fourteen courtesans inside an underground cellar, entered himself, and had the lid closed. He believed they were safely sheltered. When the fire subsided and the lid was opened to check inside, every single one of them was dead. People were left utterly stunned and speechless. This plan seemed like a good idea, but it was in fact profoundly foolish. Fish live in water yet are unaware of water; humans live in air yet are unaware of air. If a person falls into water, drinks a great deal of it, and the belly fills up with water, they die. Likewise, when air ceases to circulate from above and below, they also die.
