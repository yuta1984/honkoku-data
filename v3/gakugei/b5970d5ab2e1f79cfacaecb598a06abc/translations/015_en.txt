【Right folio】

Within the city of Edo (the *gofu-nai*), residences and other buildings had been constructed as plastered houses (*nuriya*) or earthen-walled storehouses (*kurazukuri*) in order to protect against fire. As a result, the sheer number of storehouses (*kura*) found here is unmatched anywhere in the country. Among all places where storehouses are particularly numerous, none compares to the view from Aramebashi Bridge, from which one can look out in all four directions and see more storehouses than from any other vantage point in the city. Now, the damage to ordinary buildings was too extensive for the eye to take in; but the sight of walls shaken apart and brought down, rooftiles hurled to the ground, and earth and debris scattered in all directions was truly a scene beyond all lamentation. Of course, there was not a single storehouse left unscathed; but because such a spectacle was so extraordinary, it has been depicted here in illustration so that people in distant provinces and faraway regions may come to know what it looked like. One can only imagine the severity of such devastation in a place like this. That there was no great fire accompanying the earthquake must be counted as a mercy.

*Ichitōsai Yoshitsuna, artist*

【Left folio】

△ Although events of the distant past are recorded in various writings, no one knows them as reliably as the elderly people of the present age. As for events of the past thirty years, these are things seen with one's own eyes, and so no person need harbor any doubt. Below I record an outline of matters such as have never before been heard of in previous generations.

○ In the fifth month of the twelfth year of Bunsei (1829), the year of the Tiger, the one-shu silver coin (*isshu-gin*) was put into circulation.

○ In the second month of the third year of Tenpō (1832), the year of the Dragon, the two-shu gold coin (*nishu-kin*) was put into circulation. In the seventh month of the sixth year of the same era (1835), the year of the Sheep, the one-hundred-*mon* coin was put into circulation.

○ In the seventh year of Tenpō (1836), harvests failed throughout the country. Around the third month, one hundred *mon* bought only two *gō* and eight *shaku* of rice, and great numbers of people died of starvation. Seventy-five years prior to this, in the third year of Tenmei (1783), one hundred *mon* bought three *gō* of rice; and in the seventh year of the same era (1787), the year of the Sheep, it bought four *gō* and five *shaku*. It is said that at that time, rice merchants' shops were attacked and destroyed in many places, and great disorder prevailed. And yet, in the aforementioned Tenpō year of the Sheep (1835), the sumo wrestlers Inazuma Raigoro and Ōnomatsu Midorinosuke were drawing enormous crowds, and the kabuki theater was enjoying a great triumph with the farewell performances (*nagori kyōgen*) of Nakamura Utaemon — giving no appearance whatsoever of a famine year. This was surely due to the generous relief measures provided by the authorities (the shogunate).

○ In the eighth year of Tenpō (1837), the year of the Rooster, the five-*ryō* coin (*goryō-ban*) and the one-*bu* silver coin (*ichibu-gin*) were put into circulation.

○ In the ninth year of Tenpō (1838), the year of the Dog, harvests again failed throughout the country. One hundred *mon* bought four *gō* and eight *shaku* of rice, and relief provisions were distributed.

○ From the eleventh day of the eleventh month of the twelfth year (1841), the year of the Ox, the Great Reform of the realm (the Tenpō Reforms) was enacted: kabuki theaters were relocated to Asakusa, brothels in various places were abolished, merchant guilds (*nakama*) and trade associations (*kumiai*) along with their various levies (*unjō*) were dissolved, and as for lending and borrowing of money… *(continues on the following page)*
