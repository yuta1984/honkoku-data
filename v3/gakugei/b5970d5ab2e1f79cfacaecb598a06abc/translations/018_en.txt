△ Furthermore, there arose a widespread rumor that the wooden statue (*mokuzō*) at the Thunder Gate (*Kaminarimon*) of the Asakusa Kannon had gone missing — that it had, through its divine powers (*jinzū*), foreseen the earthquake and fled. At this time, the temple's administrative office (*bettō-sho*) posted a notice at the site stating that the statue had been sent to a sculptor's workshop for repairs, and thereby brought the false rumors to a stop — or so it is said. Nevertheless, if one supposes that the statue had a natural divine power by which it sensed the coming earthquake, and that while it may indeed have gone to the sculptor's for repairs, it had also managed to escape with the assistance of human hands — then this, too, is a most curious affair.

△ Behind the main hall of the same temple in Asakusa, there was a wooden horse — it was not entirely clear who had offered it — but when this horse was observed carefully on the day following the earthquake, its four legs were found to be soiled with mud. The prevailing rumor became that this was without doubt the work of the Kannon, who had ridden the horse and gone out somewhere. This, too, is one of the extraordinary tales of the time.

　　While I am on the subject, let me add the following. Long ago, in the fourth year of Hōei (1707), Mount Fuji erupted greatly, and it is said that this gave rise to the formation of what is now called Mount Hōei (*Hōei-zan*). Of that time — so the story goes — after the mountain's eruption had quieted, one of the two wooden horses that stood before the Fuji Sengen Shrine disappeared. There was not a single person who saw or heard of this and did not think it strange. As time passed, the rumor spread ever more widely, until at last a messenger arrived from the head priest of Miho Myōjin Shrine —

　　— with the following message: "After the eruption of the mountain, it was reported that one of the wooden horses before the Sengen Shrine had gone missing. As it happens, here at our Miho Myōjin Shrine, a wooden horse has appeared — it is unclear who brought it — and though we have made enquiries on all sides, no one admits to having offered it. Might it perhaps be your horse?" Acting on this information, someone went to look, and indeed it was unmistakably the very same horse, so it was brought back and placed again before the Sengen Shrine. But before long, the horse had made its way back to the grounds of Miho Myōjin Shrine, as if of its own accord. It was concluded that this must be the work of the divine, and the matter was duly reported to the authorities (*ōyake*). To this day the wooden horse remains tethered before the deity at Miho, and donations for its repair and upkeep have also been made. If one takes this as evidence of divine activity, then it becomes difficult to say that the wooden horse at Asakusa is any less wondrous.
