【Right folio】

△ At a water teahouse (*mizuchaya*) in Okuramai, a certain person arrived by palanquin (*kago*), rested, and was about to board the palanquin again when water began to seep up from the spot where the palanquin bearer had planted his rest-staff (*ikizue*). The proprietor found this curious, and when he dug out the hole left by the staff, the water gushed up with ever-increasing force. By the end of that same day, the surrounding ground was so thoroughly saturated that one could not walk without *koma-geta* (high wooden clogs). Accordingly, a well-ring (*ido-gawa*) was set in place over the spot to form a proper well.

It so happens that this location had, before the Reforms, been the garden of *Kihachi-dango* (a dumpling shop), where there had once been a dug well; the site had been filled in when the establishment was cleared away. It seems that before the earthquake, the subterranean energy (*chiki*) had grown full, causing the water in the old underground channel to increase and burst forth in this manner. Besides this, there were many accounts of well water rising at various locations — all things one would do well to bear in mind.

　　As an aside: from what I have heard of the situation in Shinshū, after the earthquake there the well water *decreased*, and people fought over drinking water. The district officials (*agata-mori*) therefore dispatched functionaries to attend each well, rationing water so that each person was permitted to draw one hand-bucket (*te-oke*) in turn. This too, I suppose, is owing to differences in the nature of the land, and so one cannot make a blanket statement — but in either case, these are signs (*zui*) of underground change (*chihen*), and are things one ought to keep in mind.

【Left folio】

△ My friend Nakamura Daisaku had business in Nakayama in Shimōsa Province and went there on the first day of the tenth month. Then, on the night of the second — the very next day — the great earthquake struck. He therefore instructed his manservant Jūsuke to see to matters there, and hastened back to Edo on his own. Meanwhile, Jūsuke put the necessary affairs roughly in order and set out from Nakayama after the lower half of the *Hitsuji* hour on the fifth day (past 3 o'clock in the afternoon), hurrying back as fast as he could — yet time passed of its own accord, and by the time he had made it as far as Honjo Oshiage, it was already past the lower half of the *I* hour of the night (past 10 o'clock at night). At that point, Jūsuke was utterly exhausted, and sat down to rest a while on a field path to the south of Saikyōji temple——
