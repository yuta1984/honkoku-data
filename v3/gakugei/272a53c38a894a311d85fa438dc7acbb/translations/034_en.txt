**Episode 80**

Long ago, there was a person who had planted wisteria at a house fallen into ruin and neglect. On the last day of the Third Month (Yayoi), as a light rain was falling that day, the person wished to break off a branch and send it to someone, but being unable to deliver it in person, composed this poem (from the *Kokinshū*):

　*Nuretsustu zo / shiite oritsuru / toshi no uchi ni / haru wa ikuka mo / araji to omoeba*

(Even while being wetted by the rain, I have forcibly broken this branch — for I think that within this year, how few days of spring can remain.)

**Episode 81**

Long ago, the Minister of the Left (Minamoto no Tōru) was living (in the capital) in the present age. At the bank of the Kamo River, in the vicinity of Rokujō, he had built a house of quite remarkable elegance and lived there. Toward the end of the Tenth Month (Kannazuki), at a time when the chrysanthemums were beginning to fade yet were still at their peak, and when the maple leaves were visible in a myriad of hues, he had various Princes attend upon him, and the company drank wine and made merry through the entire night. As the night moved toward dawn, those present composed poems praising the beauty of the mansion. Among those present was an old man known as Katai-no-okina, who crept beneath the floorboards and waited until all the others had finished composing their poems, and then composed:

　*Shiogama ni / itsuka ki ni ken / asana (na) ki ni / tsuri suru fune wa / koko ni yoranan*

(When did I ever come to Shiogama? — I wonder. O boats that go out fishing every morning, do come and moor here at this place.)

The reason he composed this poem is as follows: the old man had once traveled to the Province of Mutsu (Michinoku), and found that there were many places of mysterious and remarkable beauty there. Yet among all the sixty-odd provinces under our sovereign's rule, there was no place that resembled Shiogama. And so the old man, moved anew by his admiration for this very place (the Rokujō mansion), composed the poem "When did I ever come to Shiogama?"

**Episode 82**

Long ago, there was a Prince known as Prince Koretaka. Beyond Yamazaki, at a place called Minase, there was a palace (御殿). Every year at the height of the cherry blossoms, he would make his way to that palace. At such times he would invariably bring along the man who served as the Right Master of the Horse. As time passed and many years went by, the name of that man has been forgotten. Rather than devoting themselves seriously to falconry, they drank wine, and drinking, devoted themselves to the composition of *yamato-uta* (Japanese poetry). The cherry trees now blooming at the shore residence — the direction in which the present falconry hunt proceeds — and the cherry trees of that palace in particular were of exceptional beauty. They descended to sit beneath those trees, broke off branches to insert in their hair as *kazashi* (hair ornaments), and all — high, middle, and low — composed poems. The poem composed by the man who served as the Right Master of the Horse (Ariwara no Narihira), (recorded in the *Kokinshū*), was:

　*Yononaka ni / taete sakura no / nakariseba / haru no kokoro wa / nodokekara mashi*

(If there were no such thing as cherry blossoms at all in this world, how tranquil and at ease would the heart be in springtime.)

And so he composed it. Another person's poem was:

　*Chireba koso / itodo sakura wa / medetakere / ukiyo ni nani ka / hisashikaru beki*

(It is precisely because they scatter that the cherry blossoms are all the more wonderful — in this sorrowful world, what indeed could be expected to last?)
