**Section 63**

Once upon a time, there was a worldly-wise and thoughtful woman. She longed to meet a man of true feeling and compassion, but having no means to express this openly, she gathered her three children and told them what seemed like a believable dream she had had. Two of the children responded without feeling and the matter ended there. The third child, a boy, said, "I will bring you a fine gentleman," and arranged a meeting. This woman's manner was exceedingly charming, while others seemed entirely lacking in sensitivity. The child felt strongly that he must somehow bring her together with this Zaigo-no-Chūjō (the man of Ariwara).

When the man happened to be out hunting, the child encountered him, took hold of his horse's bridle on the road, and explained the circumstances. Moved with compassion, the man came and spent the night with her.

After that, the man ceased to appear, so the woman went to his house and peeped in. The man caught a faint glimpse of her and composed a poem:

*Of a hundred years, lacking but one — such is the white-haired one (tsukumogami). She seems to long for me; I can see it in her face.*

Seeing him prepare to leave, the woman caught herself on brambles and thorns as she made her way home and lay prostrate with grief. The man, in the same manner that the woman had done, stealthily stood and peered in. The woman lay sighing and lamenting, composing:

*Spreading my robe alone on a narrow mat, must I again tonight sleep without meeting the one I love?*

The man was deeply moved and spent that night with her. As is the way of the world, one loves those one loves, and does not love those one does not love — yet this man had a heart that made no such distinction.

**Section 64**

Once upon a time, a man did not even attempt to court a woman in secret, so in wonder at where she might be, he composed:

*If only I could make myself into the blowing wind — I would seek out the gaps in the jeweled bamboo blinds and slip inside.*

(Reply)

*Even if you were a wind that cannot be restrained, who would permit the gap in the jeweled blinds? How could you seek that opening?*

**Section 65**

Once upon a time, there was a woman whom the Emperor favored and kept in service, one who had been granted permission to wear robes of the higher court colors. She was the cousin of the one known as the "Great Imperial Consort" (Ōmiyasundokoro). A young man of the Ariwara clan, still very much in his youth, who attended at the imperial court (tenjō), was acquainted with this woman. Since the man had been granted access to the women's quarters, he came to where the woman was and sat facing her. The woman said, "This is most improper. It will be my ruin. Please do not do this," to which he replied:

*In the face of love, endurance and restraint have lost. In exchange for having met you — let come what may.*

He descended into the small inner chamber, and just as he always did in this small chamber, not knowing that people could see him, he climbed up and sat there. The woman, overcome with distress, withdrew to her family home. Thinking what good could come of this, the man continued to visit her, and everyone who heard of it laughed.
