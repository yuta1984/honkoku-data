【Section 39】

Long ago, there was an Emperor known as the Emperor of Nishiin. This Emperor had a child known as Takaiko, who had fallen ill at this time. When that child passed away and on the night of the funeral, a man who lived next door to the palace rode along in a lady's carriage to see the funeral procession. He had been sitting inside for a long time and was on the verge of breaking into tears, when the great amorous man of the realm, a person called Minamoto no Itaru, also having come to observe the proceedings, saw this as a lady's carriage and drew close to it, behaving flirtatiously in one way and another. Meanwhile, Itaru caught a firefly and placed it inside the lady's carriage. The lady in the carriage said, "Shall the light of this firefly fade away? Let me snuff out the lamp then," whereupon the man riding in the carriage composed:

*Idete inaba / kagiri naru beki / tomoshi keshi / toshi he nuru ka to / naku koe wo kike*
(If you go away, it must be the end — with the lamp snuffed out, hear the voice that cries out as if asking whether the years have passed by.)

Itaru's reply:

*Ide aware na / naku zo kikoyuru / tomoshi keshi / kiyuru mono to mo / ware wa shirazu na*
(Ah, how moving — yes, I hear the crying, with the lamp snuffed out. But please do not think of me (my feelings) as something that fades and disappears.)

As poems from the foremost lover in the realm, these were still something worth noting. Itaru was (Shitagau's?) great-uncle. The prince's wish was not granted (went unfulfilled).

【Section 40】

Long ago, a young man was in love with a woman who was not of particularly low birth. There was a prudish parent who, worried that the man would grow too attached, tried to send the woman away elsewhere. And yet, one could not simply drive away another's child, and since the man did not yet have sufficient means or influence, he had no power to hold her back. The woman, too, was of humble station, and had no power to remain. In the meantime, his feelings grew only more and more intense. Suddenly the woman was to be moved away. The men shed tears, but there was no way to stop it. She was taken away. The man, weeping bitterly, composed:

*Idete inaba / tare ka wakare no / kata karan / arishi ni masaru / kyō wa kanashi mo*
(Once she has left, who could find this parting anything but unbearable? Beyond anything that has ever been before, how sorrowful is today.)

And having composed this, he lost consciousness. The parent was thrown into a panic. The parent had spoken (to separate them) thinking still of the man's best interest, not imagining it would come to this, but since he had truly lost consciousness, the parent fell into confusion and made vows to the gods and buddhas. The man had lost consciousness around the time of the evening bell that day, and it was not until around the Hour of the Dog (around 8 p.m.) the following day that he barely managed to revive. Young people of old experienced such deeply affecting sorrows of love. Surely the old men of today would not die from such a thing.

【Section 41】

Long ago, there were two sisters. One had a husband who was of low birth and poor; the other had a husband who was of noble birth. The one with the lowly husband, on the last day of the twelfth month (New Year's Eve), washed her husband's outer robe and starched it herself. Her intentions were admirable, but since she was unaccustomed to such menial tasks, she ended up starching only one side of the robe. Having no recourse, she simply wept and wept.
