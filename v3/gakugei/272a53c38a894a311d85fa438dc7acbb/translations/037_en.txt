(From the *Kokinshū*):

　*Omoedomо / mi wo shi wakeneba / me kare senu / yuki no tsumorizo / waga kokoro naru*

(Though I think of you constantly, I cannot divide my body in two — and so it is this ever-watching, ever-accumulating snow that embodies my heart for you.)

When he recited this, the Prince was deeply and profoundly moved, and removed his own robe and bestowed it upon him.

**Episode 86**

Long ago, a very young man and a young woman were in love with each other. As both had parents, they held back out of discretion, and the affair ended inconclusively. After several years had passed, the man — perhaps thinking he would still fulfill his old intentions — sent a poem to the woman (from the *Shin Kokinshū*):

　*Ima made ni / wasurenu hito wa / yo ni mo araji / onoga samazama / toshi no henureba*

(There can surely be no one in this world who has gone on remembering another this long — for we have each gone our separate ways, and the years have passed.)

With that, the matter came to an end. Both the man and the woman had drifted apart from each other and had gone off to serve at court.

**Episode 87**

Long ago, a man went to live at Ashiya Village in Mubara District, Settsu Province, having some connection to that place. In an old poem (from the *Shin Kokinshū*):

　*Ashi no ya no / Nada no shioyaki / itoma nami / tsuge no ogushi mo / sasezu ki ni keri*

(At a humble reed-thatched house, tending the salt fires of Nada keeps one so busy that there is no time even to put in a small boxwood comb.)

— this was the very village sung of in that poem. This place was called "Ashiya no Nada."

Now, this man was something of a court official, and on account of that connection, various deputy governors of Settsu (*e no suke*) gathered around him. This man's elder brother was also the governor of Settsu (*e no kami*). They strolled along the seaside before the house, and said: "Come now, let us climb up to see the Nunobiki Waterfall said to be at the top of this mountain." So they climbed up and looked — and the waterfall was utterly unlike anything else they had ever seen. It was some twenty *jō* (approx. 60 meters) in height and about five *jō* (approx. 15 meters) wide,

cascading down the face of the rock like a great white cloth wrapped around a boulder. Above the falls there jutted out a rock the size of a straw sandal, and the water rushing off the edge of that rock fell in a stream no wider than a small thread of *kōji* mold being reeled, spilling and dropping away. The people assembled there were each asked to compose a poem about the waterfall, and the governor of Settsu (*e no kami*) composed first (from the *Shin Kokinshū*):

　*Waga yo woba / kyō ka asu ka to / matsu kai no / namida no taki to / izure takaken*

(My life, which I wait to end like a shellfish waiting day by day — which do you suppose flows higher: that tearful waterfall, or the cascade of my own tears?)

The host then composed next:

　*Nuki midaru / hito koso arashi / shiratama no / manaku mo chiruka / sode no sebaki ni*

(There must be someone here unraveling and scattering white gems — for these white droplets fall without cease, as if more than a narrow sleeve could hold.)

When this was recited, the bystanders apparently burst out laughing. Everyone was delighted with the poem, and on that note things came to a close.

The road home was long, and as they passed before the house of the late Kunakyō Mochiyoshi, night fell. Looking out toward the direction of their lodgings, they could see many fishing fires of the sea-people (*ama*) burning out on the water, and so the host composed a poem (from the *Shin Kokinshū*):

　*Haruru yo no / hoshi ka kawabe no / hotaru ka mo / waga sumu kata no / ama no taku hi ka*

(Are those the stars of a clear night, or fireflies along the riverbank? Or are they the fires that the fishermen burn, there in the direction of my home?)

With this he composed, and they returned home. That night, a south wind blew and the waves grew very high. Early the next morning, the women of the house went out and gathered up the floating seaweed (*ukimiru*) that had been washed ashore by the waves, and brought it inside the house.
