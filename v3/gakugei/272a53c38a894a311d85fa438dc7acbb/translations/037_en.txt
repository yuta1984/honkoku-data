*(Episode 85, conclusion)*

(*Kokinshū*)

　*Omoedomo / mi wo shi wakeneba / mekaresenu / yuki no tsumoru zo / waga kokoro naru*

(Though I think of you, I cannot divide my body in two, and so I cannot be apart from you — like the snow that keeps accumulating without cease, such is the state of my heart, ever piling higher with longing for you.)

Having composed this poem, the lord (who had taken holy orders) was moved with the deepest compassion, and taking off his own robes, he presented them to the man.

**Episode 86**

Long ago, a very young man and a young woman had declared their feelings for one another. But as each of them had parents, they held back, and what had been half-spoken simply faded away. Years passed, and the man, perhaps thinking he would at last fulfill his old intention, sent a poem to the woman (*Shin Kokinshū*):

　*Ima made ni / wasurenu hito wa / yo ni mo araji / onoga samazama ni / toshi no henureba*

(Surely there can be no one in the world who has remained unforgotten for so long as this — and yet each of us has gone our separate ways, and all these years have passed.)

And with that, it came to nothing. Both the man and the woman had gone their separate ways into court service.

**Episode 87**

Long ago, a man went and settled in the village of Ashiya, in the district of Mubara, in the province of Tsu (Settsu), on account of some connection (or landholding) he had there. In an old poem (*Shin Kokinshū*):

　*Ashiya no / Naniwa no shioyaki / itomanami / tsuge no ogushi mo / sasazu ki ni keri*

(The salt-burners of Ashiya in Naniwa are so busy they have no leisure — they have come without even inserting a small comb of boxwood into their hair.)

This poem was composed about that very village. This place was known as "Ashiya no Nada" (the Ashiya offing).

This man happened to be engaged in court service, and making use of that connection, the deputy governors (*suke*) of Settsu came and gathered there. This man's elder brother was also the governor (*kami*) of Settsu. They went out to enjoy themselves along the shore in front of the house, and said: "Come, let us climb up to see the Nunobiki Waterfall, said to be above this mountain." They climbed up and beheld it, and the waterfall was quite unlike anything else. Over a stone face some twenty *jō* in length and five *jō* in breadth, it was as though white cloth had been wrapped around the rock.

Above the fall there was a stone jutting out, about the size of a straw sandal. The water rushing from over that stone fell in a stream no wider than a small rice-malt strainer, spilling and tumbling down. The man had everyone present compose poems on the waterfall, and the governor of Settsu composed first (*Shin Kokinshū*):

　*Waga yo wo ba / kyō ka asu ka to / matsu kai no / namida no taki to / izure takaken*

(I wait for my fleeting life to end, whether today or tomorrow — between the crab's tears and this waterfall, which falls from the greater height?)

The host composed next:

　*Nukimidareru / hito koso arashi / shiratama no / manaku mo chiru ka / sode no sebaki ni*

(There must be someone who has strung these white jewels and scattered them so — how ceaselessly the white drops fall, too many even for a narrow sleeve to catch.)

Those present may well have laughed at this (last poem). All admired it, and the gathering came to an end.

The road back was long, and by the time they reached the house of the late Lord of the Bureau of Palace Affairs, Mochiyoshi, night had fallen. Looking toward the direction of the lodging, many fires of fishing boats could be seen, and the host composed a poem (*Shin Kokinshū*):

　*Haruru yo no / hoshi ka kawabe no / hotaru kamo / waga sumu kata no / ama no taku hi ka*

(Are they stars on a clear night? Are they fireflies along the river? — Or are they the fires that the fishermen kindle in the place where I dwell?)

Having composed this, they returned home. That night the south wind blew, and the waves rose very high. The next morning, the servant girls (*menoko*) of the house came outside and gathered up the floating seaweed (*ukimiru*) that had been washed ashore by the waves, and brought it inside the house.
