…she did not think it entirely impossible, yet because there were so many watchful eyes about, they could not meet. As the man was a person of high standing among the imperial messengers, he was not lodged far away; his room was placed near the lady's own bedchamber. The lady, having quieted the people around her, came to the man's room at the hour of the first night watch (around 11 p.m.). The man, unable to sleep, was lying down gazing outward toward the moon, which was hazy and indistinct, when he saw a small girl-attendant walking ahead, followed by a figure standing there. Overjoyed, the man led her into his sleeping quarters. They remained together from the first night watch until the hour of the ox (around 2 a.m.), yet before they had exchanged even a word of heartfelt conversation, she had already gone back. The man was deeply saddened and lay awake through the night. The next morning, though he was anxious to know her thoughts, it was not his place to send a messenger first; so he waited in uneasy suspense. When dawn had fully broken and a little time had passed, from the lady's side came no spoken words, only this poem:

　*Kimi ya koshi / ware ya yukiken / omohoezu / yume ka utsutsu ka / nete ka samete ka*

(Was it you who came, or was it I who went? I cannot tell — was it a dream or reality, were you sleeping or awake?)

The man wept bitterly and composed:

　*Kakikurasu / kokoro no yami ni / madoi niki / yume utsutsu to wa / koyoi sadame yo*
　(From the Kokinshū)

(Lost in the darkness that overwhelms my heart, I have wandered astray — whether it was dream or reality, determine that tonight.)

He sent this poem and went out to the hunt. Though he was in the fields, his heart was up in the air, and he thought only of how that very night, once people had settled and grown quiet, he would meet her as quickly as possible. But then the Governor of the Province, who also served concurrently as the Director of the Saigū Office…
