【Section 23】

Long ago, the children of people who lived in the countryside used to come out and play by a well. As they grew older, both the boy and girl had come to feel bashful around one another. The boy thought he wished to have this girl as his wife; the girl thought likewise of this boy — and so, even when their parents tried to arrange other matches for them, neither would listen. And so, from the boy next door, this poem arrived for the girl:

*Tsutsui tsutsu / izutsu ni kakeshi / maro ga take / sugi ni kerashi na / imo mizaru ma ni*
(The height I once measured against the well-curb — it seems I have grown past it, in all the time since I last saw you.)

Reply:

*Kurabeko shi / furiwake gami mo / kata sugi nu / haru narazu shite / tare ka agubeki*
(The parted hair we once compared has now grown past my shoulders. If it is not yet the season of coming-of-age, who then shall pin it up for me?)

Exchanging poems such as these, they finally were united as they had wished. As the years went by, however, the woman lost her parents and had no one to rely upon. Thinking they could not go on living in such poverty together, the man began visiting a place he had found in the district of Takayasu in the province of Kawachi. Even so, the original woman showed no sign of resenting this and would send him off without complaint. The man, suspecting that she must have someone else in her heart — why else would she act so — hid himself in the garden shrubbery and, pretending to set off for Kawachi, watched her closely. The woman applied her makeup with great care, gazed out in a pensive reverie, and composed:

【Left Page】

*Kaze fukeba / okitsu shira nami / Tatsuta-yama / yowa ni ya kimi ga / hitori koyu ran*
(When the wind blows, white waves rise on the open sea — is it on Tatsuta Mountain that you are crossing alone in the dead of night?)

Hearing this, the man was moved beyond measure with tenderness for her, and he ceased going to Kawachi altogether. Yet, on the rare occasions he did visit Takayasu, he found that while the woman there had at first comported herself with a certain refinement and reserve, she had now grown entirely unguarded — he saw her scooping cooked rice with her own hands and piling it into a bamboo-woven vessel. His heart cooled at the sight, and he stopped going there entirely. And so that woman of Takayasu gazed toward the direction of Yamato and composed:

*Kimi ga atari / mitsutsu wo ran / Ikoma-yama / kumo na kakushi so / ame wa furu tomo*
(I wish to keep gazing toward where you are — O Mount Ikoma, do not hide that place with clouds, even if the rain should fall.)

As she gazed out with these words, a message arrived at last saying that the man from Yamato was coming. She waited with joy, but time and again the nights he promised to come went by without him, and so:

*Kimi kon to / iishi yo goto ni / sugi nureba / tanomanu mono no / koi tsutsu zo □ (furu)*
(Each night you said you would come has passed without you; and so, though I no longer place my hopes in you, still I spend my days longing for you.)

So she lamented — but the man never came again.
