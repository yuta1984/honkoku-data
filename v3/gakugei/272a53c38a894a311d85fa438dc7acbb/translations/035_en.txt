(Episode 82 — continued)

They arrived at a place called Amanogawa ("River of Heaven / the Milky Way"). The Right Master of the Horse presented wine to the Prince. The Prince said: "Take as your topic the fact that we have spent the day hunting at Katano and have arrived at the bank of Amanogawa, and compose a poem and offer me the cup." And so the Master of the Horse composed this verse and presented it (from the *Kokinshū*):

　*Kari kurashi / Tanabata-tsume ni / yado karan / Amanogawa-hara ni / ware wa ki ni keru*

(Having spent the day in the hunt, I find I shall have to borrow lodging from the Weaving Maiden — for I have come, without quite knowing it, to the bank of the River of Heaven.)

The Prince recited the poem over and over again, and was unable to compose a reply. Ki no Aritsune had been in attendance as one of the Prince's escort. His reply poem was:

　*Hitotose ni / hitotabi kimasu / kimi mateba / yado kasu hito mo / araji to zo omou*

(Because she waits for you, my lord, who come but once a year, I think there is surely no one who would offer her lodging to another.)

The company returned and the Prince entered the palace. They drank wine and conversed until deep in the night, and as the Prince, now drunk, was about to retire, the moon of the eleventh day was also on the verge of disappearing behind the mountains — whereupon the Master of the Horse composed this poem (from the *Kokinshū*):

　*Akanaku ni / madaki mo tsuki no / kakururu ka / yamanoha nigete / irazu mo aranан*

(Not yet grown weary of gazing, and already the moon hides itself? O ridge of the mountains, flee away somewhere and refuse to let the moon enter behind you!)

On behalf of the Prince, Ki no Aritsune (composed):

　*Oshinabete / mine mo taira ni / nari nanан / yamanoha nakuba / tsuki mo iraji wo*

(Would that every single peak were leveled flat! If there were no mountain ridge, the moon would never set behind it.)

**Episode 83**

Long ago, Prince Koretaka, who used to make his way to Minase, set out as usual for a hunting excursion,

【Left folio】

and the old man who served as Master of the Horse (Narihira) attended upon him. After a number of days had passed, the Prince was to return to his palace. (Narihira) was thinking to see him off and take his leave, but the Prince, wishing to bestow wine and gifts of reward upon him, kept him back and would not let him go. This Master of the Horse, growing impatient and anxious for his departure, composed:

　*Makura tote / kusa hiki musubu / koto mo seji / aki no yo to dani / tanomarenanaku ni*

(I shall not even bother to gather grass and bind it for a pillow — for I cannot even take comfort in the thought that this is at least an autumn night, a night of lingering elegance and feeling.)

He composed this because the time was the last day of the Third Month (Yayoi, i.e., late spring), quite unlike the contemplative mood of an autumn night. The Prince did not retire to his chambers, and spent the night through in wakefulness. While (Narihira) had continued to attend upon the Prince in this devoted manner, quite unexpectedly the Prince took holy orders and became a monk. Wishing to pay his respects in the First Month (Mutsuki), (Narihira) made his way to Ono, but as it was at the foot of Mount Hiei, the snow lay extremely deep. Forcing his way through, he went up to the Mimuro (御室, the Prince's monastic retreat) and was granted an audience. The Prince appeared to be spending his days in solitary melancholy, in a state of deep and sorrowful detachment. (Narihira) attended upon him for quite a long while, calling to mind and speaking of things from the past. Although he wished he could remain in attendance forever, there were matters of official court business to attend to, and so he could not remain — and as he prepared to leave at dusk, he composed this poem (from the *Kokinshū*):

　*Wasurete wa / yume ka to zo omou / omoiki ya / yuki fumiwakete / kimi wo min to wa*

(Forgetting for a moment where I am, I think — can this be a dream? Who could have imagined that I would come, treading through the deep snow, to look upon you, my lord?)

And with these words, he departed, weeping as he went.
