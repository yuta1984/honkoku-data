(Episode 82 — continued)

They arrived at a place called Amanogawa ("River of Heaven," the Milky Way). There the Master of the Right Horse presented the Prince with wine. The Prince commanded: "Take the subject of having gone hunting at Katano and arrived at the bank of the Amanogawa, compose a poem on this theme, and pass the cup." And so the Master of the Horse composed and offered up this poem (from the *Kokinshū*):

　*Kari kurashi / tanabatatsume ni / yado karan / amanogawara ni / ware wa ki ni keru*

(Having spent the day hunting until dark, let me beg lodging from the Weaver Maiden — for I have come all the way to the banks of the River of Heaven.)

The Prince recited the poem over and over again, charmed by it, but was unable to compose a return poem. Ki no Aritsune was among those in attendance serving the Prince. His return poem was (from the *Kokinshū*):

　*Hitotose ni / hitotabi kimasu / kimi mateba / yado kasu hito mo / araji to zo omou*

(Since she waits for you, my lord, who comes but once a year — I think the Weaver Maiden would find no one willing to lend her lodgings to another.)

The company returned and entered the palace (at Minase). They drank wine and conversed until deep into the night, and the host — the Prince himself — had become drunk and was about to retire. The moon of the eleventh night also appeared to be on the verge of disappearing behind the hills. And so the Master of the Horse composed this poem (from the *Kokinshū*):

　*Akanaku ni / madaki mo tsuki no / kakururu ka / yamanoha nigete / irazu mo aranan*

(Though I have not yet had my fill of gazing upon it, has the moon already hidden itself away? Oh, ridge of the mountains — flee away, and do not let the moon set behind you!)

In place of a reply from the Prince, Ki no Aritsune offered:

　*Oshinabete / mine mo taira ni / nari nanamu / yamanoha nakuba / tsuki mo iraji o*

(Would that all the mountain peaks might level themselves flat! If there were no mountain ridge, then the moon would never set.)

**Episode 83**

Long ago, Prince Koretaka, who had been visiting Minase, — with the usual hunting party in attendance —

【Left folio】

— was served by an old man who held the post of Master of the Right Horse (Ariwara no Narihira). After several days had passed, the Prince was to return to his palace. (Narihira) thought to see him off and then withdraw, but the Prince pressed wine upon him and, wishing to bestow gifts (roku) upon him, would not let him go. The Master of the Horse, growing anxious (as the hour grew late and the possibility of spending the night arose), composed the poem:

　*Makura tote / kusa hiki musubu / koto mo seji / aki no yo to dani / tanomarenanaku ni*

(I shall not even bother to gather and knot grass for a pillow — for I cannot even rely upon this being an autumn night, long enough to make such rest worthwhile.)

The time was the last day of the Third Month (Yayoi). The Prince did not retire to his chambers, but instead passed the night awake together with (Narihira). Having thus continued to attend upon the Prince on repeated occasions, Narihira was taken utterly by surprise when the Prince suddenly had his hair cut and took holy orders. Wishing to pay his respects in the New Year (the First Month), Narihira went to Ono, but as it lay at the foot of Mount Hiei, the snow lay extremely deep. Forcing his way through, he made his way to the御室 (Mimuro — the Prince's retreat), and when he came to pay his respects, the Prince was there in a state of deep, quiet melancholy. Narihira remained in attendance for quite a long while, recalling and speaking of matters from the past. He wished that he might remain there in attendance indefinitely, but as there were public duties at court, he could not stay, and as he departed at dusk, he composed this poem (from the *Kokinshū*):

　*Wasurete wa / yume ka to zo omou / omoiki ya / yuki fumiwakete / kimi o min to wa*

(When I forget (my duties) and find myself here, I wonder if this is a dream. Who could have thought — that I would come trudging through the snow to see you, my lord?)

And with that, weeping as he went, he departed for home.
