【Right folio】

*(Episode 96, conclusion)*

"— I will surely come to meet you." So she had written. But around the time of waiting for autumn, rumors began to spread from here and there that the woman was about to leave for another man's side. And so, the woman's elder brother suddenly came to take her away. The woman then had someone gather the first crimson leaves of the maple, composed a poem, wrote it upon the leaves, and sent it to the man:

　*Aki kakete / iishi nagara mo / ara naku ni* / *ko no ha furishiku / e ni koso arikere*

(Though we promised to meet when autumn came, it was not to be — it is like a river inlet where the fallen leaves pile up and drift away, signifying something already lost.)

Having written and left this, she said, "If someone comes from over there, give this to him," and departed. And so, even to this day, nothing is known of her. Whether she fares well or ill, and where she has gone, no one knows. That man, striking the back of his hand, is said to be cursing her still. How dreadful a thing. One wonders: does another person's curse truly reach its mark, or does it not? "Now at last we shall see," he is said to declare.

**Episode 97**

Long ago, there was a person known as the Minister of Horikawa (*Horikawa no Ōtodo*), who was alive at that time. On the day when his fortieth-year celebration (*shijū no ga*) was held at the Kujō residence, an old man who then held the rank of Middle Captain (*Chūjō*) composed:

　*Sakurabana / chirikaikumure / oiraku no* / *kon to iu naru / michi magau ga ni*

(O cherry blossoms, scatter and cloud the sky — so that old age, which they say is coming, may lose its way along the path.)

**Episode 98**

Long ago, there was a person known as the Great Minister (*Ōki Ōtodo*). A man who served him, around the ninth month (*Nagatsuki*), attached a pheasant to an artificial branch of cherry blossoms and presented it to the Minister, with a poem:

　*Waga tanomu / kimi ga tame ni to / oru hana wa* / *toki shimo wakanu / mono ni zo arikeru*

(The flowers I pluck for you, my lord, upon whom I depend — these are things that know no season; they bloom for you at any time.)

Having received this poem, the Minister found it exceedingly clever and delightful, and rewarded the messenger with a gift (*roku*).

**Episode 99**

Long ago, on the day of the fire-kindling ceremony (*hiwori no hi*) at the Right Guard's riding ground (*Ukon no baba*), the face of a woman in a carriage parked across the way was faintly visible beneath the lowered bamboo blinds. A man who was then a Middle Captain composed and sent this poem:

　*Mizumo arazu / mimosenu hito no / koishiku ba* / *ayanaku kyō ya / nagame kurasan*

(If I find myself yearning for someone I have neither truly seen nor not seen at all, shall I spend this day in an aimless, distracted reverie?)

Reply:

　*Shiru shiranu / nani ka ayanaku / wakite iwan* / *omoi nomi koso / shirube narikere*

(What reason is there to make distinctions between those we know and those we do not? It is only the feeling of longing that serves as our guide to one another.)

Afterwards, they came to know who each other was.
