**"Section 56"**

Long ago, a man lay thinking, rose thinking, and overcome with longing, composed:

*My sleeve is not a grass-thatched hermitage, and yet—each time night falls, it becomes a lodging for the dew.*
(His sleeve is drenched with tears every night.)

**"Section 57"**

Long ago, a man was pining in secret. He sent this to a cold and indifferent woman:

*I have grown weary of love—like the weed-crab (ware kara) said to dwell in the seaweed the fisher-folk harvest, it is none but myself who has broken my own body to pieces.*
(Playing on *ware kara*, meaning both a type of crustacean and "from myself / of my own doing.")

**"Section 58"**

Long ago, there was a man with refined sensibility and an amorous nature, who had built a house and was living in a place called Nagaoka. In the grove of a shrine neighboring that place, some unremarkable country women happened to be present. Coming to cut rice, and catching sight of this man, they exclaimed, "My, what a splendid connoisseur of love—what a thing to behold!" and gathered and came inside. The man fled and hid himself in the back of the house. The women then said:

*How desolate it has become—ah, how many ages has this dwelling stood? The one who lived here sends not even a visit.*

And so they gathered and sat in the shrine. The man then sent out this poem:

*The wretched thing about this dwelling, overgrown with mugwort and fallen to ruin, is that even in passing, demons have come to make their noise here.*

The women said they wished to gather fallen ears of grain, and the man replied:

*Had you let me know that you were gathering fallen ears in your sorrow, I too would have gone out to the edge of the paddies.*

**"Section 59"**

Long ago, a man—whatever he may have thought of the capital—resolved to live in the Eastern Hills (Higashiyama), thinking:

*I have grown weary of living here—now, at last, I shall seek a dwelling in the mountain village where I may hide myself away.*

And so it was that he fell gravely ill and seemed near death. They poured water over his face and did what they could, and when he revived, he composed:
*(Note: attributed in the Kokinshū to an anonymous poet)*

*Is it dew being laid upon me? Or is it the dripping of the oar of a boat crossing the Milky Way?*
(The water on his face felt to him like dew from the heavens, or the spray from a celestial vessel.)

And having recited this, he came back to life.

**"Section 60"**

Long ago, there was a man. During a time when he was busy with court service and his heart was not devoted, a woman from the neighborhood, saying she would be loved faithfully, went off with someone to a distant province. This man, traveling to that province as an imperial envoy, heard that she had become the wife of a provincial official there. He declared, "Have the lady of the house take up the cup—otherwise I shall not drink," and so she came out holding the cup. He took a mandarin orange (*tachibana*) that had been set out as an accompaniment, and composed:

*When I catch the scent of the orange blossoms awaiting the fifth month, it is as though I smell the sleeve of a person from long ago.*

At this, the woman was overcome with memories, became a nun, and retreated into the mountains.
