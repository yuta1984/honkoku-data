When the Meido Kokyō Zen Master (known as Ittōteki) resided at the Yōshun-an hermitage in the same district, the master immediately sought an audience with him. His doctrinal discourse was free-ranging and wide-ranging, his responses rang out like echoes—truly he was a golden carp slipping through the net, a green stallion brought to a sudden halt. When Kokyō moved to Nansō-ji in the same district, the master served as his attendant, carrying towel and water-vessel, pursuing his studies day and night. Kokyō, knowing that the master had achieved a certain enlightenment, bestowed upon him a certificate of dharma transmission and gave him the name "Takuan," composing a verse (gāthā) to expound its meaning. The master commissioned a painter to copy Kokyō's portrait from life and asked for an inscription. Kokyō took up his brush and wrote: "The coarse face is easy to paint, but the inner brow is difficult to capture. In ordinary stratagems, one enters the realm of demons and returns, subduing the demonic school. With the living mechanism freely entering the realm of Buddha, able even to slay the Buddha—with a swift flick of the whisk, it suddenly bursts forth. One says: is not the one who steals his father's sheep and conceals it none other than the Zen monk Hō? I (Kokyō) burst out laughing and say: why do you not ask about bringing great peace to the realm?" The master received this as a precious treasure and guarded it dearly. In the same district there was a man named Sōmu who specially invited members of the clergy for a memorial rite for his late father and requested that National Master Enkan preside over the dharma interview. The master's responses were quick and agile, like jade rolling and pearls turning. At this time, Kokyō was bedridden at Yōshun and, hearing of the master's keen exchanges, was amazed and delighted, saying in admiration: "Truly, this is a child who has leapt over the stove (a rare prodigy)!" When Kokyō passed away, the master was elevated by the congregation to fill the seat at Yōshun. In the year Keichō Hinoto-Hitsuji (1607), when the master was thirty-five, he was appointed head of the board at the home temple, succeeding Rintoku Zen. In the eighth month of the same autumn, he became abbot of Ryūkōzan Nansō Zen temple. After two years, he entered the ranks at Daitoku (Daitokuji), serving as a memorial incense offering for Kokyō. His manner of residing on the mountain had the relaxed flavor of the ancients. Before long, he announced his retirement and returned to Sennan. The clergy and laity of Sennan went out to the suburbs to welcome him, rejoicing as if they were seeing a living Buddha. A man named Sōin in the same district founded a hermitage, named it Shōun, and invited the master to be its founding abbot. The master composed a dharma verse to celebrate and praise it. In Sennan and Ryūhō, watching whether people came or stayed, one could gauge their priorities. Lord Nobukimi of the Yō (Haru) household one evening entered the master's Zen chamber and questioned him about the Way, also sending a letter of thanks. In the year Mizunoto-Ushi (1613), the bell tower of Nansōji was entirely rebuilt; in the year Kinoe-Tora (1614), the Jūunken of Daisen was reconstructed. In the spare moments between Zen sittings, the master compiled the Daitō Chronology, which was preserved at Unmon-an. In the year Kinoto-U (1615), Nansōji suffered a devastating fire; the master consulted with the district magistrate and together they arranged for its reconstruction to the south of the district—neither hastily nor slowly—and in due course the temple's former appearance was restored. The master regarded fame and profit as dust and ashes, and sound and color as bubbles and illusions. At times he stayed in Sennan's tranquil district, loving its secluded and deep quietude; at times he lodged at Hōrin-an in Nankyo (Kyoto), concealing his brilliance; at times he entered the scenic area of Hase, embracing the mist and nurturing his spirit; at times he resided at Myōshōji in Shinanoki of Jōshū, preserving the void and stillness.

Throughout his life, he eventually returned to his native place in San'in and built a small thatched hut at the foot of the main mountain of Sōkyōji, naming it "Tōen-ken" (Pavilion of Casting Into the Deep). In a pot with a broken leg, he cooked hemp, wheat, millet, and beans for his daily meals, yet showed no sign of hunger. In the year Kanei Tsuchinoto-Mi (1629), the master had occasion to share banishment with Master Gyokushitsu to a remote and desolate place. Yet knowing that his comings and goings were determined by fate, he did not change his expression. In the year Mizunoe-Saru (Kanei 9, 1632), the shogunate issued an order recalling both masters. The master arrived at Buryō (Edo) and erected a small hermitage on the outskirts of the city in a village, within earshot of a single cow's lowing, calling it "Kensoku" (Restraint), where he temporarily resided. The shogunate summoned the master to the castle compound, frequently asking about the dharma, and treated him with generous favor; his reputation grew ever higher. In the autumn of the year Tsuchinoe-Tora (Kanei 15, 1638), the master went up to the capital, where the Retired Emperor (ex-Emperor Go-Mizunoo) summoned him into the imperial retreat and he lectured on the *Yuanren Lun* (Treatise on the Origin of Man); his eloquence surged like a great river pouring down. The imperial heart was greatly pleased. The master memorialized: "The Second Abbot of our temple (Daitokuji), Tettō Yuiyū Zen Master, has the title of Zen Master but lacks the title of National Master; we humbly request that an imperial decree be issued." The Retired Emperor approved, and the imperial document with its precious black seal was issued without delay; the posthumous title "Tennō Daigen Kokushi" (National Master of Heavenly Response and Great Manifestation) was granted. His contribution to the ancestral founder was thus accomplished. The shogunate built a modest temple to the south of Kinshiro (Edo Castle) at Shinagawa and had the master serve as its abbot; the mountain was called Banshōji and the temple Tōkai. On the day of its completion, a celebratory verse was composed and offered in praise. Thereafter, when the shogunal palanquin entered the mountain, even the trees and grasses seemed to shine with radiance. Receiving the shogunal command, the master composed a waka poem praying for the firm foundation of the nation and for the enduring prosperity of the newly built temple. In the year Kanei Tsuchinoto-Mi (1641), an envoy was dispatched to the home temple (Daitokuji) to restore the regulations governing the temple's institutional ceremonies, reinstating the former rules. That this command was issued was due to the master's wishes. His contributions to the home temple may be understood from this. In the summer of the second year of Shōhō (1645), a painter was commissioned to paint a single enso (Zen circle) by the master; the master personally added a single dot to the center, wrote an inscription above it in ink, and presented it as a birthday gift. In the middle of winter of that same year, he fell ill, foreknowing that his karmic bonds were exhausted, and left these final instructions: "Bury my whole body in the mountain behind the temple. Do not chant sutras or hold memorial feasts. Do not accept condolences or funeral offerings from monastics or laypeople. The monks should put on their robes and eat their meals as on any ordinary day. Furthermore, do not trouble the court with memorials seeking a posthumous title. Do not place a memorial tablet in the founder's hall of the home temple," and so forth. A full month passed without recovery. One morning at dawn, he took up a brush, wrote the single character 夢 (Dream), and passed away peacefully. It was truly the eleventh day of the twelfth month. His worldly age was seventy-three; his monastic seniority was fifty-nine years. His whole body was buried on the hill to the northwest of Tōkai, with only pine trees planted above—no pagoda was erected, following his dying wishes. His disciples in Sennan erected a pagoda at Shōun-an and named it "Jakunen" (Serene Stillness).
