**Nekomata Bridge (Nekomatahashi)**　This bridge spans the flow of the Koishikawa River to the west of the same area. According to the *Nankōtei Sawa* (a miscellaneous writing):
　In former times, a forked root of a large tree was used in place of bridge timber, and it is from this that the name derives.
　《Marginal note: Upon reflection, in the rural custom of the eastern provinces, the root of a tree is called "nékko" (根ッ木). This explanation seems plausible. Likewise, the alley in Kanda Matsushitachō that is commonly called "Nekoya Shimmichi" (猫屋新道) is so named because many timber merchants lived there and there were many houses selling such root-timber.》

**Hall of the Ten Rākshasī (Jūrasetsunyodō)**　This hall is located to the south of the Fujibashi river in Sugamo Honmura. Its administrative temple is of the Shingon sect, called Fukuzō-in (福蔵院). An elder of the village says: in former times, an image of Kishimojin (the demon mother-turned-guardian deity) was also enshrined here, but it was stolen by thieves and is now said to be in Zōshigaya. Whether this account is true or not cannot be confirmed, but we record it here simply as it has been passed down. The sacred rites are performed on the eighteenth day of the ninth month.

**Itabashi Post Town (Itabashi no Eki)**　This is the first post station on the Nakasendō highway, located two *ri* (approx. 8 km) from Nihonbashi. Travelers passing through are in constant, unbroken succession. Because the Tōkaidō route involves many troublesome river crossings, in recent times the lords and other travelers have come to favor this road, so that inns and sake shops line the eaves in an unbroken row, making it a thriving place. In the middle of the post town, there is a small bridge over the Shakujii River (Ishigami River), and it is here that the name "Itabashi" (Board Bridge) originates.

**Itabashi Plain (Itabashi no Hara)**　This likely refers to the entirety of the area called Upper and Lower Itabashi. This land has from ancient times been a broad, open plain. According to the *Chūko Chiranki* (中古治乱記):—On the twenty-sixth day of the fourth month of Jōji 6 (1367), the Kamakura Deputy (kanrei) Ashikaga Motouji, Lord of the Left Stables, passed away. Seizing upon the resulting disorder, Haga Nyūdō Zenka's son, Iganokkami Takata, and his eldest son Hachirou Takamasa, among others, set out to advance upon Kamakura. In the first month of Ōan 1 (1368), they led more than five hundred cavalry out of Echigo Province, and on the sixth day of the same month, they emerged onto the Itabashi Plain in Musashi Province. When word of this reached Kamakura, the Chief Administrator (shitsuji) Uesugi Noriaki remained to guard Kamakura himself, while he dispatched his son Hyōgonokkami Norimasa and Hyōbu-shōyū Yoshinori as generals, with Chiba-no-suke Naotane, Oyama Tomoaki, and others—a force of more than two thousand cavalry in total. These troops took up position at the place called Sugamo in Musashi Province on that same day, while a detachment of five hundred cavalry under Chiba and Oyama was sent ahead and placed in the forest of Ōji.
