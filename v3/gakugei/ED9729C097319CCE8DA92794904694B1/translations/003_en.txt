**Muryōzan Dentsu'in Jukyōji**

This temple is located approximately two *chō* (roughly 220 meters) to the northwest (the direction of *inui*) of Ushitenjin in Koishikawa. It is one of the Eighteen Danrin (centers of Buddhist learning) of the Jōdo sect. The principal image of Amida Nyorai (Amitābha) is a work by the monk Eshin Sōzu (Genshin), and the temple was founded by the Venerable Ryōyo during the Meitoku era (1390–1394). [Marginal note: There are more than one hundred residential quarters (*ryōsha*).]

**Mausoleum (Otamaya)**　[Marginal note: This is the mausoleum of Lady Dentsu'in-dono (Odai no Kata, the birth mother of Tokugawa Ieyasu). In accordance with her dying wish, a mortuary shrine was built, and the temple was named after her posthumous Buddhist title. Her remains are enshrined at Sōkeiji Temple in the same vicinity. The *Wakan Sansai Zue* records her death as the twenty-ninth day of the eighth month of Keichō 7, the year of *mizunoe-tora* (1602).]

**Founder's Hall (Kaisandō)**　[Marginal note: Located to the right of the main hall.]

**Hachiman Shrine**　[Marginal note: Located on the same grounds. During the Tenshō era (1573–1592), an elderly man obtained a stone plaque bearing the characters for "Hachiman Shrine" through a divine dream. The shrine's *bettō* (administrator) bears the name Keikyū-in.]

**Benzaiten Shrine**　[Marginal note: This shrine was originally located on the grounds of Hakusan Palace, where it stood alongside the Hakusan and Hikawa shrines. The sanctuary of the Nyotai Gongen was later moved to this site and re-enshrined as Benzaiten.]

**Takuzōsu Inari Shrine**　[Marginal note: Located near the rear gate of the temple precinct. In ancient times, it is said that a fox transformed itself into a monk, called itself "Takuzōsu," and came night after night to the temple's study hall to debate Buddhist doctrine. It was later enshrined as an Inari deity and became the temple's guardian of the Dharma (*gohōjin*).]

**Constant Nembutsu Hall (Jōnenbutsudō)**　[Marginal note: Maintained by Shinjuin, a sub-temple (*tatchū*).]

**New Nembutsu Hall (Shinnenbutsudō)**　[Marginal note: Likewise maintained by Zuishin'in.]

**Daikokuten**　[Marginal note: Located at Fukuju'in within the temple complex. According to Kikuoka Senryō, when a well was first being dug, this sacred image was found within the earth, and so the well was named "Ofuku no I" (the Well of Good Fortune). In former times this was part of the Dentsu'in precinct, but now that well has become part of the estate of the Lord of Harima of the Matsudaira clan. Upon reflection, it may have been confused with the Gokuraku-mizu (Water of Paradise). That well too is within the same estate, and there being two springs in so small an area, they are in all likelihood one and the same. According to the temple's founding legend (*engi*), the Daikokuten enshrined here is a sacred image transmitted from three countries (India, China, and Japan), and represents in a single form the three deities of Daikoku, Tamon (Bishamonten), and Benzaiten. During the reign of Emperor Kōtoku, a man named Toko of Rokurei, a minister of the Kingdom of Koguryō (*Kōrai*), brought it to Japan, where it was kept in Gamō District, Ōmi Province. During the Meiwa era (1764–1772), the Venerable Hōyo Reio received it through spiritual inspiration and enshrined it here. On the days of *kinoe-ne* (the first day of the sexagenary cycle), crowds of worshippers gather. The hall's plaque reads "Fukuju-den," and is in the brushwork of Nyokū, the twenty-third abbot of this mountain.]

**Sutra Repository (Kyōdō)**　[Marginal note: A plaque reading "Shōkyō-kutsu" (Cave of Sacred Teachings) hangs on the front, and inside the entire *Tripitaka* (*Issai-kyō*) in the Ming edition is stored. It is noted that for good reason, this is revered and treasured as one of the temple's sacred Dharma treasures.]
