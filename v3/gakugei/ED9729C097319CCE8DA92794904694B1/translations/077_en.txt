**The Ruins of the Castle of Shibukawa Saemon-no-suke Yoshiyuki**

It is believed to have been situated somewhere around the post-town of Warabi, but the exact location is no longer certain today.

*(Marginal note: According to the* Kamakura Ōzōshi*: On the twenty-third day of the sixth month of the first year of Chōroku (1457), Shibukawa Saemon-no-suke Yoshiakira was dispatched as commander-in-chief to Musashi Province. Since Yoshiakira was a close kinsman of the Kubō and his family had served as Kyushu Tandai (Deputy Governor-General of Kyushu) for generations, all the great houses regarded him with considerable respect. Moreover, his grandfather, Saemon-no-suke Yoshiyuki, had long served as the provincial governor of Musashi, establishing the place called Warabi in Adachi District as his castle town, and the family had held jurisdiction over this area ever since. For all these reasons, it was deemed appropriate that this man should take the role, and so Yoshiakira was appointed Tandai. His orders were conveyed throughout the province; the soldiers of Musashi and Kōzuke were informed accordingly; and proclamations were issued to the effect that Nariuji should be subdued, the Uesugi installed as Kantō Kanrei (Deputy Shogun of the Kantō), and order restored to the eastern provinces, and so forth.)*

**Tsuki no Miya (Tsuki Shrine)**

Located about three *chō* (approx. 330 meters) before the post-station of Urawa, in a place called Kishi Village. The shrine stands to the right of the highway and is known today as "Tsukiyomi no Miya" and associated with the Twenty-Third Night observance. Its *bettō* (administrative Buddhist temple) is called Gassan-ji, and it is under the joint administration of Gyokuzō-in in the town of Urawa. *(It belongs to the Shingi Shingon sect and is affiliated with the Sanbō-in monzeki.)* The annual festival is held on the twentieth day of the ninth month. The tablet bearing the inscription "Tsuki no Miya" displayed on the shrine's *kōhai* (front porch canopy) is in the calligraphy of Matsudaira Fusasada, Asomi.

Enshrined deity: Tsukiyomi no Mikoto (one seat). Buddhist counterpart (*honji*): Seishi Bosatsu (Mahāsthāmaprāpta). *(The association with the Twenty-Third Night derives from this Buddhist counterpart deity.)*

*(Marginal notes:*
- *The* Engishiki Jinmyōchō *states: "Musashi Province, Adachi District, Tsuki Shrine," etc.*
- *The* Musashi no Kuni Fudoki *states: "Adachi District, Ōtsuki Township, Tsuki Shrine, six* *tan* of divine fields…")*

【Left Page】

*(Marginal note continued from right page: "…twelve bundles [of rice], two-character field. In the third month of the year* *kinoto-tori* of the reign of Emperor Yamato Neko Hiko Ōhi (Emperor Keikō), the deity enshrined was Seoritsuhime. There are *kambe* (shrine households) and *kannagihe* (shamaness households), and so forth.")*

The shrine record states: This shrine was founded by imperial vow of Emperor Sujin. Later, in the third year of Kenmu (1336), Ashikaga Takauji made a vow to subdue the rebels, and the divine response was not in vain; accordingly he undertook the construction of the shrine buildings. On the fifth day of the second month of the second year of Engen (1337), land comprising five villages was granted as shrine estate. Furthermore, during the Jōwa and Kannō eras (approximately 1345–1352), the imperial (*miyakata*) forces rose up, and as a consequence all temples and shrines fell into ruin and abandonment. Therefore, in the second year of Kōryaku (1380), Sasaki Ōmi-no-kami Minamoto no Mochikiyo undertook the restoration of the shrine, and in the first month of the second year of Shitoku (1385), land comprising two villages was additionally granted. However, in the eighteenth year of Tenshō (1590), during the battles attending the fall of the Hōjō clan of Odawara, the sacred treasures were lost and scattered, and the divine estate too fell into desuetude of its own accord. Then, at the time of the "entry into the domain" (Tokugawa Ieyasu's assumption of the Kantō), the Divine Founder (*Shinso*, i.e., Ieyasu) graciously heard of the shrine's origins and history, and once again granted fertile fields, forests, and the like in perpetuity. Finally, in the second year of Keian (1649), an official red-seal document (*shujō*, i.e., *shuin-jō*) was bestowed upon the shrine.
