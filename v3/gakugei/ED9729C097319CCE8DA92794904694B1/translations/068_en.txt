[Furthermore, there is a place called the "Horikane Well" located approximately six *chō* to the south of this site — a depression of about twenty *bu* in area — which is also called the Horikane Well. In the village of Kita-Iruma as well, there is a place called the "Horikane Well," bearing the local place-name *Nanakurinoi* ("Seven-Winding Well"). It spans some six or seven paces across and lies beside a farmhouse. Local tradition holds that this well was dug in Bun'ei 7 (1270), and that in former times the entire village drew its water from it. However, in later ages the well-channel collapsed and fell into disrepair, so that today wells have been dug here and there, and no one draws from this one any longer. Consequently, the area around the well has become overgrown with miscellaneous trees and is densely wooded. Beside it, moreover, ancient stone monuments bearing dates from the Bun'ei, Bunpō, and Kanshō eras still survive. In general, places called "Horikane Well" exist in locations such as Otome Shinden and Takaido as well, and the Horikane Well is by no means confined to a single site. On further reflection, because Musashino was vast and desolate and had been poor in water since ancient times, wells were dug here and there, yet it was difficult to obtain water easily — and so, in all likelihood, the name came to be applied. It would therefore seem reasonable to say that this well is not limited to any one place.]

**Kuruma-kaeshi Amida Nyorai (The "Carriage-Turning" Amitābha)**

　This image is enshrined at Raikōji Temple (Mountain name: Tōkōzan), a Zen temple of the Sōtō school located in Horinuchi Village, which is a sub-temple of Futamata Kaizenji. The principal image (*honzon*) is a standing figure of Amitābha (*Amida Nyorai*), three *shaku* in height. The two attendant images (*wakiji*) — Kannon (Avalokiteśvara) and Seishi (Mahāsthāmaprāpta) — each measure two *shaku* in height, and all are works of the Buddhist sculptor Unkei. [The principal image, together with its pedestal, is decorated with a fine *makie* (gold lacquer) design of arabesque scrollwork (*karakusa*) and gentian (*rindō*) crests, the ornamental beauty of which is beyond description. The gentian referred to here is what common people call *sasa-rindō* (dwarf gentian).]

　According to tradition, long ago Hidehira of the Date clan of Ōshū (northern Japan) commissioned the sculptor Unkei to carve the three sacred figures — Amida, Kannon, and Seishi — as a unified *ikkō sanzon* (one-halo trinity), with Unkei performing three reverential bows after each stroke of the chisel (*ittō sanrai*). On the day of the eye-opening ceremony (*tengen kuyō*), the living body (*shōjin*) of the Tathāgata itself seemed to appear and descended in person, radiating a great light that illuminated the newly made images. The new images, in turn, also radiated light of their own.
