**Miyamoto Hikawa Daimyojin Shrine**

Located at the southern foot of Mimuroyama, in Miyamoto-go, approximately twenty chō north of Ōmiya Station.

The local people call it the "Miyamoto Hikawa Shrine" and also refer to it as the "Nyotai-gū" (Shrine of the Female Deity). The enshrined deities are the same as those of the Ōmiya (Hikawa) Shrine: the central shrine houses Ōanamuchi no Mikoto, with Susanoo no Mikoto on the right and Kushiinadahime no Mikoto on the left.

The shrine grounds are densely covered with cedar, cypress, and sacred sakaki trees, lending the precinct an imposing and solemn atmosphere. The sacred fence (mizugaki) is smoothly covered in moss. The sound of the drums struck by the shrine's kannagi (spirit medium), the morning prayers, the evening thanksgiving offerings, and the voices of ritual purification echo through the mountain forest, stirring one to feel the very presence and response of the divine — truly a most sacred and awe-inspiring place.

The Mukasa clan has served as hereditary Shinto priests (kannushi) here for generations and has been granted a shrine estate of fifty koku. Around the founding of the Edo shogunate, the "Divine Ancestor" (i.e., Tokugawa Ieyasu) himself visited the home of the kannushi and bestowed donations of shrine lands and sacred treasures. Ancient implements and old documents are said to have been handed down and kept in the family of the kannushi to this day.

**Minuma (The Sacred Pond)**
[In the Kujiki it is written as "Mizunuma"; it may also be written as "Minuma" (見沼)]

This pond lies in front of the main torii gate of the shrine and serves as the sacred ablution basin (mitarashi) of the shrine. It is said that in former times it extended four to five ri in length and more than twenty chō in width. In the twelfth year of the Kyōhō era (1727), by order of the shogunate, this pond was developed into new rice paddies (shinden). Today, only a faint remnant of the pond's former shape remains...
