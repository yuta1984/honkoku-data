…Yet when one climbs to the top of this mountain (Hakkokusan), the field of vision opens up vast and boundless, and indeed one can take in at a single glance the distant peaks of eight provinces: **Mount Fuji** in Suruga, **Mount Amagi** in Izu, **Mount Hakone** and **Aburifuri (Ōyama)** in Sagami, **Mount Taba** in Kai, **Mount Asama** in Shinano, **Mount Azuma** in Kōzuke, **Mount Nikkō** in Shimotsuke, and **Mount Tsukuba** in Hitachi — and it is for this reason that the mountain bears the name "Eight-Province Mountain" (Hakkokusan).

### Kumegawa (久米川)

Rising from the land to the west of Kumegawa Village — from Megurida Village, Shimizu Village, and other places — two small streams converge behind Tokuzōji Temple in Noguchi Village, then flow through Kumegawa Village; and it is for this reason that the river is called Kumegawa.

There is also what is called the **Futase-gawa**: it rises in Kume Village, Akitsu Village, and other places in Iruma District, flows along the district border between Tama and Iruma, and joins the main stream at Kumegawa Village. [From that confluence, about one *ri* (approx. 4 km) downstream, the riverbed gradually widens and becomes the Ishikawa. Above Kumegawa, the river is a narrow valley stream, barely four or five *ken* (approx. 7–9 m) wide.] Both streams then pass through the vicinity of Hikimata and Niikura, and ultimately join the Arakawa River. During the battles of Musashino in the eras of Shōkei and Kyōtoku, encampments were set up along the Tamagawa, the Irumagawa, and the Kumegawa — demonstrating that, because the open wilderness was so poor in water, troops necessarily relied upon these riverbanks.

The place called **Horikane-no-i (the "Hard-to-Dig Well")** is also a reminder of how scarce water was in ancient times — one should bear this in mind.

[From *Kaikoku Zakki* ("Miscellaneous Notes from Traveling the Provinces"): "There is a place called the Kume-kume River. The houses in the village have no wells, and the people draw from this river alone for their morning and evening needs, I was told."]

> *Satobito no, kume-kume-gawa to yūkure ni, nari-naha mizu wa, kōri mo zo suru*
> (The villagers name it "Kume-kume River"; as evening falls, the water grows so cold it nearly turns to ice.)
> — Dōkō Jungō (道興准后)

### Tairyūzan Eigen Zenji (大竜山永源禅寺)
**Eigen Zen Temple of the Great Dragon Mountain**

Located in Kume Village. It lies about five *chō* (approx. 540 m) to the north of Hakkokusan, across a small stream. It is a Zen temple of the Sōtō sect, affiliated with Tatsugaya Ryūon-ji. [In former times it was affiliated with Kenchō-ji in Kamakura.]

It is a temple founded by the Ōishi clan during the Tenbun era (1532–1555). The founding patron (*kaiki*) was a member of the Ōishi family, whose posthumous Buddhist name was **Eigen Dōshun Daikoji** (英巌道俊大居士). [The version at Chōgen-ji in Yasumatsu that writes "Dōshun" as "Dōshun 道春" is likely an error.] The founding abbot (*kaisan*) was called **Isshu Chōton Zenji**. [He is said to have been a younger brother of Hōjō Ujiteru. He passed away on the 27th day of the 2nd month of Eiroku 8 (1565).] The principal image of worship is a seated statue of Śākyamuni, approximately two *shaku* (approx. 60 cm) in height, said to be the work of the Great Bodhisattva Gyōki.
