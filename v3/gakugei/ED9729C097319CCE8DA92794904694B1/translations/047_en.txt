The Master's words were as follows: "When our Great Ming Taizu, the High Emperor, came to possess the realm, he opened Heaven with his bare hands and founded a unified dynasty. His glorious and resplendent great enterprise is worthy of pride throughout all ages. By his own power alone he restored righteousness, and his meritorious virtue reached to quelling the extraordinary chaos; [the dynasty] was achieved within China. All the people of China were brought back to China; all the land of China was fully returned; the supreme virtue of All-under-Heaven was accomplished. The High Emperor of the Great Ming is truly the most exalted of all. Human relations were made clear from above; the regalia of high office [were worn appropriately]; robes and caps were restored; rites and music were established according to proper regulation; through their practical use, moral transformation was spread across all the land. Look upon these people—emerging from the deep and lasting night, beholding the high heavens on all four horizons: it was as if the primordial chaos was again divided and Heaven and Earth re-created. Can one say otherwise than that the Great Ming is like the sun and moon?"

And yet into this world there suddenly appeared a wicked and treacherous figure: one called Yao Guangxiao (姚広孝), who transformed from beast into monk and manifested in that age. He dared to conspire with Yan Wang (燕王), the fourth son of the High Emperor, and used force to usurp the heavenly mandate of legitimate succession. If one were to call him a monk, his person and deeds had nothing to do with the laws of the secular world; yet if one were to speak of principle, right in the midst of the radiant virtue of the Ming, shining its light upon heaven and illuminating the earth, he stirred up treacherous comets of rebellion, disordered the founding ancestors' legacy that grandson had received from grandfather, and in service of the Yan principality launched his reckless enterprise. He turned against righteousness without cause, defied Heaven without restraint, slaughtered the living without end, wielded seniority and power so that sons slew fathers, grandsons destroyed elder brothers, and younger brothers wiped out elder brothers—and yet all this was proclaimed to the realm under the phrase "reverently receiving the Mandate of Heaven" (奉天承運). These four characters alone are in broad outline an affront to virtue for ten thousand generations. How resolute and upright these words! This was no private resentment of one individual—it was the public judgment of All-under-Heaven.

Alas! The Master encountered the time of dynastic ruin, and would not consent to rest comfortably in that land; furthermore, he was ashamed to submit himself beneath those who came after, and in the end crossed the sea, concluding his life as one dwelling beyond worldly bounds [i.e., as a Buddhist monk]. His unbending, lofty, and transcendent spirit will not be worn away through a thousand ages. The Master's meritorious deeds and enterprises are not to be even cursorily glimpsed, but his lofty sentiments and free-ranging thoughts, spread through his verse and poetry—will not men of eloquence in later generations all feel themselves unable to attain to them? Indeed, he is one to be esteemed and revered. Can anyone read this text and feel no stirring of emotion? Originally, with sincere admiration and longing, I had wished to seek from the great ministers of the court and the men of profound virtue from famous mountains grand prose and elegant verse to illuminate the traces left by my Master. Someone encouraged me, saying: "Why not exert yourself and not leave it to others?" I had always thought myself inadequate. Yet since seeking [such contributors] proved unsuccessful, I resolved to rely on my own effort; and so, basing myself on what I had personally absorbed through direct discipleship during his lifetime, and seeking out those aspects of his inner life that shone clearly, I could not bring myself to avoid the prolixity of the writing, and reverently set down a record of his traces, composing the following inscription:

> *My person is not a Han envoy—I swear I shall not return alive;*
> *My conduct has only righteousness—I have thoroughly tasted hardship and danger.*
> *Many loyal men of the Ming house, toying with arrows amid rivers and mountains;*
> *In the clear night, rubbing my thighs, I tread the path through the many barriers.*

The water that flows throughout the precinct of this temple (Heirinji) was, at the time of the land reclamation of Nohitome (野火留) New Fields in the first year of Jōō (承応, 1652), diverted by the Izu no Kami (Lord of Izu), Lord Matsudaira Nobusuna, from the land of Ogawa Village (小川村), more than two *ri* to the south, and channeled here from the Tama River (多摩川).
*Marginal note: The diversion of the Nohitome irrigation water to Muneoka (宗岡) was carried out during the time when Lord Akimoto held Kawagoe (川越), some forty years after the Meireki era.*

Anshōzan Chōgen Zenji (安松山長源禅寺) is likewise situated in Yasumatsumura (安松村) to the west. It is a Sōtō-sect Zen temple affiliated with Kenshinji (乾晨寺) in Hachiōji (八王子). The founding abbot bore the title Ketsuyō Zenji Tokue Daiosho (傑用禅師徳英大和尚). *Marginal note: He passed away on the twenty-fifth day of the seventh month of the third year of Genki (元亀, 1572), the cyclical year Mizunoe-Saru (壬申).* The founding patron is called Eigandōshun Koji (英岩道春居士). *Marginal note: The inscription on the bell cast at this temple during the Genroku era reads: "Chōgen Zenji is the hermitage opened by Ketsuyō Zenji and was founded by Lord Ōishi Dōshun (大石道春公)." However, the year, month, and death-anniversary of the Ōishi family [founder] are not known in detail.
