### Hizaori Village (膝折里)

This belongs to Niikura District. It lies on the highway from Edo to Kawagoe (Kōshi), one *ri* (league) from Shiroko, where there is a post station. From Tokorozawa it lies to the northeast, a distance of over three *ri*.
*[Interlinear note: In the Hōjō clan's register of stipends (*bungencho*), among the lands listed as belonging to Lord Rokugō, there is an entry reading "Within Kawagoe: Hizaori, twenty *kanmon*."]*

*[Interlinear note: The* Kaikoku Zakki *(Miscellaneous Notes on Traveling the Provinces) states: "In the village called Hizaori there was a market in session, so I rested for a while in a temporary shelter and, as was my custom, composed a verse and recited it to my traveling companions."]*

> *Akinbito wa / ikade tataramn / Hizaori no / ichi ni kakke wo / e ni zo arikeru*
> (How could a merchant stand [at market]? At the market of Hizaori, one has surely come down with "kake"!)
> — The Cloistered Imperial Prince Dōkō (道興准后)

*[Interlinear note: The word "kakke" (脚気) as used in this poem is properly written as "kake" (家笥) or "katske" (家器), meaning the vessel used to store tableware — a utensil used by farming households in this area since ancient times to hold food containers. This accords with the commentary in the* Liji *(Record of Rites), which states: "The 'si' (笥) is a vessel for holding food." Today it is used to hold tea bowls. The verse by Dōkō is a clever *shūku* (witty wordplay) in which he plays on the name "Hizaori" ("knee-fold") by substituting "kakke" (脚気, lit. "leg affliction") for "kake" (家笥, "food-basket"), honoring the name of the place.]*

### Muneoka Post Station (宗岡宿)

This lies to the north of the Hikimata post station, on the opposite side of the waterway called the Uchikawa.
*[Interlinear note: Muneoka belongs to Iruma District; Hikimata falls within Niikura District.]*
This place was the ancient Ōshū Kaidō (the highway to the northeastern provinces) and was, in those days, the route to Kamakura in Sagami Province. Travelers would enter from places along today's Nakasendō such as Ageo and Urawa, then turn south of Muneoka, Hikimata, and Nohitome, pass through the Kiyoto area, proceed toward Fuchū in Tama District, and travel along what is now called the Ōyama Tsūdō before reaching Kamakura.

*[Interlinear note: There is an irrigation channel in this area. It flows through the center of Hikimata post station and reaches the bridgehead of the Uchikawa. There a sluice box (*masu*) has been constructed, and a wooden conduit (*toi*) carries water across the river to the district of Muneoka. Around the Hōei era, when Lord Akimoto held the domain of Kawagoe, he had the Nohitome irrigation water drawn here to assist agriculture. At that time, a certain man named Shirai devised the method of hanging this conduit in forty-eight sections,]*

### Uchikawa River (内川)

Its headwaters rise from the mountain valleys of Tama District, Chichibu District, and others; it enters Iruma District, flows eastward to the north of Kawagoe, and at a point to the west of Hikimata and Muneoka it joins with the flow of the Yanasegawa River. It then flows along the south of Uchimaki and joins the Arakawa River — this entire waterway is called the Uchikawa.
*[Interlinear note: The Arakawa flows along the northern edge of Iruma District; this river flows to its south, hence the name "Uchikawa" (Inner River).]*
At the point where the river reaches the stretch between Muneoka and Hikimata, it is more than fifteen *ken* (approximately 27 meters) in width. On the Hikimata side it serves as a waterway for boat transport, the distance to Edo being approximately nine *ri* (about 35 kilometers).

### Jūgyokuin Temple (十玉院)

Also known as Nanjōzan Hachimanjī (南城山八幡寺). It stands to the west-northwest of Muneoka, about eighteen *chō* (roughly two kilometers) distant, in the village of Shimo-Nambata (下南畑村). It is a temple of the Honzan branch of Shugendo mountain asceticism. The principal image is a standing statue of Fudō Myōō (Acala), measuring one *shaku* seven *sun* (approximately 51 cm) in height; the flanking attendants — two boy figures (*dōji*) — stand about one *shaku* (approximately 30 cm) tall. All three are said to be works of the Great Master Chishō (智証大師).
