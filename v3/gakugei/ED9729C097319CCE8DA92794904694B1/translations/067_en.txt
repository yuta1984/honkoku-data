**[Right Folio]**

This depression in the land is the very site of the so-called Horikane Well. Fearing that the location would eventually be lost to time, a stone well-coping was placed [at the center of the monument], a stone stele was carved and erected nearby, and all this was prepared as a record for future generations.
　According to local tradition, the place is so named because "digging here, one could not obtain water." The character *kane* (兼) is said by some to mean *kata-shi* (難, "difficult"), though this is merely a popular explanation, and the true etymology remains unknown.
　　　Written on the first day of the third month, Hōei *Boshi* year (1708)

From the *Senzaishū* (Collection of a Thousand Years), the "Monks' Section," evoking the spirit of the Chinese verse *"Gradually seeing moist muddy earth, one knows for certain that water is near"*:

> *Musashino no / Horikane no i mo / aru mono wo / ureshiku mizu no / chikaduki ni keri*
> (Even the well of Horikane on Musashino Plain does exist — how joyful it is that water has now drawn so near!)
> — Shunzei

From the *Uji Hyakushu* (One Hundred Poems of Uji):

> *Musashino ya / Horikane no i no / fukaku nomi / shigeri zo masaru / yomo no natsukusa*
> (On Musashino, at the Horikane Well, the summer grasses grow ever thicker and deeper on all sides.)
> — Reizei no Daijō-daijin (the Minister of the Left of the Reizei line)

> *Asakarazu / omoeba koso wa / honomekase / Horikane no i no / tsutsumashiki mi wo*
> (Only because my feelings are deep do I hint at them — like the well of Horikane, my heart is modest and restrained.)
> — Toshiyori

> *Ikatemo to / omou kokoro wa / Horikane no / i yori mo nao zo / fukasa mosarero*
> (My yearning heart surpasses even the Horikane Well in depth.)
> — Ise

From the *Fuboku* (Fuboku Wakashō, the Fuboku Anthology):

> *Musashi naru / Horikane no i no / soko asami / omou kokoro wo / nani ni tatoen*
> (The Horikane Well in Musashi is shallow at its bottom — to what shall I compare my longing heart?)
> — Tamenari

From a *Personal Collection* (Kashū):

> *Kumite shiru / hito mo arikemu / onodukara / Horikane no i no / soko no kokoro wo*
> (Perhaps someone has drawn from it and understood — the depths of the heart, like the Horikane Well, reveal themselves naturally.)
> — Saigyō

From the *Shūgyokushū* (Collection of Gathered Jewels):

> *Ima wa ware / asagokoro wo / wasure-mizu / itsu Horikane no / izuku naru ran*

> — Jichin

From *Renga Ryōzai* (Good Materials for Linked Verse):

> *Michi naru sora zo / Musashino no hara* / *Hito ni yoru / Horikane no i ya / nori no mizu*

From *Hokkoku Kikō* (Travel Record of the Northern Provinces), composed near the Horikane Well:

> *Soko to naku / no wa ase ni keri / kono hara mo / Horikane no i no / kusaba naranedo*
> (Somehow the fields have faded — though this plain is not the very grasses of the Horikane Well.)

From *Makura no Sōshi* (The Pillow Book): "Among wells, the Horikane Well; among *hashiri-i* (running springs), the one at Ōsaka — these are charming," and so forth.

Local people transmit the following account:
　Long ago, when Prince Yamato Takeru made his eastern campaign, water was scarce on Musashino Plain and all the troops were tormented by thirst. The Prince ordered the people to dig wells here and there, but in the end no water was obtained, so he commanded the dragon deity to draw a flow of water to the place. [Some say this refers to what is now the Toshikosazu River, or alternatively the Iruma River.]
