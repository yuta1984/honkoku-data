## [Right Page]

…for some reason, the local people call all of these "Sayama-ga-ike" (Sayama Pond). This can be understood through the description "at the foot of the mountain" in the *Hokkoku Kikō* (Northern Provinces Travel Journal) cited below. Sei Shōnagon wrote "as for ponds, Sayama Pond," apparently because the poem using the word *mikuri* struck her as charming. Mikuri (three-edged bulrush, *Scirpus* sp.), ayame (iris), and nunaha (*Brasenia schreberi*, water shield) are the noted local products.

**[Uta-makura — Poetry Anthology]**
> Ayame grass — like the long roots of Sayama Pond, these too are drawn out like mikuri — Kanemasa

**[Same]**
> On the occasion of gathering mikuri at Sayama Pond, the trailing threads of green willow too are drawn along — Ryūyū

**[Same]**
> In the depths of spring, at Sayama Pond, the submerged roots of the nunaha — effortlessly the frogs cry out — Nakazane

**[Matsuba-shū]**
> The mikuri of Sayama Pond in Musashi — pull as one might, it snaps; will I too be cut off?

> Treading through Sayama, now buried in snow — no one comes to gather the mikuri of the pond — Hidenō

**[Shinsen Rokujo]**
> At the pond in Sayama, waiting for someone to keep coming, as if to harvest the mikuri roots down to the very tips

**[Hokkoku Kikō]** "Traversing Musashino, along the edge of the fields, there lies the famed Sayama. Treading through the morning frost, at the foot of a modest hill, there is a pond of modest proportions."
> Treading through the frost-bound, withered fields at the icy water's edge — going on, the autumn wind at Sayama Pond — Gyōe

**Sayama** refers to the range of hills extending from Kume Village westward to Hakonegasaki, a distance of some three *ri* (approximately 12 km). [Marginal note: Shōgun-zuka (the General's Mound) is]

## [Left Page]

[Marginal note: located on the eastern peak of the same range.] The local people also call it by the name "Obikiyama." The narrow path along the ridge forms the border between Tama and Iruma districts; from both the south and north, it is about two hundred paces from the foot to the summit. Someone has stated that, according to the *Musashi no Kuni Fudoki Zanpen* (Fragmentary Records of the Musashi Province Gazetteer), which says "the north of Tama District is bounded by the facing hill (*mukai no oka*)," this long ridge may well be that "facing hill."

[Marginal note: The local people refer to the continuous range of mountains to the north of Obikiyama as "Sayama." Considering this, the mountains in this area are all narrow and elongated, running in several parallel ridges; it is therefore likely that the name "Sayama" (Narrow Mountain) comes from being enclosed between mountains. In that case, as the local people say, the name "Sayama" encompasses all the mountains of this area collectively, and is not a name limited to a single location.]

**[Senzai-shū (Anthology of a Thousand Years)]**

> In the autumn wind, the kudzu vines of Sayama trail and sway — oh how my heart aches, unable to give voice to my resentment — Emperor Go-Toba

**Hachikoku-yama (Eight-Province Mountain)** belongs to Kume Village. Located about eighteen *chō* (approximately 2 km) to the west of Shōgun-zuka, it is part of the same Sayama range, and the name refers to a slightly prominent point along it. It is not at all the kind of peak that soars above the clouds into the blue sky…
