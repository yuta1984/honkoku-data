The Nagasaki magistrate Tachibana Masanobu petitioned for the Master to remain, submitted a memorial to that effect, and sought to have the Master settled conveniently. Boku Tanken (濮澹軒) composed a poem titled "Longing for Daishi Mankō, who took up his book-box and headed east toward Japan, never to return." The following year, Kōgo (甲午, 1654), in the seventh month, Fushō Kokushi (the National Master Fushō) responded to a summons and crossed east, exerting great religious authority. The Master lamented that his years were now approaching sixty and that his remaining time was uncertain; with firm resolve, he shed his white lay robes and, while there was still breath left in him, sought to complete his commitment by entering the Buddhist order. He thus returned to the seat of the National Master and received tonsure and the dyeing of robes. This was on the eighth day of the twelfth month (rōgetsu) of that same year. His Buddhist name (imina) was Shōeki (性易); his style (azana) was Dokuryu (独立, "Independent"); his sobriquet was "Tenka Ikkanmin" (天下一間人, "The One-Room Person Under Heaven"). The Master described his abandonment of Confucianism and return to Buddhism as repaying the spiritual landscape of a single, unified nature. Free and unrestrained (rakuraku), clad in his mountain robe with his double sleeves fluttering, there was something especially worthy of deep contemplation—something that defied calculation—in him. A fellow traveler through the "blue-sky, white-dream" world of shared dust [i.e., the secular world], he bore equally the burdens of Confucianism and Buddhism and was broadly versed in classical texts. Those who knew him said: "He is of the lineage of the enlightened, a worthy heir to the brilliance of the Ming." In the eighth month of the year Itsuhi (乙未, 1655), he served Fushō [Kokushi] as secretary. In the ninth month of Manji 1 (万治元年, Bōjutsu, 1658), he accompanied the National Master on a journey east to Musashi (武城, i.e., Edo). The officials and leading men of Edo could not help but marvel that the Master's talent and virtue were superior to those of his contemporaries. The senior official Lord Minamoto Nobusuna (源信綱, i.e., Matsudaira Nobutsuna) and the commissioner Minamoto Masashige (源正重) wished the Master to take up permanent residence [in Edo], but circumstances prevented this from coming to fruition. In Manji 2 (1659), the year Tsuchinoto-I (己亥), he was granted leave on account of illness and returned to Nagasaki. In the year Kanoe-Ne (庚子, 1660), he entered a hermitage, spending three years at Genki-zan (幻寄山). Not long after leaving the hermitage, a great fire broke out—this was on the eighth day of the third month of Kanbun 3 (癸卯, 1663). Thereafter, he did not choose his place of residence but lived without attachment, following conditions freely; he spread the Way along with those of like mind, without regard to direction, and administered medicine with a lively spirit, so that the entire country praised him as if he were divine. He once said: "To relieve people and benefit all beings is the fundamental practice of the Bodhisattva. To apply means according to circumstances is the living road of the Patriarchs and Masters alike." As for his calligraphy: his upright brushwork pursued the divine spirit of antiquity, and those who obtained even a scrap of paper or a single character from him treasured it as they would ink-traces of Zhong You (鐘繇) or Wang Xizhi (王羲之). Indeed, this too was a supreme and extraordinary art. In the spring of Itsushi (乙巳, 1665), the Venerable Sokhi Oshō (即非和尚) of Seppō (雪峰) opened Kōjuzan (広寿山) in Bungo (豊後, present-day Ōita Prefecture). The eastern master [Sokhi] sent a palanquin to welcome him, keeping half the seat of honor vacant in anticipation. The Master then went lightly and readily, sparing no effort and exerting himself to the fullest, despite his remaining strength being limited. The lord of Bungo was moved by the Master's diligent effort even in his old age, and had a quiet room constructed especially in the hills as a place of rest. The Master himself inscribed it "Hakuun-shitsu" (白雲室, "White Cloud Chamber"). Thereafter, while traveling to make a visit of reverence to the National Master, he fell ill en route and turned back, sending his attendant Somei (祖命) to go in his place. Ordinarily he ate heartily, as vigorous as a man in his prime; but now that he had reached this age, his appetite had somewhat declined. When those around him urged him to take medicine, he would not listen, saying: "The reward-body [one's physical form] is not ill—what use is medicine?" He lay reclined on his sickbed, composing verse and amusing himself. Then, one morning, he suddenly rose and sat up, called for a brush, and wrote:

> *Sakusaku chiri-chiri no kaison* (鑿々塵々の海邨)
> *Zanmu wo wasurezu, kūken wo meguru* (残夢を忘れず　空軒を繞る)
> *Totsu, makase yo, tōsetsu suru baika no kage* (咄、任せよ　凍折する梅花の影)
> *Sekkyaku su, kōnan, hakugyoku no tamashii* (接却す　江南　白玉の魂)

*(Chiseling and dusty, the seaside hamlet—
The lingering dream circles the empty eaves, unforgotten.
Hah! Let it be—the shadow of the plum flower, snapped by frost;
Receiving them: the white-jade souls of Jiangnan.)*

When he had finished writing, he passed away in an instant.

## [Left Page]

His countenance was as if still alive. A pair of jade columns [perhaps a reference to an unusual posthumous physical phenomenon], each over a foot in length, amazed all present, who could not cease their grief and mourning. He was seventy-seven years of age, with nineteen years of monastic life (gerō); and this was on the sixth day of the eleventh month of the winter of Kanbun 12, Mizunoe-Ne (壬子, 1672). On the day before he passed into nirvana, he summoned the Venerable Senkai Oshō (千呆和尚) to Kōzen-an (広善庵) and entrusted to him his final affairs. [Senkai] asked: "Right now—what hour is it?" He replied: "At noon, it strikes the third watch." The Master burst into laughter, and the Venerable Oshō thereupon rose and presided over the cremation (kabi) at Seijuzan (聖寿山). The attendant Emyō (慧明) escorted the sacred bones and they were interred on Ōbaku-san (黄檗山). There exist several volumes of the *Tengai Rōjin Shū* (天外老人集, "Collected Works of the Old Man Beyond Heaven").

Alas! It has already been forty-seven years since my Master departed this world. All this while I have carried my unfulfilled feelings, having repaid his immense kindness to the most meager degree; the accumulated shame and remorse are beyond measure. Unexpectedly, in the twelfth month of Kichū (己丑, 1709), I received from afar the letter of summons, and hastening by day and by night, I set out on the journey; crossing into the second month of Kōin (庚寅, 1710), I arrived directly at Edo (武城). On the first day of the third month, I was granted an audience at the palace and was commanded to enter the Hanlin Academy. Looking around, I found that my own position had already been filled, but what I lamented was that there was no place to enshrine my Master's image. My wish was for even a small shelter of a few rafters, and only Minamoto Tamesasa (源為政) heard this wish, approved of it, and thought it could be accomplished. "Beyond ten *ri* from the capital, in the territory of Musashi, there is a great temple. The mountain is called Kinpō (金鳳), and the temple is Heirinji (平林). It was rebuilt by the former Lord of Izu, Minamoto Nobusuna (源信綱). Wasteland was cleared and pine and cedar trees planted; water from the Tamagawa River (玉川) was channeled in a meandering course, with branches dividing and distributing water to supply the kitchen and bath; it never runs dry even in the longest drought, and all find it convenient. The sub-temple Renpōken (聨芳軒), in the northern corner, has a vacant plot of land on which a building can immediately be erected. The head priest Unpō Zenji (雲峰禅師) and his elder teacher Mokuun Oshō (黙雲和尚) have jointly deliberated and given their approval." Thereupon skilled craftsmen were specially engaged, timber was loaded and transported, an auspicious day was chosen, and the ridge-pole was raised. Wing-like in appearance, facing east, it was a structure of barely three bays (ma); modest in materials, it was thatched for roofing and plastered with lime for walls. In a matter of days, the completion was announced. This was on the seventh day of the third month of Shōtoku Heishin (正徳丙申), i.e., the first year of Kyōhō (享保元年, 1716). It was inscribed "Taikeidō" (戴渓堂, "Hall of the Stream-Bearer"), and within, an image of the Great Being cast in gilt bronze (范金大士) was enshrined as the presiding object of devotion and the place to which the guiding master would be revered on return. To the left, the Master's portrait was reverently placed and offerings made; the hanging plaque bears the inscription "Baika Kanshu" (梅花関主, "Master of the Plum Blossom Barrier"), written as a gift by Fushō Kokushi to commemorate the Master's three years of seated seclusion (zakan). Ah! That the time should have been one in which we could not yet fully bathe in the grace of those above—how could [this hall] have extended to this day and made itself known to the world of its own accord? Thinking back on those former companions who shared in our labors, they have all, one by one, fluttered away [like birds in flight]; now only I alone remain, a single surviving child. And my years, too, are old. There remains only a little of the spirit and will; it is enough to make a modest effort to follow his example. In deep contemplation, my Master's lifelong words of loyal indignation—spoken with passionate sincerity, of such weight that they could not lightly be carved in print—amounted to some tens of volumes. Here I briefly bring them forward.
