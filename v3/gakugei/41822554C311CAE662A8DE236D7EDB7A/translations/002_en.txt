**Title: Onkun Kanazukai (A Guide to the On and Kun Readings and National Characters)**

The Japanese *iroha* syllabary is also called "women's writing" (*onnaji*), and if one knows even its forty-seven characters, there is nothing in the world one cannot communicate. However, Chinese characters each have their own inherent readings, and in particular, among the national syllabic characters (*kana*), there are distinctions of weight and lightness—such as between *i* and *wi* (い・ゐ), *o* and *wo* (を・お), and *e* and *ye* (え・ゑ)—and so there are many cases where matching them to characters does not align correctly. Therefore, this work records the distinctions in *kana* usage for both *on* (Chinese-derived) and *kun* (native Japanese) readings, and, extending even to matters of *kan-on*, *go-on*, and *tō-on*, it aims to make known the correct correspondences between sounds and characters, free of error.

**Preface to Onkun Kanazukai**　[Square seal]

First there was spoken language, and thereafter writing was born. First there was writing, and thereafter *on* and *kun* readings were born. *On* and *kun* are, in turn, also a form of language. What conveys meaning to those sharing the same room is speech; what reaches across the distance of nations is the written character.

In the remote past, from the rustic simplicity of knotted cords and bird-track-like markings, through the refined elegance of the various script styles—seal, great seal, standard, and cursive (篆・籀・楷・草)—could it be that these things remained confined to a single court alone?
