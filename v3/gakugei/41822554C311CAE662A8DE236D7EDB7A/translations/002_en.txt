Written by Master Takei Ranzan

《Title: Onkun Kanazukai (A Guide to the On and Kun Readings of Japanese Characters)》

The Japanese iroha syllabary is also known as "women's script," and if one knows its forty-seven characters, there is nothing in the world one cannot communicate. However, Chinese characters each have their own inherent readings, and in particular, since Japanese kana distinguishes between characters of similar sound—such as "i/wi," "wo/o," and "e/we"—there are many cases where matching characters to readings goes awry. Therefore, this work records the distinctions in kana orthography for both on (Chinese-derived) and kun (native Japanese) readings, and by correlating kan-on, go-on, and tō-on with their respective characters, it aims to make clear where no error exists.

**Preface to Onkun Kanazukai**

It is only after language exists that characters come into being. It is only after characters exist that on and kun readings come into being. On and kun readings are, in turn, language itself. That which communicates between people sharing the same seat is "speech"; that which reaches across the boundaries of nations is "writing." In the most ancient times, things were simple—like the knotted cords and bird-track marks of primitive writing—but refined in both form and substance, and arriving at the beauty of seal script, large-seal script, regular script, and cursive script: these things do not merely belong to foreign courts alone.
