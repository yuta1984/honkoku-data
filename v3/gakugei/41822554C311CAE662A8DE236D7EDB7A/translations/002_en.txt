《Title: Onkun Kokujikaku (A Guide to On and Kun Readings of Characters)》

The iroha syllabary of our country is also called "women's script" (onna-moji), and as long as one knows the forty-seven characters, there is nothing in the world one cannot communicate. However, each Chinese character has its own inherent reading (on), and particularly because there are distinctions of weight (light and heavy) among native kana such as "i/wi," "o/wo," and "e/we," there are many cases where the characters do not match up correctly. Therefore, this work records the distinctions in kana usage for both on (Chinese-derived) and kun (Japanese native) readings, and extends even to the matters of kan-on, go-on, and tō-on, matching these to the characters, so as to make known where there is no error.

Preface to Onkun Kokujikaku　【Square Seal】

First there was spoken language, and afterward characters were created. First there were characters, and afterward on and kun readings came into being. On and kun readings are themselves, again, a form of language. That which can be understood when sharing the same seat is the spoken word. That which can convey meaning even across nations separated by distance is the written character. In the deep antiquity of old, from the simplicity of knotted cords and bird-track script, to the refined harmony of literary form and substance, to the flourishing of the many script styles [such as seal, standard, and cursive], how could the differences be confined to a single court alone?
