【Right Folio】

In Japan, from ancient times, there must certainly have been characters, yet they have been lost and not transmitted. To seek to prove this, however, there is no means by which to do so. Only the iroha kana of the priest Kūkai and the katakana of Lord Kibi have been transmitted down through a thousand years, and these are called "kokuji" (national characters). From the forty-seven sounds in their open and closed forms, they give rise to hundreds of millions of readings (kun), and through the five vowel sounds and fifty characters, they possess limitless utility. In our country, even the youngest of children are able to know the national characters. Yet because they do not know the rules governing them, they often read the on and kun with errors. It is here that a discord arises between spoken language and on-kun readings. And what are those rules?—

【Left Folio】

—They are the norms (regulations) for making the spoken language and the written on-kun readings uniformly consistent with one another. I (this unworthy one), during moments of leisure from literary affairs, committed to writing the Onkun Kokujikaku (A Guide to On and Kun Readings of Characters) and gave it to my young students. Before the dust on my desk had even been swept clean, the proprietor of the bookshop Seiunndō repeatedly urged me toward publication. Regrettably, as the matter proceeded in great haste, I fear there may be omissions and errors. As it cannot be helped, I shall note here that I await the corrections of learned scholars. On the day of the vernal equinox in the ji-mi year of the Kansei era [1799]. Written by Takai Tomohiro (Bankan).
