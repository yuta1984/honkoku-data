In the distant past of Japan, there must certainly have been indigenous characters. Yet they have been lost entirely and are not transmitted to us. Though one might wish to prove this, there is no means by which to do so. Only Kūkai's *iroha* and the katakana of Lord Kibi have been transmitted across a thousand years and are called "national characters" (*kokuji*). The forty-seven open and closed syllables of the *iroha* give rise to countless readings (*kun*), and the fifty characters of the five-tone system possess inexhaustible uses. In our land, even a small child can come to know the national characters. And yet, because people do not know the rules (*hōsoku*) governing them, they commit errors in *on* and *kun* readings. It is for this reason that spoken language and written *on-kun* come into discord with one another. The rules in question are—

**[Left Page]**

—the standards (*kiku*) for making that which one speaks in language and that which one writes in *on-kun* readings equal and uniform. I, your unworthy servant, in the intervals of literary affairs, set down in writing *The Rules of On-Kun and National Characters* (*Onkun Kanazukai no Kaku*) and gave it to my young students. Before the dust had even been swept from my desk, the proprietor of the publishing house Seiundō pressed me repeatedly to have it cut onto woodblocks for printing. I regret that, as the matter arose in such haste, there are omissions and errors. As I cannot help but proceed, I shall await the corrections of those of discerning knowledge.

On the day of the Spring Equinox, in the *己未* year of Kansei [1799], I, Takai Bankan, write this preface.
