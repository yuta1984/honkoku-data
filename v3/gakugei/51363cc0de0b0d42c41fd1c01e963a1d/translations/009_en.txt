**[Right folio, upper section]**
**Matters to Always Keep in Mind**

First: With all due reverence — from the time when heaven and earth first opened and the deity Kuninotokotachi was born, there have been seven generations of divine beings: Kuninosatsuchi no Mikoto, then Toyokumunu no Mikoto, Uhijini no Mikoto, Suhijini no Mikoto, Ohotonoji and Ohotomabe no Mikoto, Omotaru no Mikoto, Kashikone no Mikoto, and then Izanagi no Mikoto and Izanami no Mikoto. These are referred to as the Seven Generations of Heavenly Deities. It is said that the two deities Izanagi and Izanami gave birth to the great land of Yamato (Japan) with its eight great islands, and then gave birth to the rulers of the land.

*Illustration: The Age of the Gods*

**[Left folio, lower section]**

Long ago, a man came of age (underwent his coming-of-age ceremony) and went to the village of Kasuga in the ancient capital of Nara — to a place he had ties with (said to be a fief held by Narihira) — to go hawking. In that village lived sisters of remarkable grace and beauty. This man (Narihira) caught a glimpse of them through a gap in the fence. Unexpectedly, to find such women in this ancient village seemed utterly out of place, and his heart was thrown into confusion. Cutting the hem of the hunting cloak (karigi-nu) he was wearing (since there were no flower branches to be found at the time), he wrote a poem on it and sent it to them. The man had been wearing a hunting cloak dyed in the Shinobu-zuri pattern.

*(From the Shinkokinshū)*
> On the fields of Kasuga,
> the young purple of the printed robe —
> the turmoil of "shinobu" (longing / the fern-pattern)
> knows no limit.

He sent this poem, chasing after the women on the spur of the moment (this scene has a charming quality — it was composed instantly, borrowing from an old poem, and the reply poem follows). The women, too, must have felt something stir within them.

*(From the Kokinshū)*
> *(This is a poem by the Lord of Kawahara, the Minister of the Left, Minamoto no Tōru)*
> The Michinoku shinobu-mojidzuri —
> for whose sake
> did my heart begin to be dyed in disorder?
> It was not of my own doing.

The poem above captures the spirit of this exchange. People in ancient times (referred to as "Uto") practiced this kind of swift and keen elegance (miyabi) — the swiftness of wit referred to as "ichihayaki miyabi."
