## Section 22

Long ago, their relationship had ended without any particular occasion, yet she apparently still could not forget him, for from the woman came a poem:

"Though I drift in sorrow, I cannot forget you at all — resenting you, yet still longing for you."

Upon receiving this, the man replied, thinking, "So that is how it is":

"When we meet, my heart, like the waters of the Kawashima River, I believe will never cease to flow."

But that very night, he went to her. They spoke of the old days and what had passed, and she composed:

"Comparing a thousand years of autumn nights to a single night — could there ever come a time when we might sleep eight thousand nights together?"

(Reply) "Even if a thousand autumn nights were made into one, words would remain unspoken, and the dawn bird would cry before we could say all."

And so they continued to meet, with even deeper feeling than in former times.

## Section 23

Long ago, there were children of a man who wandered the countryside. They used to play together at the well, but as they grew into adults, the boy and girl both became bashful with each other. The boy thought he would like to make this girl his own; the girl thought the same of the boy. Though their parents tried to match them with others, neither felt inclined. Then from the boy next door, a poem arrived:

"Like the tube-well's frame against which we once measured our heights — my stature has grown past it, it seems, while I have not seen you."

(Reply) "The hair we once compared, parted and hanging down, has now grown past my shoulders. Who but you should be the one to dress it up?"

And so, exchanging poems like these, they were at last united as they had hoped. As the years passed, the girl's parents died and she was left without support. Thinking it would not do for them to live in such poverty together, the man began frequenting a woman he had found in Takayas in Kawachi Province. Yet his original wife showed no sign of resentment or jealousy, and sent him off without complaint. Suspecting she must have feelings for someone else to be so unconcerned, the man hid in the garden shrubbery, pretended to leave for Kawachi, and watched. He saw his wife dress herself with great care and gaze pensively into the distance, composing:

"When the wind blows and white waves rise on the offing — over Tatsuta Mountain in the dead of night, are you crossing alone?"

Hearing this, the man was moved to the depths of his heart, and he stopped going to Kawachi altogether. On one of the rare occasions he did visit Takayas, he found that while at first the woman there had carried herself with elegant reserve, she had now let down her guard entirely and was scooping rice herself with a paddle into a rough wooden vessel.
