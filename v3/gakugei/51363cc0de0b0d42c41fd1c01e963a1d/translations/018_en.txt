【Right folio, upper section】 (Etiquette for those in service)

One: Concerning the matter of presenting flowers for the master's viewing.
For flowers on a branch (*ki no hana*, flowers of trees and shrubs), hold the stem end downward — just as it grows on the tree — and show them in that manner. It is also acceptable to hold them in the left hand. For herbaceous flowers (*kusabana*), droop the blossoming tip downward, hold them in the right hand, and when presenting them for viewing, take the opportunity to straighten and tidy one's own hair.

【Right folio, lower section】 (Ise Monogatari, Section Sixteen, continued)

(Continued from previous page) ...yet knowing nothing of how to make his way in the world, the wife with whom he had grown accustomed to living over many years gradually distanced herself from his bed, and at last took Buddhist vows, going to the place where her elder sister, who had taken the tonsure before her, resided. (Ki no Aritsune) had not truly enjoyed a deeply intimate relationship with her, yet at this very moment of her departure, he found it deeply moving (*aware*). However, as he was so poor, there was nothing whatsoever he could do (as a parting gift).

Grieving over this, he wrote to his close friend (Ariwara no Narihira), saying: "It is for such-and-such a reason that (my wife) is departing into religious life. I cannot do even the smallest thing to send her off properly." And at the end he attached a poem:

*Te wo orite ai mishi koto wo kazofu reba / to to iitsutsu yotsu wa e ni keri*
(Counting on my fingers the years we have met and been together — saying "ten," we have added four more years besides.)

His friend (Narihira), upon reading this, found it deeply moving, and sent provisions for the night along with a poem:

*Toshi dani mo tō tote yotsu wa e ni keru wo / ikutabi kimi wo tanomi ki nu ran*
(Even the years — saying "ten," four more have been added — yet how many times over have I depended upon you?)

When this was sent, (Aritsune replied):

*Kore ya kono ama no hagoromo mube shiko so / kimi ga mikes hi to tatematsurikere*
(This must be it — the heavenly feather robe: how fitting that it should be offered up as your lordship's own robe.)

Unable to contain his joy, (Narihira) composed once more:

*Aki ya kuru tsuyu ya magau to omou made / aru wa namida no furu ni zo ari keru*
(I wondered whether autumn had come, or whether (dew) mingled in confusion — but what was falling was none other than tears of joy.)

【Left folio, upper section】 (Precepts for women)

One: For women, whether of high or low station, marriage (*totsugu*) is to be regarded as the foremost matter — the foundation upon which one's life is settled. Once having married, it is of vital importance to conduct oneself in such a way that one never returns to one's parents' home a second time. That said, when the match is ill-suited in temperament, the bond will naturally be severed — and therefore, one must examine (the prospective match) very carefully from the very beginning.
