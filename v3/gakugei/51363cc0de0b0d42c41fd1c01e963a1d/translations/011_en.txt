**[Right folio, lower section]**

*(Continued from the previous page)* — The man *(note: referring to Narihira)* had been visiting and attending upon *(note: meaning he had been growing familiar with her through repeated visits)* a woman to whom he was deeply devoted, but around the tenth day of the First Month, she concealed herself away elsewhere. He had heard tell of where the woman was hiding *(note: meaning the place where the woman had hidden herself)*, but it was not a place where one could easily come and go. So he passed the days feeling ever more despondent and heavy-hearted *(note: "nawoushi" means feeling increasingly weary and burdened, time having accumulated)*.

Then, in the First Month of the following year, at the height of the plum blossoms, longing for the previous year, he went to the place. He stood and gazed; he sat and gazed — yet nothing resembled how it had been the year before. Weeping aloud, he lay down upon the dilapidated *(note: "abara" means ruined or fallen into disrepair)* floorboards until the moon tilted in the sky, and recalling the previous year, he composed this poem:

**[Cited from the Kokinshū:]**
> *Tsuki ya aranu / haru ya mukashi no / haru naranu*
> *Waga mi hitotsu wa / moto no mi ni shite*

*(Is the moon no longer the same moon? Is this spring no longer the spring of old?*
*My own body alone remains as it always was.)*

Having composed this poem, as the night gently brightened toward dawn, he returned home, weeping all the while.

**[Section 5]**

Long ago, there was a man. He was making very secret visits to the vicinity of the Eastern Fifth Avenue *(note: this refers to the Nijō Consort)*. As it was a place for furtive rendezvous *(note: meaning a place for slipping in secretly and discreetly)*, he could not enter through the main gate; instead, he passed through a crumbled gap in the earthen wall that the servant children had broken through by trampling. The place was not particularly crowded with people, yet as his visits accumulated in number, the mistress of the house *(note: referring to the Somedono Consort)* got wind of it. Along that secret passageway, every night she posted people *(note: meaning guards were placed on watch)* to keep watch—

**[Left folio, upper section]**

*(Calligraphy and reading)* are things that neither high nor low in station can do without knowing. Beyond these, the texts one ought to read and study are poetry anthologies (*utasho*). First among them:

- **Hyakunin Isshu** *(note: compiled by Lord Fujiwara no Teika, written on shikishi cards at Mount Ogura)*
- **Ise Monogatari** *(note: regarding its author, as stated earlier in this text)*
- **Genji Monogatari** *(note: composed by Murasaki Shikibu)*

Beyond these, there must be a great many works for those who are earnest about poetry to consult. As for the imperial anthologies (*chokusen wakashū*) compiled by the command of successive emperors, they are: the *Kokinshū*, *Gosenshū*, *Shūishū*, *Goshūishū*, and further the *Kin'yōshū*, *Shikashū*, *Senzaishū*, and *Shin Kokinshū* — these together are called the "Eight Imperial Anthologies" (*Hachidaishū*). In addition to these, there are also collections known as the Thirteen Imperial Anthologies and the Twenty-one Imperial Anthologies, but it is not necessary here to set forth all their titles.
