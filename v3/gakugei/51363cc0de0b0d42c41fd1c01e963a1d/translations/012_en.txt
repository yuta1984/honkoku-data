**[Right folio, upper section]**

On the game of *Kai-ōi* ("Shell Covering"): this game cannot be played without knowledge of *The Tale of Genji*, because it is a game of matching pictures depicting scenes from Genji. It should not be called *kai-awase* ("shell matching") — the correct term is *kai-ōi* ("shell covering").

**[Cited from the Kokinshū:]**

> *yoiyoi goto ni / uchi mo ne naramu*

*(The guardian of my secret passageway —*
*the one none but I know —*
*must surely fall asleep each and every night.)*

Upon the composition of this poem, *(note: the Somedono Consort was deeply moved, and came to feel tenderness and affection for Narihira)* the mistress relented and permitted him entry. However, as the news of his secret visits to the Nijō Consort *(note: meaning Narihira had been making his visits to her)* spread as rumor throughout the world *(note: "yono kikoe" means the talk and gossip of society)*, her elder brothers had the residence placed under guard *(note: meaning they had watchmen posted)*.

**[Section 6]**

Long ago, there was a man. There was a woman he absolutely could not obtain; for many years he had continued to court her *(note: "yobai" means to call out and communicate one's feelings to a woman repeatedly)*. With great difficulty *(note: "karōshite" means having gone to considerable trouble)*, he at last stole her away *(note: it was the Nijō Consort whom he stole away)*, and set off through the pitch darkness of night. As they made their way along the river called the Akutagawa *(note: meaning he was leading her along with him)*, the woman asked the man, "What is that?" pointing to the dewdrops that lay upon the grass. The way ahead was long *(note: "ōku" means far and distant)*, and the night had grown deeply late. Not knowing that it was a place where demons dwelled, even the thunder *(note: meaning thunder above all else)* roared terribly, and the rain fell in torrents *(note: "itau" means severely, to an extreme degree)*. **[Left folio, upper section]**

The reason that the first day of the First Month is called *Ganzan* ("Three Origins") is that it marks the beginning of the year, the beginning of the month, and the beginning of the day — thus it is called the "origin of three beginnings."

On the matter of the Five Seasonal Festivals (*Gosekku*): the seventh day of the First Month is called the "Young Herb Festival" (*Wakana no Sekku*), and this is the first of the seasonal festivals.
