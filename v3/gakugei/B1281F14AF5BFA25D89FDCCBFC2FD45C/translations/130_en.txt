...there may well have been cases where people were made to believe it. According to popular tradition, it has been transmitted as a poem composed by the poet Saigyō when he was living in Sarashina:

　　*Yamagawa ni shio no michihi wa shirarekeri akikaze samuku kajika naku nari*
　　(In the mountain stream, the ebb and flow of the tide can be felt — the autumn wind is cold, and the kajika cries.)

It is unclear from which book this poem originates. Nevertheless, when I asked a certain person about the meaning of this poem, they answered: "When the tide comes in, the kajika faces upstream and cries 'gishi-gishi,' and when the tide goes out, it faces downstream and cries 'kori-kori.'" In that case, the interpretation seems to point to fish such as the gori and kiki, which makes it even less convincing as ancient evidence. As for things that make sounds in the water, these are generally frogs or waterfowl, and one does not hear of precedents for their being subjects of poetic appreciation and recitation since ancient times. That said, Saigyō was a person who composed verses freely as he pleased, and even if he likened something to some creature in his poem, there is no particular need to argue about it. Still, this poem is most likely a forgery. Furthermore, there is a poem said to be from the *Man'yōshū* — with a side note reading: *One account reads this as the "Falls of Ochiai" and transmits it as a poem composed by Kenreimon'in at Ōhara* —

　　*Yamagawa ni koishi nagaruru koro-koro to kajika naku naru tani no ochiai*
　　(In the mountain stream, pebbles flow along — koro-koro — and in the gorge's confluence, the kajika cries.)

This poem also does not exist in the *Man'yōshū*. Nor have I heard of it being recorded in any other texts.

Furthermore, in the *Fubokushū*, Volume 24, Miscellaneous Section 6, there is a poem attributed as an imperial composition by Emperor Okamoto:

　　*Ōumiji no toko no yamanaruisaya-gawa kono koro-koro ni koitsu tsu aran*
　　(On the road of Ōmi, in the Isaya River that flows by Mount Toko — in these koro-koro days, I shall remain longing.)

This has been handed down with the claim that the "koro-koro" in the poem was meant to evoke the cry of the kajika. This too is a most egregious error. This poem appears in *Man'yōshū*, Volume 4, as:

　　*Ōumiji no toko no yamanaruisaya-gawa ke no koro-koro wa koitsu tsua ramu*
　　(On the road of Ōmi, in the Isaya River that flows by Mount Toko — in these misty days of "koro-koro," I shall remain longing.)

The commentary in the *Daishōki* notes that "ke" refers to moisture/vapor, meaning river mist, and that "koro-koro" simply means "days" (koro), just as in the reading of "nemokoro-koro" — and thus this is by no means a poem about kajika. While the poetic evidence for kajika is uncertain in various respects, since there is at present no definitive proof to identify it as one thing or another, whether it be a fish or an insect, there would be no particular objection to simply following popular convention and calling whatever cries in the water in autumn a "kawa-no-shika" (river deer), or broadly speaking, a "kajika."
