(Continuing from the previous page) Known as the "*tanikuguri*" (valley-lurker) (Note 1), this is a creature that lives in mountain streams and has a most fascinating voice. Since it cries even today from summer through autumn, it is presented under the topic of autumn in the *Man'yōshū*. This is not the creature that lives in fields and marshes (*hataku*) (Note 2) and cries in an unpleasant, noisy manner. That later generations compose poems exclusively about (noisy, paddy-field) frogs shows that they do not know the poetry of antiquity.

In the *Man'yōshū*:
"*Omohoezu / kimaseshi kimi wo / Sahogawa no / kawazu kikasete / kaeshinu ru kamo*"
(You came to visit unexpectedly; having let you hear the frogs of the Saho River, I have let you return home—alas!)

Even from this poem alone, one can appreciate how charming the voice of this creature is. What is commonly called "*kashika*" today must be none other than the "*kawazu*" of antiquity. Furthermore, this creature does not yet begin to cry in spring, but cries from mid-summer through autumn. (And so forth.)

○ In my humble opinion, since I have not been able to obtain definitive proof that the "*tanikuguri*" specifically refers to a frog, I shall for the time being treat this as one theory. That it is praised for living in mountain streams and having a fascinating voice does indeed appear to accord with the spirit of the *Man'yōshū*; however, it is difficult to determine from the anthology as a whole that this creature necessarily belongs to autumn. It is the common practice in ancient, unadorned (literary style) not to deliberately distinguish between

**[Left Page]**

different species, but rather to mix them together under the same name—and examples of this are not few. Nonetheless, the poem in Book Six:
"*Omohoezu / kimaseru kimi wo / Sahogawa no / kawazu kikasete / kaeshitsuru kamo*"
and also among the love poems composed with the frog as their subject:
"*Asakasumi / kabiyadoga shita ni / naku kawazu / koe dani kikaba / ware koi me ya wa*"
(If only I could hear the voice of the frog crying beneath the deer-fire hut in the morning mist, would I be tormented by such longing?)
—these are poems that take as their subject a creature that cries in autumn, and their topic is indeed autumn.

Furthermore, in the *Gosen'shū*, under the heading of "Miscellaneous, Part Four" (*Zatsu-shi*), with the preface "On Hearing Frogs":
"*Waga yado ni / ai yadori shite / naku kawazu / yoru ni nareba ya / mono wa kanashiki*"
(O frog that shares a lodging with me at my dwelling and cries—is it because night has fallen that things feel so melancholy?)
This too can be understood as referring to an autumn creature.

Also, in the *Man'yōshū*:
"*Sahogawa no / kiyoki kawara ni / naku chidori / kawazu to futatsu / wasure kanetsumu*"
(The plovers crying on the clear riverbanks of the Saho River, and the frogs—these two things I cannot forget.)
—all of these, too, are clearly expressions of admiration for the (frog's) voice.

Phrases such as "frogs crying at the Yoshino River," "frogs crying at the Mutsuta shallows," "frogs crying at the Kaminabi River," and "frogs crying on the clear riverbanks" consistently appear paired exclusively with clear mountain-stream settings; nowhere in the entire *Man'yōshū* is the creature composed of in connection with paddy fields or wild plains.
