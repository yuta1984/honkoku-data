　The crabs (used as bait) are left with only their legs, and with stones attached, they are bound in two places with hemp cord. About three of these are attached to a hemp line of forty to fifty fathoms (approximately 73–90 meters) in length and cast into the water. The octopus, wishing to eat the crab meat, climbs onto the board. Feeling the resistance, one pulls the line up, and when it nears the shore or the water's edge, the octopus, startled and attempting to flee, becomes caught on the fishhook. In Senshu (Osaka area), this same method is used, and to catch small octopuses, cuttlefish shells and buckwheat flowers are used as bait. Around Akamagaseki (modern-day Shimonoseki, Yamaguchi Prefecture) in Chōshū, when a bonfire is lit at the bow of a boat, many octopuses gather beneath it and rise up dancing with heads erect; they are grabbed by hand, and a hooked tool is used for those out of reach. Skill in hand-catching is by far the most effective.

　○The common octopus measures about one to two shaku (approximately 30–60 cm) in size, and there are also small octopuses. In Kyoto, those sold abundantly at markets around October are called "Jūya-dako" (Ten-Night Octopus). The Chinese name is "Shōhassōgyo" (小八梢魚), also called "Rakutei" (絡蹄). Large ones are called "Seki-dako." Octopuses from the northern regions are extremely large, generally ranging from eight or nine shaku (approximately 2.4–2.7 meters) to one or two jō (approximately 3–6 meters), and they sometimes wrap around and devour people. When the warts on their tentacles touch human skin, they suck blood so rapidly that death comes instantly. They also catch and eat dogs, rats, monkeys, and horses.

[Left page]
　At night, they come out to the water's edge, lifting their bellies, raising their heads backward, glaring with their eyes, and running on their eight legs as swiftly as if flying. They enter fields and dig up and eat potatoes. Even during the day, when there are no people about, they do the same. It is said that farmers who spot them can sometimes catch them by striking with a long pole. In the "Yamato Honzō" (Great Harmony Materia Medica), it is recorded that a giant octopus from Tajima (northern Hyogo Prefecture) wrestled with a large snake coiled around a pine branch and ultimately dragged it, branch and all, into the sea.

　○The giant octopus of Toyama in Etchū Province (Namerikawa) likewise captures and eats cattle and horses, capsizes fishing boats, and seizes people. Fishermen have no technique for catching it. Therefore, they lie in the boat feigning sleep and wait; when the octopus cautiously approaches, extending its arms and striking them onto the boat, they swiftly cut off its legs with a hatchet and row home rapidly. The danger is such that life and death hang in an instant. Truly, when a brave warrior goes into battle and holds his life lighter than dust, it is for loyalty and righteousness, to uphold human morality, or to rid the world of evil. Yet compared to a single leg of an octopus, even the righteous deaths of Ki Shin and Yoshimitsu are, alas, scarcely worth counting.

　　As noted above, when a giant octopus's leg is hung from the eaves of a market stall, it hangs down long enough to trail along the ground.
