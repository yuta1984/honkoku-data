Itami may indeed be called the birthplace of fine sake (*jōshu*) in Japan. However, this too is not a tradition of great antiquity.

It originally began around the Bunroku and Keichō eras (late 16th to early 17th century). The first person to sell sake in Edo was a man named Yamanaka, from Kōnoike village, a neighboring village to Itami. When the enterprise began, he brewed merely five *to* to one *koku* at a time and sold it as a peddler. When production grew to twenty or thirty *koku*, there was more than enough to sell in the surrounding provinces, so he loaded it onto horses and traveled all the way to Edo to sell it. Unexpectedly, he gained great profit, and the payment too was loaded onto horses and brought back. As Edo grew increasingly prosperous, the volume of *koku* grew without limit, and he amassed a great fortune. Those who followed in his footsteps included establishments called Inatera-ya and Masu-ya, which were based in Itami. For shipping and transport, Ikeda's Manganji-ya was the pioneer. As sake brewers multiplied one after another, today fine products are also made in Itami, Ikeda, and others in the same province (Settsu), such as Nishinomiya, Hyōgo, Nada, and Imazū. Although there are many others in other provinces that have gained renown in their respective localities, the unique character of each region's water and soil, combined with each household's refined techniques, means that a hundred different flavors exist — as varied as human faces — and it is impossible to describe them all exhaustively.

Furthermore, it has only been about 130 years since sake began to be pressed (*shiboru*) to produce clear sake (*seishu*); before that, it was merely strained through a bamboo basket (*ikaki*). As for the sake brewed in the current era, there is *shinshu* (new sake; brewing begins around the autumn equinox), *aishu* (brewed between new sake and *kanmaezake*), *kanmaezake* (pre-cold-season sake), and *kanshū* (cold-season sake; in general, the later in the season, the more is produced and the higher the price). Among these, new sake is especially famous as a specialty of Itami, and its fragrance is exceptionally fine. It is made by selecting an auspicious day around the autumn equinox in the eighth month, and washing the koji rice four days before that day (though in recent years it has begun around the solar term of *kanro* [Cold Dew] in the ninth month).

**Sake mold (***sakekōji***)**  
*Annotation in original: In the past, it was made from wheat, which is why the character 麹 is used. The Chinese method is extremely complex, but the Japanese method is convenient.*

Around the equinox, on the morning four days before the set day of adding the *moto* (yeast starter), the rice is washed and soaked in water for one full day. The following day it is steamed into cooked rice, spread out on a straw mat, stirred and leveled with a paddle tool (*ekai*), and when it has cooled to body temperature, it is transferred entirely into the *toko* (a wooden box for rice), covered with a straw mat, and left inside the koji chamber (*muro*) for approximately half a day. Around the Hour of the Horse (noon), the clumps are broken up, and at that point *moyashi* (seed koji / koji spores) is added — approximately two *gō* per one *koku*. At around the Hour of the Ox that night (approximately 2 a.m.), it is removed from the *toko*, heaped in a mound in the center of koji trays (*kōji futa*), and stacked ten trays at a time. During the following day it is turned once, and waiting until evening, it is spread evenly across the full surface of the trays. When the trays are stacked again at an angle, by the Hour of the Tiger that night (approximately 4 a.m.), the koji will have turned yellow and white.
