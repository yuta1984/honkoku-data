**[Right Page]**

(Continuing from the previous page) What Chōmei described as the "ide-no-kawazu" (well-bank frog) in his *Mumyōshō* is precisely what is called "kashika" today.

○ The text states:
"The ide-no-kawazu is found nowhere else but only in this stream at Ide. It is somewhat dark in color and is not very large. It does not leap about openly as ordinary frogs do. It lives entirely in the water, and when it cries in the late hours of the night, its voice is remarkably clear and evokes a deep sense of *mono no aware* (pathos)." (And so forth.)

This creature is now sought in Yase in the capital (Kyoto), and the people of Naniwa (Osaka) capture it near the Tsuzumi Falls at Arima. It is, without doubt, the same creature as the "ide-no-kawazu," and there is no room for doubt that this is indeed the "kashika" of today.

Therefore, in *waka* poetry it is read as "kawatsu," and not as "kashika." The name "kashika" should be perfectly appropriate as a *haigon* (colloquial word used in haikai poetry). That the "ide-no-kawazu" was fondly admired in ancient times is seen in numerous texts. Not all of the creatures captured today at Yase, Arima, and Ide necessarily possess that (beautiful) voice; only those that cry with quiet elegance have a character distinct from the rest.

**[Left Page]**

This is a variety of toad (*hikigaeru*), bluish-black in color. Its hind feet have no webbing, all its toe-tips are round, and it lives in clear water. Its nighttime cry resembles that of the *koma-dori* (Japanese Robin), sounding like "*koro-koro*"; during the sixth and seventh months it cries once every *hitotoki* (approximately two hours) through the night. It also cries during the day, when its voice resembles that of a shrike (*mozu*). Since it is particularly quick on its feet and not easy to catch, one can only capture it by watching for the moment during the summer *doyo* period when it rests on the riverbed.

The current confusion that mixes fish with this creature stems from the spread of the *haigon* "kashika," through which people forgot the ancient "kawatsu." Since the time of Chōmei, the world has changed many times over, and in recent years people have only heard the name "kashika" and, going out to riverbanks and mountain streams, have heard the sound of the *gori-giku* fish and, because the location where it cries is the same, concluded "this must be the kashika"—from which point the original thread, tangled as disordered hemp fibers, was lost.
