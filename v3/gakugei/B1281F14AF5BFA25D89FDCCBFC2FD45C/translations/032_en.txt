## Whetstones (Toishi)
※Fine-grained ones are called "shi (砥)" and coarse ones are called "rei (礪)"

There is a saying that "whetstones are never found more than five ri from the royal capital," and the claim that they are produced in accordance with the imperial capital is not entirely without basis. In ancient times, the white stone extracted from deep within Mt. Kasuga in Yamato Province (present-day Nara) was used as a finishing whetstone for swords, but now it is no longer quarried, leaving only traces behind. Today, the finest whetstones in the land come from the area around Saga in Yamashiro Province (present-day Kyoto), specifically from Narutaki and Takao, and there are few elsewhere to compare with them. These are produced at Sakaihara-yama on the border of Yamashiro and Tanba provinces, and are called "uchikumori (inner haze)" or "asagi (light blue-green)." They are also produced at Shirotani in Tanba Province. All of these are used as finishing whetstones for swords, razors, and by carpenters and craftsmen of all kinds.

The Tozawa whetstone from Kōzuke Province (present-day Gunma Prefecture) is a high-quality stone that can be used for sharpening without water. The Nagura whetstone from Mikawa Province (present-day Aichi Prefecture) has a pale white color with mottled patterns. The Echizen whetstone, commonly called "Jōkenji," is inferior to the uchikumori. The above are all varieties of finishing whetstones (awaseto), referred to in herbal encyclopedias as "etsushi (Y越砥)."
※All of these stones have an outer skin (rind). When extracted from the mountain, they are cut into long rectangular pieces and loaded onto horses four stones at a time — this measure serves as the standard unit.

○ Green whetstones (aoto) come from Hirao, Somata, Minamimura, Monzen, Nakamura, Ide no Kuro, Yufune, and other places. Among these, Minamimura and Monzen are located about seven ri northeast of Kyoto in a mountain with a circumference of seven ri. In Tanba Province, there are many varieties from Inokura, Saiki, Ashinoyama, Ōgitani, Nagatani, Ōbuchi, Iwatani, Miyakawa, and many others. In Hizen Province (present-day Saga/Nagasaki), there is Amakusa; in Yōshū (Iyo Province, present-day Ehime), there are white and red varieties...

---

All of these are also called "medium whetstones (nakato)." The grades of quality for each are so numerous that there is no leisure to describe them all in full. Both the finishing whetstones and medium whetstones described above are found embedded in the rock and soil of mountains, so a pit (quarry) is dug at the mountain entrance, bored deep inside, and ventilation windows are opened at various points. Workers carry oil lamps made from turban shells (sazae) and follow the veins of stone — the operation is entirely comparable to mining ore in a gold mine. When the stone is exhausted, the supporting timbers and framework are removed and the mountain itself is collapsed. For this reason, the interior of the tunnels always appears ready to cave in, making it terrifying, and those who are not experienced workers in this trade shudder at the mere sight of it.

○ Items to be listed below correspond to stones suited to various uses according to their qualities. Among them, for polishing mirrors and for smoothing the joints of lacquerware, the "worm-eaten whetstone" (mushikuito) of Tsushima Island is used. Since this stone cracks when immersed in water, it cannot be used for polishing swords, but it is well suited for molds used in silver-crafting. However, for casting molds for net sinkers, the white whetstone of Iyo Province (Ehime Prefecture) is used. This white whetstone is itself a remarkable curiosity: stone chips scattered and accumulated in valleys gradually coalesce over a long period of time to form a single complete stone once again. It is therefore quite common to find curiosities such as "leaf stones" — stones formed when a leaf happened to be sandwiched between the stone chips as they bonded together — and most of these are found in this mountain.
