The mash is consolidated into four batches, and after approximately eight to nine days, it is lifted out using a lifting bucket, placed into bags, and packed into the sake press (fune). The target volume ranges from over three hundred to five hundred (units). Stones are stacked one after another on the pressing post (otoko-bashira) to gradually press the mash. What emerges from this process is clear sake (seishu). This is placed into a large settling vat called the "shichi-sun" (seven-sun) vat, and after four or five days, the product is called "araori" or alternatively "arabashiri." This is packed into four-to (four-bushel) barrels for shipment. Seven to five shō constitutes one load (ichida), which equals two barrels, and altogether approximately eleven to twelve loads are produced.

The above method describes only the practice of a single household in Itami-gō. Other households have their own closely guarded secrets, and the quantities of koku and proportions differ slightly from house to house, though the broad approach is the same. It should be noted that over one hundred years ago, the brewing volume was around eight koku to eight koku and four or five to, and forty to fifty years ago, eight koku and eight to of polished rice was considered the finest grade. Today, what is called the finest grade may reach over nine koku or even ten koku. These changes, too, are difficult to fully articulate.

The addition of clarifying ash (sumashi-bai) is done only for lower-grade sake, thin sake, or spoiled sake, and is never used for premium sake (jōshu).

Regarding the proportioning of rice additions for ai-shu (mid-season sake): in former times, the increase was three to across the board, as with new sake (shinshu). But at some point, it became standard practice to add five shō at the first yeast starter addition, one to at the middle addition, and one to and five shō at the final addition—this is now considered the best method. The same should apply to kan-mae (pre-cold-season) and kan-shu (cold-season sake). Ai-shu takes over forty days from the starter addition,

kan-mae takes over seventy days, and kan-shu takes eighty to ninety days before the sake is ready. It is especially important to adjust quantities, strategy, and the number of days based on the warmth or coldness of the given year.

However, it should be noted that in former times, before new sake (shinshu), there was a brewing method called "Botai," which was also referred to as new sake. Even today, rural sake brewers (yamaka) use only this method. In Osaka and elsewhere in former times, premium sake (jōshu) was not a drink for common people. Those who occasionally indulged had Botai sake brewed at their own households. But now, after two hundred years of peaceful rule, even those who live from day to day can drink to their hearts' content, clapping hands and calling out "banzai" (long live!) in the back alleys of town. One cannot help but feel profound gratitude to have been born in such an age.

【On Rice】
The base rice (moto-mai) uses locally sourced aged rice (furugome), with rice from Kaga, Himeji, and Awaji being preferred. For the addition rice (soe-mai), aged rice from the northern provinces is considered the finest, with rice from Akita and Kaga being especially prized. For the starter (moto) prepared from the pre-cold season onward, newly harvested grain from Takatsuki, Naya (warehouse) rice, Yodo, and mountain districts is used.
