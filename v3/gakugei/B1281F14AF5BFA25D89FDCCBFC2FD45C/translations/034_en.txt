○Reishi mushroom (saihaitake) 《Commonly called reishi (靈芝). Also known as: kameisō, fushisō, fukusō. Japanese readings: nukatodetake, sakikusa》

In the Honzō (Chinese materia medica), it is called the five-colored zhi, a divine herb. Since the Four Elders of Mount Shang gathered and ate zhi, it has become the regular food of immortals. In addition to the five colors, there is also purple zhi (shishi), making six varieties in total. Among these, purple zhi is most common. It grows from the ground, appearing in sandy or stony soil or beneath pine trees; once it grows in a spot, it returns to the same location for many years. When first sprouting it is yellow, then as days pass it takes on a reddish color, and when fully grown it becomes purplish-brown. The stem is black and lustrous, and the underside of the cap is finely textured and smooth. In terms of flavor, each of the five colors possesses one of the five tastes. This is an auspicious plant (zuisō) that blooms three times a year; it also appears in Japan's Engishiki under the section on auspicious omens. The Zuimeirei states that "when a ruler governs with benevolence and compassion, zhi grass grows"—this is precisely what is meant. ○As for its form: some grow as single, isolated specimens; others grow in clusters; others grow layered on a single stem, resembling maitake mushrooms; others produce branched stems with caps; and yet others produce only stems, growing to about three shaku in height with antler-like branches resembling a deer's horns. These are called rokkakushi (deer-antler zhi), a rare variety that was reportedly found in the mountains of Ise in recent years. In total, the varieties of zhi number around six

Saruno-koshikake (Ganoderma/bracket fungus) 　This is a variety of zhi. It grows on trees and has no stem. Large specimens can reach four or five shaku in diameter.

○Shiitake 《Also known as: kōko, kōkin, shojin》

The product of Hyūga province is considered the finest quality. Large quantities are also produced in the Kumano region. Those that grow on chinquapin (shii) trees are considered the authentic variety. However, naturally occurring ones are rare. To cultivate them, chinquapin trees are cut down and left to rot in the rain; rice-washing water is poured over them, they are covered with rush matting, and after some days the mushrooms emerge. Some are also made by cutting oak trees. After harvesting, they are not dried in the sun but rather dried by roasting over fire, which fully preserves their fragrance. "Ki-boshi" (tree-dried) refers to those dried while still on the tree, which have an exceptionally fine flavor and aroma. These are called kashinjin in Chinese.
