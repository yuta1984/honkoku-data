However, a dispute arose between the chief priest's party (神主方) and the celebrant's party (祝方) regarding the enshrinement ceremony (遷宮), which caused the ceremony to be postponed. Thereafter, in the ninth year of the Genna era (1623), the community of shrine people (社人中) carried out the reconstruction, and the enshrinement was performed by the chief priests Minbu and Roppo. Down to the time of the present reconstruction carried out by the Shogunate (御公儀), that shrine has been one built and maintained by the community of shrine people.

One item: The claim that we have unlawfully appropriated (押領) over five hundred koku of household domain (家領) is, above all, most objectionable. As we have already stated, we submitted a cadastral declaration (指出) based on the documentary records (旧記) from the Tenshō era (1573–1592), and were granted the amount declared therein. Furthermore, after repeated official investigations (御吟味), we received the successive generations of vermillion-seal documents (御朱印) and have held the domain as our lawful tenure (知行) ever since — yet we are now charged with unlawful appropriation. If holding land under the authority of the Shogun's vermillion seal is to be called unlawful appropriation, then every person throughout all of Japan who holds land by grant would likewise be an unlawful appropriator. Moreover, the claim written in the petition (目安) that there was no such thing as "household domain" (家領) under prior precedent is a complete falsehood beyond all reason. From ancient times, these lands have been referred to variously as household domain (家領), private domain (私領), or individually inherited holdings (則別相伝), and shrine officers (社司), shrine households (社家), and shrine monks (社僧) alike all possess household domains. Documentary evidence can be produced for inspection. These are lands transmitted as individual inheritances within each household, separate from the communally rotated fields (廻田), and it is for this reason that they are called household domains...
