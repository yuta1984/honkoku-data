(Continued from the previous page: the visit to the residence of Soga Kizaemon)

They entered through the back gate on Senbon-dōri and went down to the right, where on the western side of the outer waiting area, a nameplate reading "Soga Kizaemon / same [household] Yahei" was displayed. They were announced, shown into a reception room, and after a short wait, Kizaemon returned home and came to meet them. After exchanging the usual pleasantries, they explained the matter of Koyama-gō in detail. He acknowledged each point and responded: "In any case, since this concerns the fixed-rate tax (jōmen) that has been in place since the Kanbun era, it has become an established precedent over the generations. However much the villagers may petition, it would be difficult to readily accept a request for reassessment (mikake). I believe there are also established precedents among the court nobility and others against accepting such reassessments. I will consult further with my colleagues and make inquiries of those well-versed in such matters, and will then convey our views to you."

Hodo stated: "As you are aware, Koyama-gō has a total assessed yield (kokudaka) of 650 koku, of which 561 koku, 4 to, 4 shō, 5 gō belongs to the divine domain (shinryō). The remaining 80-odd koku is held by eight proprietors (happon-sho) under our care, including the Hōkyōji Palace, the Reizei household, and others such as Kitano, Shōbaiin, and Daitokuji. Ōno-gō has a total yield of 300 koku, of which over 250 koku is divine domain, making the combined divine domain over 817 koku in total. Of this, the田地(farmland) whose cultivation rights (shitaji) are held by the villagers of Koyama amounts to over 416 koku, while the remaining over 400 koku represents land whose cultivation rights are held by Kamo-gōchū (the Kamo community). Against the assessed yield of over 416 koku, the fixed delivery of rice (nōmai) has been over 254 koku, which when averaged out amounts to approximately 61 percent (roku-bu ichi-rin). Within this, there are approximately 27 plots of land classified as 'takanari' (high-yield or over-assessed land), yielding over 36 koku in rice deliveries, and this situation has continued for many years. Whether 'takanari' exists elsewhere, I cannot say. Of course, these plots are all large fields, and it is my understanding that because there remains a surplus even after paying the high-rate levy, the takanari payment has been maintained. If the rice from these plots were to be consolidated as repair rice (shūfuku-momi) for our shrine, or paid collectively, a reassessment or a slight reduction in the tax rate could be managed, though it would cause some inconvenience. However, since it is distributed in small amounts to individual shrine families — one koku here, two koku there, or five to or three to per allocation — and since the tax rates on these lands vary (seven-tenths, eight-tenths, or five-tenths), and since the takanari payments are made separately, we find this to be particularly burdensome. Furthermore, as the villagers say, whereas formerly there were several hundred villagers, they have declined to barely over forty people due to impoverishment, and they are now overextended in cultivating the land. If things continue to deteriorate in this way, we are anxious about the continuity of the divine domain. According to what the villagers say: even if the land were assessed by actual inspection (mitōri) this year, they would work hard on favorable fields while neglecting unfavorable ones — leaving them to poor cultivation — which would be very troublesome. Perhaps there are similar precedents elsewhere; or perhaps, since the number of villagers has declined considerably, this may be a fitting time to submit a formal petition. It was with the intention of consulting privately and borrowing your wise counsel that we have opened up this matter and sought your opinion."

To this he replied: "Indeed, I have heard this in detail. I had heard of it before through informal accounts. To speak thus — it may seem presumptuous to ask how your parties feel — but I appreciate that even a small reduction from a modest stipend causes great difficulty. Regarding takanari, there are also such cases in the lands under my administration. Although they are not rice fields, the areas of Higashi-wakinawate, Honto-machi, and the Gion vicinity are all cases of 'sukumi' (compressed or reduced plots). This is something that exists anywhere. It is because those fields are particularly large. An overall average rate of 61 percent is not what one would call a high tax rate. In the lands under my administration, there are also a few cases near 'Hanakaragake' in a jointly-held area with the lower Reizei household — I believe it is around 65 percent or so. Elsewhere, around Mibu and Tōji, most areas deliver at seven- or eight-tenths. I have heard that Koyama-gō consists mainly of superior upper fields (jōden), and yet, perhaps because it is a 'take no funyū-sho' (a bamboo administrative district with special exemption status?), I have heard there are places where one 'tan' is counted as three 'tan.' As the villagers have fallen into poverty in recent years, there are fields with an assessed yield of five to that actually produce two koku of crop revenue (sakutoku); yet they attach about two to of the five-to assessed value and transfer the land to others, so that unknowingly, townspeople or court nobles end up holding land with an assessed yield of only two or three to while delivering three or four koku. In any case, I have thoroughly understood the gist of your intentions. Given that this is a matter of a long-standing fixed-rate tax assessment..." (continued)
