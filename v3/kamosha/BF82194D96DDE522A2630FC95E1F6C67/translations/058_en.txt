In the seventh month of the fourth year of Kanji (1090), during the reign of Emperor Horikawa, 745 chō of fuyuden (tax-exempt fields) from various provinces were dedicated as sacred supply fields (gokuden). This was due to a shortfall in shrine tax revenues. Beyond this, there were also several additional donations made to the various scattered establishments of the Mikuriya-dokoro at different times, but since the middle ages (chūko), virtually all of these have fallen into ruin and desuetude — a state of affairs we find most lamentable. Moreover, there exist numerous regulatory documents (kakubumi) prohibiting lawless monks (ransō) and slaughterers (tosha) from residing within the four boundaries of the shrine precincts, and forbidding the pollution of the Kamo River — too many to enumerate here.

Regarding the ceremonies of imperial visits (gyōkō and miyuki): while various historical sources cite the fifth year of Tengyō (942), during the reign of Emperor Suzaku, as the origin (ransho) of imperial visits to shrines,

there were also imperial visits (gyōkō) during the Enryaku era (782–806) of Emperor Kanmu's reign, and in the imperial founding document (okoshibumi) it is written: "The relocation of the imperial capital (miyako) to

During the reign of Emperor Juntoku, in the Kenryaku–Kenpō era (1211–1219),

the Retired Emperor (Go-Toba) repeatedly visited in person the waterside pavilion (senten) of Negi (shrine priest) Suketuna. Suketuna was elevated to Junior Second Rank (Jūnii) in the first month of the third year of Jōkyū (1221). Such are the venerable and auspicious connections the shrine has enjoyed. As for other matters such as the divine authority (shinni) and miraculous efficacy (reigen) of the shrine, these are known to all, and I shall accordingly omit them here.
