The skewers, six by six (thirty-six in total), represent the three hundred and sixty days of the year, and five grains are used. This may be for prayers for a successful harvest of the five grains. This should be investigated further.

One, regarding the formal presentation ceremony on the fifth day of the horse racing meet: when attending, a ceremonial tray (sanpō) bearing konbu (kelp), katsu-guri (dried chestnuts), noshi (abalone), myōga (ginger bud), and salt-cured sea bream is passed in a ceremonial manner. The meaning of this ritual offering lies in wordplay: myōga ("叶う" - to grant wishes), the auspicious "kachi-guri" ("勝ち" - victory), "yorokonbu" (konbu = "喜ぶ" - to rejoice), and "medetai" (tai/sea bream = "めでたい" - auspicious). These are all celebratory expressions.

One, during Shinto rituals, tabi (split-toe socks) are not worn. It goes without saying that shitōzu (court socks) are worn with formal court dress (sokutai) and ceremonial cap and robe (ikan). However, this practice was problematic for samurai households.

【Left Page】
Needless to say, since warriors wear straw sandals (waraji) during military campaigns and horseback riding, from the time of the Ōnin War onward, the samurai class designated these as "tabi," splitting the area at the big toe, and came to use them exclusively. Since braided leather stirrup straps (itokohai) are used in the horse racing meet, it may be acceptable to wear tabi.

One, it is said that there are taboo words regarding curtains during Shinto rituals. Viewing curtains are said to be "held up" (motsuberu); raising them is called "shiboru" (wringing/drawing); storing them away is called "osameru" (putting away). Ship curtains are said to be "run out" (hashirakasu); one's own side's curtains are called "utsu" (striking); the enemy's are called "hiku" (pulling). Mourning ceremony curtains are called "haru" (stretching). One must never say "hazusu" (removing) or "tatamu" (folding) for any of them.
