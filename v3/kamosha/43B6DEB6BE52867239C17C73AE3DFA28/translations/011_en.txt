Article: The matter of the reconciliation between shrine officers and clansmen at the Kantō (i.e., the Edo Shogunate)

In the fourth year of Kanbun (1664), when a delegation traveled to the Kantō to pursue litigation regarding repairs to this shrine, this same dispute was raised once more. At that time, a deliberation (僉議) was convened, and the ancient records, genealogies, and other historical evidence were found to be unambiguous. Furthermore, the principle enshrined in the imperial rescript — namely, that persons of the same surname be treated equally in a single line, with offices transferred in sequential order (次第転任) — was shown to be clearly established shrine practice with solid grounding, and a definitive ruling had already been issued.

At this juncture, those then holding the aforementioned offices came together to lament, arguing that if the ancient rule of sequential transfer were applied and the many clansmen were all to be appointed to shrine offices, those who had served in recent times would be permanently reduced to ruin (沈淪). This argument was not without its logic. Accordingly, the following persons — Matsushita, Moritori (森鳥居), Ōji (大路), Hayashi, and additionally Umetsuji, Tomino, Shaka (社加), Okamoto, Miyauchi, and Chikamei (親明) — having transferred from the status of clansman to shrine office and risen to the rank of chief priest, were established as the model precedent (亀鑑). These seven houses in total were designated as those entitled to serve as negi and hafuri of the Honmiya (main shrine), the Go-no-miya (five subsidiary shrines), and the shrines of Kataoka and Kibune.
