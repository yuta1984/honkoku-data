Article: On the reconciliation between the shrine officials (shashi), uji clan members, and the Kantō (the shogunate).

In the fourth year of Kanbun (1664), when representatives traveled to Kantō to pursue a lawsuit regarding repairs to this shrine, this same dispute over shrine office appointments arose once again. At that time, a full deliberation (僉議) was conducted, and the old documentary evidence from shrine records and ancient genealogies was found to be clear and unambiguous. Moreover, the purport of the imperial rescript — namely, that the established shrine practice of appointments within a single line of the same surname proceeding through successive ranks (次第転任) — was plainly evident and confirmed as factual, and a definitive ruling was already handed down. However, the incumbents of that time presented a collective petition of grievance, arguing that if the ancient rule of successive transfer were applied and large numbers of uji clan members were all appointed to shrine offices, those who had faithfully served in recent times would be permanently reduced to ruin and obscurity. This argument too was not without its own merits.

Accordingly, it was determined that the following — namely, those of the families Matsushita, Mori, Torii, Ōji, Hayashi, and further Umetsuji, Tomino, as well as Shaka, Okamoto, Miyauchi, and Chikamei — that is, those who had transferred from the status of uji clan members to shrine offices and risen to the position of chief priest (kannagi) — comprising in all seven houses, should be entitled to serve as negi and hafuri of the Five Shrines of the Main Shrine (Honmiya) together with Kataoka and Kibune shrines.
