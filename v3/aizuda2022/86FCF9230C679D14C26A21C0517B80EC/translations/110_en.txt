In accordance with the arrangements established in advance, the village headmen (*nanushi*) and laborers (*ninpu*) of each village were to be assembled together, each village's identifying banner (*mejirushi-hata*) raised, and [the assembled parties] were to proceed swiftly to their respectively assigned posts at shore batteries (*daiba*), naval station headquarters (*funade go-jinya*), and other such locations. Furthermore, regarding the distribution of provisions (*hyōrō*), and after investigating those in possession of large iron cauldrons (*ōgama*) at the headmen's residences and elsewhere, locations for field kitchens (*taki-dashi*) were designated in advance, with arrangements made for rotation [of personnel and supplies] to those cooking locations so that, upon arrival, matters could be executed smoothly and without confusion, and the allocation of personnel (*hito-wari*) was accordingly established.

As the first order of business in the newly acquired domain territories, since the regulatory ordinances (*go-seirei*) had not yet been consolidated, [the administration] drew upon the five-household-group regulations (*go-ninGumi okite*) applicable to shogunate-administered lands (*go-ryōsho*) and the longstanding governance methods (*go-shihō*) of successive generations of the Aizu domain, and—combining the practices of Aizu administration with local customs (*dozoku no furiAi*), the shogunate's laws for its own territories (*kōgi go-ryōhō*), and the established customs of the previous domain lord's administration—conducted thorough deliberations and promulgated a set of regulations (*go-jōmoku*).

Regarding the domain's territories: up to this point, [the lands had been] interspersed in a complex, interlocking fashion—like the interlocking teeth of dogs (*kengyo no gotoku*)—among the territories of the Lord of Matsudaira Yamato-no-Kami and the stipend lands (*kyūchi*) of scores of shogunal bannermen (*hatamoto*), to such an extent that even within a single village, [the land] was held by two different lords. In the case of such stipend lands, [the holders] concerned themselves solely with collecting rice and monetary dues, with absolutely no attention given to moral instruction or education (*kyōka*) of the people, and no effective control over wrongdoers. Licentiousness (*in-fū*) and gambling (*bakueki*) were openly practiced, and the local customs were such that [the people] showed no discernment between right and wrong.
