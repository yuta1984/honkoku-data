...were it to come to such a pass, it would go against the intentions of the Edo authorities, and we would be unable to make amends. Although it was deeply regrettable, it was an unavoidable circumstance. Furthermore, those who had been stationed at this location — including Heibei, Tokunai, the guards, and the craftsmen — had originally planned to wrap up matters and withdraw after our departure. However, as stated above, since we would be remaining until the very last possible moment when a crossing would no longer be feasible and would then withdraw, if we were to leave even one person from Japan behind in a state from which they could easily cross over later, all our efforts would have been for naught, and there was a considerable risk of such an outcome. Therefore, it was arranged that every single Japanese person would withdraw together without exception. We were told at length and with great care that, while Orinojō-dono would no doubt find this most regrettable, since it came to this after every effort had been made, it was unavoidable, and that we should understand we would be putting in at whichever harbor in the Tsugaru domain proved most convenient. Since this was the unavoidable state of affairs, we gave our reluctant assent, and turning to the matter of withdrawal, we carefully attended to clearing out, cleaning, and putting the camp in order — burying refuse, dismantling the hearths — and from the morning of the 23rd we ordered the ship's crew to begin cooking and provisioning, and made ready the vessels. At around the Hour of the Snake (approximately 10 a.m.), word came that the wind conditions were favorable. We therefore went to call upon Denjūrō-dono, Heibei-dono, and Tokunai, conveyed our reports, and expressed our sincere gratitude for their efforts during the period of encampment. Together, we all burned incense at the graves of those who had died on this island, and then boarded the ships.
