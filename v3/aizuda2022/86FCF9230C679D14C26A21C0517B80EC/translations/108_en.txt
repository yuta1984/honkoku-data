...should there be an inspection of weapons aboard ships departing [from the garrison], it would cause delay in operational timing (*jiki*); therefore, following consultations (*o-kakiai*) with the Uraga Magistrates (*bugyō-shū*), it was arranged that such inspections would take place upon returning after the matter had been concluded. When a foreign ship (*ikoku-sen*) was sighted, the area to the west of Ōshima Island would be the responsibility of our domain (*o-ie*, i.e., the Aizu domain), and the area to the east would be the responsibility of the Shirakawa domain (*Shirakawa-ke*), with each dispatching an inspection vessel (*kenshi-sen*); furthermore, both sides were to dispatch scout vessels (*monomi-bune*) to ascertain the situation. In the event that, during the night or in dense fog and the like, it could not be determined from which direction a vessel had entered, an inspection vessel was to be dispatched as soon as it was sighted. Upon initial sortie, our domain would display a yellow flag (*ki-hata*), and the Shirakawa domain would display a white quarter-flag (*shiro-shihan no shirushi*). Should the foreign ship appear to be entering in full force or to be offering resistance (*temuka*), our domain would switch to a red flag (*aka-hata*), and the Shirakawa domain would switch to a flag bearing a silver circle (*gin-maru*) as its mark, thus signaling to one another. At that point, the shore batteries (*daiba*) would not need to wait for further reports on the situation but were to open fire and repel [the vessel]. In the event of a sudden emergency (*fuji*), both domains' shore batteries were to fire signal rockets (*noroshi*), and additionally dispatch fast boats (*hayabune*) to deliver urgent reports without delay. Should wind and waves be rough, making it difficult to send reports between Jōgashima Island and Sunosaki Cape, fast land couriers (*riku hayagake*) were to be sent to notify Kannonsaki and Hyakushu; from those locations, fast boats were to set out blowing conch shells (*kai*) and displaying conspicuous signal flags (*medatsu hata*). If the wind and waves were particularly severe and it proved impossible to land on the opposite shore, a signal gun (*gōhō*) was to be fired from aboard ship to signal the emergency, and upon hearing it, a corresponding signal gun was to be fired on land, with a reception boat (*mukae-bune*) promptly dispatched to receive and ascertain the situation.
