Is there anyone in the world who does not enjoy the moon and flowers? (Surely there is not.) Among all such people, the garden that the retired Shōshō [Lieutenant General/Major Counselor] lovingly tended has exhausted every refinement of both Chinese (*kara*) and Japanese (*yamato*) elegance, and its vistas of spring and autumn are said to be without peer—so it has long been told. Kibun of Kōnantei, ever frequenting this place and at the request of the master of the house, composed poems for various spots within the garden, and also depicted the scenery of blossoms and autumn foliage. Upon viewing those works—wrought by the master's own hand—one's heart could not help but be stirred, and this has long been so. Now then, even were one to attempt to enumerate each of these beautiful spots one by one, words would surely fall short. Yet, first crossing the immovable Bridge of a Thousand Ages (*chitose no iwahashi*), taking the branch of early-blooming plum as one's guide—plum that begins to flower even within the year—one seeks out the village of incomparable fragrance, and in the garden of matchless color and scent, [one finds] the garment-cap fashioned in imitation of the flowers the warbler is said to sew [i.e., plum blossoms]. The willow, from the inner gate (*uchimikado*) onward, is well known by name; dividing one's way along the path beneath the blossoms stretching far into the distance from the spring-wind pond (*harukaze no ike*), one can well imagine the scene where places such as Kumo-ga-fuchi [Spider's Deep] and Nishiki-jima [Brocade Island] cast their reflections upon the water. A heron flying through the wisp-like clouds that rise from the crags—how one would wish to tread the bridge of blossoms crossing the shores of Kashima. How precious and regrettable that one cannot! The Yamaake Barrier (*yamake no seki*), whose color speaks beyond words, cannot be passed without tears, and the hill known as Nomanji (*nomanjū*)…
