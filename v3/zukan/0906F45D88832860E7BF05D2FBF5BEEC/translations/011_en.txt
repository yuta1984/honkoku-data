In summer it produces fruit. The fruit is initially green and, upon ripening, becomes purplish-red. The inside is hollow. Although it is called "mukwaika" (fig, 無花果, "flower-less fruit"), it does in fact produce small white flowers at the tips of the branches in spring. The form of these flowers is not clearly discernible, and thus the plant received this name. Further details are omitted here. Among plants that have separate male and female individuals, there are some in which the female bears fruit without visible flowering. This is noted under the entry for the公孫樹 (kōsonju / ichō, the ginkgo tree).

**The *Nanfang Caomu Zhuang* (南方草木状, "Accounts of Plants of the Southern Regions") states:**

"The榕樹 (rongshù) is widely planted in Nanhai and Guilin. Its leaves resemble those of the muma (木麻), and its fruit resembles that of the winter-green (冬青, dongqing). Its branches and trunk are gnarled and twisted, and the grain of its trunk is deeply furrowed. When burned, it produces no flame. Its shade covers ten mu of ground. Its branches and stems grow densely, and its leaves are likewise lush. Its fine and supple branches hang down like vines, gradually reaching the ground. When the tips of these vine-like branches enter the soil, they form roots and nodes. A single large tree may develop roots at four or five separate points. When a lateral branch reaches a neighboring tree, it joins with it to form a 'linked-grain' (連理, lianli) union. The people of the south regard this as an ordinary occurrence and do not call the tree an auspicious wood (瑞木)."

**The *Wuli Xiaozhi* (物理小識, "Minor Records of the Principles of Things") states:**

"In Min (Fujian province) and Guang (Guangdong province), the榕 is abundant. When its hanging branches reach the ground, they again become roots. Over a long span of time, they swell and become bulbous; hence the tree is called the 'Undying Tree' (不死木, bùsǐ mù). In Guilin there is a 'Rongshù Gate' (榕樹門), and in Changxi there is a 'Rongshù Hermitage' (榕樹菴). When used as firewood, it burns particularly well, but only fine branches, once dried out by popping (explosive drying), can be used to make fuses (火縄, fire-rope)."
