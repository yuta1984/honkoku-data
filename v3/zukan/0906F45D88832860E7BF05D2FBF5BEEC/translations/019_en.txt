…It resembles the bitter orange (橙) and has a pleasant fragrance, lovely enough to be cherished. The flesh is extremely thick and white, like a daikon radish, but even more coarse and spongy in texture. Though the flavor is mild, the fragrance is superb; if placed inside a clothing chest, the scent will not fade for several days. When sent to the northern regions, people treasure it greatly.

**Left Page (左丁):**

**Paulownia (桐, *tō* / *kiri*)** 　[From the *Bencao Gangmu* / *Honzō Kōmoku*]

It is planted in abundance everywhere, and is well-suited to dry ground far from water. In spring, the flowers bloom first, with the leaves following afterward. The flowers are tubular in shape, purple, with five petals; the calyx is tea-brown in color and covered with fine hairs, resembling woolen cloth (rassha). The leaves are broad and large, reaching over one shaku in size, green in color, covered with downy hairs, and somewhat sticky. The interior of the stem is hollow. When autumn arrives, buds form, and the leaves promptly fall.

According to one account, when making a koto (zither / Japanese stringed instrument) from this wood, one should use timber from trees that have habitually been exposed to the sounds of bells and stone chimes (shō and kei); the resulting instrument will produce a tone of exceptional clarity and beauty.

Furthermore, when this tree is cut down, it readily sends forth new shoots and grows back easily. It is for this reason that the *Wakun Gikai* (a Japanese lexicographical work) states: "*kiri* means 'to cut' (*kiru*)." Even when propagated by cuttings, the plant will take root; and even after several days since being felled, it will not wither and die, but will instead produce fresh new leaves.
