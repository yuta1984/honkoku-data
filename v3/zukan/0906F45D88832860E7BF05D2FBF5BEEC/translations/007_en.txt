**Right Folio**

(Continued from previous page) …The mistletoe (*yadorigi*) is also found on paper mulberry (*kashi*), cherry (*sakura*), plum (*ume*), pear (*nashi*), hackberry (*enoki*), and other trees, and varies in form depending on the host tree. This is a transformed growth from the residual vital energy (*yoki*) of the tree. The *onashinkazura* (dodder / *Cuscuta* sp.) — called *matsura* in the "Explanatory Names" (*shakumyō*) section — grows out from between the bark of trees in deep mountains and hangs down like threads. It too is a parasitic plant.

> [From the *Honzō Kōmoku*, "Explanatory Names" section]: Li Shizhen states: "According to Wang Anshi's *Zishuo* (Dictionary of Characters), pine (*matsu*) and cypress (*kashiwa*) are the chiefs of the hundred trees. The pine is like a duke (*kō*), and the cypress is like an earl (*haku*). Hence the character for pine is written with the element for 'duke' (*kō*), and cypress with the element for 'white' (*haku*)."

> [From the same work, "Collected Commentaries" (*shūkai*) section]: Li Shizhen states: "The pine tree is rugged and gnarled, tall and straight, with many knots. Its bark is coarse and thick, with a scale-like texture. Its leaves do not fall early. In the second or third month it puts forth buds and produces flowers four to five *sun* (approx. 12–15 cm) in length. The pollen from these flowers is gathered together and called *shōkō* (pine pollen / pine yellow). It bears fruit shaped like a pig's heart, with overlapping scales layered in tiers. In autumn, as the cone ages, the seeds grow larger and the scales split open. However, the leaves differ: there are varieties with two needles, three needles, and five needles per bundle. The three-needled variety is called *katsushimatsu*, and the five-needled variety is called *shōshimatsu* (the stone pine / Korean pine). Their seeds are roughly the size of cypress seeds; however, those from the Liaodong coast and Yunnan produce seeds as large as *badou* (Croton tiglium seeds), which are edible, and are called *kaimatsu-shi* (maritime pine seeds / *haematsu no mi*). The *Baopuzi* states: 'Of all things, the finest resin is that which naturally accumulates within the bark of old pines — superior to that which is chiseled out or produced by boiling. Among these, the finest of all is the so-called *inshi* (yin resin), found in damaged spots beneath the roots of old pines that are shielded from sunlight and moonlight.' As for old [pines]…"

**Marginalia (upper right, boxed):**

In Kasugai District of this domain (the Owari Domain), at Kigasaki in Moriyama Village, within the precincts of Ryōjusen — Chōmo Zen Temple — various trees produce growths resembling the leaves of Japanese cypress (*hinoki*) among their branches and leaves. These are indeed parasitic growths (*yadorigi*). It may be that they arise due to the nature of the local soil. However, as the temple has a popular tradition regarding this phenomenon and considers it a wonder, it is treated separately and not recorded in this main entry (on pine).
