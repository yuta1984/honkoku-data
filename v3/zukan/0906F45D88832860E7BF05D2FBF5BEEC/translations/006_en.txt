Its leaves are deep blue-green and long. The red pine (*mematsu*) has thin bark, and its leaves are pale blue-green and short. It is an error among common people to regard these two as female and male; both contain resin. Furthermore, beneath the roots of old pines grows *bukuryō* (poria mushroom / *Wolfiporia extensa*, known as *matsuhodo*). This is the residual energy of the pine. In spring, the pine puts forth new buds and produces flowers. Afterward, when new leaves appear, white foam forms at the base of the new shoots, and within that foam there are invariably two insects — one male and one female. By summer they undergo metamorphosis and disperse. They resemble a small yellow horsefly. These insects are most numerous on the red pine (*mematsu*). They are rare on varieties such as the five-needled pine (*goha no matsu*) [cited in *Senshū Fushi*], the white-frosted pine (*shimofuri matsu*) [cited in *Kai-i Kō*], and the Korean pine (*Chōsen matsu*) [cited in *Honzō Kōmoku*]. Furthermore, pine cones are produced during the summer and emerge at a different time from the flowers. They ripen the following year. Among related varieties, there are also those that produce cones in winter. Additionally, sometimes dozens of bud-like growths appear between the branches,

their color resembling the bark of a pine. This is probably a transformed growth of the buds. Also, sometimes from the scars of severed branches of old pines, mushrooms (*kusakira*) grow from summer through autumn. These are called "*matsurōshi*" (Pine Elder Mushroom). Their form is no different from the matsutake mushroom (*Tricholoma matsutake*) that grows on the ground, but their texture is extremely hard. It is popularly said that eating them prolongs life, or that when decocted and taken as medicine, they relieve epidemic fever (*ekinetsu*).

Furthermore, the mistletoe (*yadorigi*) lodges upon the branches and trunk of the pine. Its leaves grow in opposite pairs, narrow and slender, pale green in color. In summer it opens deep crimson tubular flowers (*tsutsu-hana*), blooming in great density. The base of each petal is itself the fruit, which is blue-green. When it ripens between the following year's spring and summer, it turns deep crimson. The inside of the fruit is extremely sticky. It falls onto the branches and trunk and sprouts naturally, resembling the first growth of a barnyard millet seedling. Its roots are like tendrils.
