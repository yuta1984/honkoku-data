When the lotus produces a "double-tiered" flower (jūdai, where the petals are layered upon one another) or "twin" flowers (sōsō, where two blooms appear together), people of the world regard these as auspicious omens. Among all flowers, the lotus holds the highest rank in terms of purity and whiteness; nothing equals it in this regard. For this reason, if impure water is poured upon it, it will immediately wither. It also abhors saltiness. As Zhou Maoshu's "Essay on Loving the Lotus" (*Ai Lian Shuo*) states: "The lotus is the gentleman (junzi) among flowers." One cannot but admire it.

The *Bencao Gangmu* (Honzō Kōmoku), in its section on the explanation of names, states: Li Shizhen says: In the *Erya*, "he" (荷) is given as the name for the root; Han's interpretation takes "he" as the name for the leaf; Lu Ji takes "he" as the name for the stem. Upon examination, the stem is that which bears (负) the leaves, carrying the sense of "bearing a load" (負荷, fùhè), so we should follow Lu Ji's interpretation. "Mi" (蔤) refers to the tender young rhizome, which extends like bamboo's underground runners. From each node, two stems are produced: one becomes a leaf, the other becomes a flower, and at the point where they are exhausted, the rhizome (ǒu, 藕) is produced. This serves as the root of the flowers, leaves, roots, and fruits—manifesting benevolence while storing its function inwardly; it accomplishes its task without dwelling upon it, and can truly be said to "withdraw into concealment in the depths." Therefore it is called "mi" (蔤). Flowers and leaves always grow in pairs; they do not grow without pairs. Hence the root is called "ǒu" (藕). It is also said that because the rhizome is adept at working through the mud, the character follows "ǒu" (耦), meaning "to plow." The character "qié" (茄) is pronounced "jiā," meaning it is added on top of the "mi." The character "xià" (蕸) is pronounced "xiá," meaning it is distant from the "mi." "Hàndàn" (菡萏) conveys the idea of being closed and not yet opened. "Fúróng" (芙蓉) conveys the idea of spreading out and displaying glamorous beauty. "Lián" (蓮) means "to connect" (連), referring to the way flowers and fruits emerge in a connected series. "Dì" (菂) means "target" (的):

The *Honchō Shokkan* (本朝食鑑, Mirror of Foods of Our Country) states: The largest of the roots can be as big as a forearm. When boiled or steamed until cooked, they turn a pale black color. However, if one adds a small amount of strong vinegar to boiling water and boils the lotus root two or three times before removing it, the root will remain white and not turn black.
