**Right page:**

The leaves resemble those of *Lobelia chinensis* (半辺蓮, *mizukakushi*). In spring, small white flowers open between each pair of leaves, roughly resembling the flowers of bush clover (*Lespedeza*; *hagi no hana*). Afterward, flat fruits are produced. Occasionally, something resembling a fruit forms, within which there is an insect. Its form resembles that of a grain weevil (*Sitophilus granarius*; *kokuzo-mushi*), and it later undergoes metamorphosis and disperses by flight.

Plants such as *Rostellularia procumbens* (爵牀, *inu-kōju*) [from *Bencao Gangmu*], *Rorippa* or *nogiku* (雞兒膓) [from *Jiuhuang Bencao*], and *tanuki-mame* [Chinese name unknown], occasionally develop swollen, bloated stems (*hōchō*) within which insects are generated. There are further examples of this kind, but they are omitted here. Things that generate insect nests (*chūka*; *mushi no su*) are also found within wood, and these will be listed in a later volume.

**The *Jijie* (Collected Explanations) section of the *Bencao Gangmu* states, citing Li Shizhen:**

This plant grows abundantly in mountain fields and open plains. In the second month, shoots emerge from perennial roots and form clumps. The stems grow upright, are white in color, and reach a height of four to five *shaku* (approximately 120–150 cm). The leaves spread in four directions, resembling those of *Artemisia* (*hao*), and are divided into five pointed lobes, with additional small points above. The upper surface is green and the underside is white, covered with soft down, and the leaves are soft and thick. In the seventh and eighth months, spikes emerge from among the leaves, bearing fine flowers resembling the spikes of *Plantago* (車前, *ōbako*); fruits form and fill the branches in succession. Inside are fine seeds. The plant does not wither until after the first frost. It is customarily cut down together with its stems on the fifth day of the fifth month, dried in the sun, and the leaves are stored.

**Left page:**

The *Jingchu Suishiji* (*Traditions of the Jing-Chu Region Through the Year*) by Zong Lin states: On the fifth day of the fifth month, before the cock crows, one gathers mugwort; any piece that resembles a human figure is plucked and kept. This is collected and used for moxibustion (*okyu*), with excellent results. On this day, mugwort fashioned into a human figure and hung above the doorway can ward off noxious vapors (*dokki*; malevolent airs). When the dry stems are soaked in hempseed oil and used to ignite the moxa cone (*kyūchū*), they moisten the moxibustion wound and relieve pain until it heals. The stems may also be used as a substitute for divination stalks (*shisaku*; yarrow stalks used in *I Ching* divination), or as candle wicks.
