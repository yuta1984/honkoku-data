Furthermore, to regard Ryōkotsuboku (龍骨木 / Kirinkaku / *Euphorbia antiquorum*) as a variety of Senninshō (仙人掌 / cactus) is an error; they are separate species. The *Karekihyakuei* states: among the varieties of Haōju (覇王樹), the one that is square in cross-section and long, resembling an iron truncheon (*kan*), is called Haōben (覇王鞭 / "Tyrant-King's Whip"). The name Haōju (Tyrant-King Tree) is an alternate name for the Senninshō (cactus), while Haōben is an alternate name for Ryōkotsuboku (Kirinkaku). Most likely, because their natures are similar to one another, some have classified them as one species.

Ryōkotsuboku (Kirinkaku) originates in southern lands and is exceedingly sensitive to cold. In winter it must be stored in a heated greenhouse (*muro*), and water must not be applied — the same as with Senninshō (cactus). It puts forth branches that twist, one to the left and one to the right, and is deep green in color. In summer it grows upward from the ridged tips. It produces leaves along its five ridges (*goryō*), with spines (*hōshi*) at their base. After autumn the leaves fall, and above the leaf scars (*yōkon*) on the ridged tips, small flowers open. They are five-petaled, brownish in color, and put forth crimson stamens; the petals are firm and thick, and do not fall. They do not bear fruit.

Stored in a warm greenhouse (*muro*), this plant thrives most vigorously. Plants growing in warm countries will likely open flowers among their leaves in midsummer. When a branch is broken, a white sap emerges. If the cutting is dried in the sun briefly and then planted as a cutting (*sashime*), it will root immediately. According to one account, this plant too is said to ward off fire. What is today called "Shishi" (Lion) is a variety of this species.

Both of these plants (Senninshō and Ryōkotsuboku) contain water within their own bodies, and in winter they do not favor being watered. If a plant appears to be withering and rotting, applying a heated iron implement (*tetsuka* / cauterizing iron tongs) to the affected area will immediately revive it. It is also said that Hōbishō (鳳尾蕉 / sago palm / *Cycas revoluta*) [*Bichuan Huajing*] likewise wards off fire, and when it appears to be withering and rotting, if iron filings (*kanakuso*) are worked into the soil around it and the roots are lightly scorched, it will revive immediately. All of these are, by their inherent nature, exceedingly resistant to fire and fond of the essence of iron (*tetsuki*). This is most likely because Metal (*kin/kane*) engenders Water (*mizu*) [according to the five-phases cycle of mutual generation], and therefore these plants are said to ward off fire.
