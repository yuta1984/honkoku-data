**Sha'i / Satō (砂糖) — Sugar**

This name is common to both Japanese and Chinese usage, and there are several varieties. What Li Shizhen (李時珍) describes refers to brown/black sugar (*kurozatō*). In the *Tiangong Kaiwu* (天工開物, *The Exploitation of the Works of Nature*), it is called *hóngtáng* (紅糖); in the *Zunsheng Bajian* (遵生八牋), it is called "black sugar" (*kurozatō*); and in the *Runan Pushi* (汝南圃史), it is called *kokutō* (黒糖). Regarding methods of production, details can be found in the *Tiangong Kaiwu*, the *Satō Seizakki* (砂糖製作記, *Record of Sugar Production*), and Hiraga Gennai's *Butsurui Hinshitsu* (物類品騭). Sugar is imported from China, Western countries, the Ryūkyū Islands, and the Netherlands, and is also produced in Satsuma Province (present-day Kagoshima). The varieties are exceedingly numerous. The finest grade, *Daihaku* (大白), is called "Jōhaku" (上白) in the *Minshu* (閩書, *Records of Fujian*), and is also known as *seitō* (清糖, "clear sugar"); in the *Guangdong Xinyu* (広東新語, *New Accounts of Guangdong*), it is called *yángtáng* (洋糖, "ocean sugar"). The middle grade, *Chūhaku* (中白), is called "Chūhaku" in the *Minshu* and also known as *kantō* (官糖, "official sugar"). The lowest grade is called "Kahaku" (下白) in the *Minshu* and also known as *funbi* (奮尾); in the *Guangdong Xinyu* it is called "□bi" (□尾). Both imported and domestically produced varieties include several further sub-grades.

**Sekimitsu — Kōrizatō (Rock Candy/Crystal Sugar) — *Kandeisoikuru*** *(Dutch term, see gloss)*

Details of the production method are found in the *Tiangong Kaiwu*. High-quality white sugar is boiled down, egg whites are added to remove impurities, green bamboo is cut into pieces of slightly over one *sun* (approx. 3 cm) and split lengthwise, then dropped into the boiling liquid. After being left for one night, it becomes crystal sugar (*kōrizatō*), so it is said. When this crystal sugar is further broken into pieces, it is called *kōriōroshi* (砕き氷糖).

**Kyōtō (饗餹)** *(from the Jijie/集解commentary)* — **Aruhei**

This product is made by boiling white sugar into a syrup-like consistency, spreading it in a mold, coloring it in five colors, and shaping it into forms resembling plants, fish, insects, and other creatures. It is offered to people at both auspicious and inauspicious occasions, and also sent as gifts to distant places. This is mentioned in Li Shizhen's account as well as in the *Guangdong Xinyu*.

**Shimitsu (刺蜜) — Manna** *(Dutch name)*

The Japanese-made variety is not well documented. It is said that manna is extracted from a type of tree described in Dutch sources under the category of aromatic woods — known in Dutch botanical literature as the *tolubakou* (兜婁婆香), called in Japanese *kurumi* or *kawakurumi* (walnut / river walnut).
