**【Right Page】**

**Watermelon (西瓜, *suika*)** — *suika*; *saiuri* (Osaka dialect); *Seitōka* (from *Runan Pushi*);
　　　　　*Tensei Byakko-tō* (from *Xigua Faming*); *Byakko-tō* (from *Zunsheng Bajian*)

According to the writings of Kaibara [Ekiken], the Japanese Buddhist monk Gidō composed a poem about watermelons. However, upon reflection, if this variety first arrived from abroad during the Kan'ei era (1624–1644), watermelons would not yet have been present in Japan during Gidō's time—so what exactly he was writing about is unclear. It is possible that the plant arrived in ancient times, then disappeared, and was reintroduced in recent years. This remains uncertain. It is said that watermelon seeds were first cultivated in Kyoto during the Kanbun and Enpō eras (1661–1681), and that they are now grown in abundance.

Today there are many varieties of watermelon. Seeds are sown in the fourth month. The leaves resemble those of the wild watermelon seedling (*Hibiscus trionum*) but are larger; they also resemble the leaves of *Abelmoschus manihot* (tororo-aoi), with many lobes and deep incisions. The flowers resemble those of winter melon (*tōgua*). The fruits ripen in the sixth and seventh months. The skin is deep green, the flesh is white, the juice (*jō*) is red-crimson, and the seeds are black. This is the common watermelon.

One variety, specifically from Kizu, has a yellow rind with red flesh, and is called "Kizu watermelon." Another variety found in Ise Province is said to have both a yellow rind and yellow flesh, but this is considered inferior in quality. Yet another variety, in which both the rind and flesh are yellow but the seeds are red, is called "*akabuka*" and is considered the finest quality.

**【Left Page】**

In Chikugo Province there is a variety called "long watermelon" (*naga-suika*). It measures five to six *sun* in circumference and approximately one *shaku* in length. The rind is pale green and resembles that of the Oriental pickling melon (*etsuka*/shiro-uri). The flesh is light pink and of fine flavor. This is also called "Nankin." It corresponds to the "snow melon" (*setsuка*) described in the *Seikōshi*, and is the variety Li Shizhen describes as "reaching up to two *shaku* in length."

The variety called "Nankin" in Unshū (Izumo Province) and Chikuzen Province resembles the common variety but has a thin rind, with flesh that is red and has a lustrous, powdery appearance, and a sweet and delicious flavor.

One variety:
The flesh, rind, and pulp are all the same [as the common variety], except that the seeds bear black spots.
