**Goji / Chinese Wolfberry** (*Kūki* / *Kuko*; *Lycium chinense*)

Names: *Numigusuri* (from *Wamyōshō*); *Kuko* (a phonetic shift from the Sino-Japanese reading *kūki*); *Tōguko* (colloquial name); *Kusai* 苦菜 (*Tōnga*); *Shōsai* 象柴 (*Kōbokushi*); *Shunro* 純盧 (same source); *Senninshō* 仙人杖 (*Sōmokusho*); *Reirō* 霊龐 (*Meibutsu Hōgen* — name for the fruit); *Chisei* 地精 (*Igaku Nyūmon*); *Tenseishi* 天精子 (*Kōso Hikki*, citing *Kōin*).

The branches and trunk resemble vines in their suppleness, and when fully grown they can reach a length of approximately two *jō* (about six meters). The plant grows in dense clumps from a single root. In spring, young shoots emerge; the leaves resemble those of the pomegranate in shape and are soft in texture. In summer, clusters of three or four flowers bloom among the leaves. Each individual flower resembles a bellflower (*Platycodon grandiflorus*) in shape, is very small, and is pale purple in color. Afterward, berries are formed. In shape they resemble cherries but are larger and somewhat elongated. When ripe, they turn red. The taste is both tart and sweet. This variety corresponds to what the Song-dynasty scholar Su Song described when he said: "Those whose fruits are long in shape and whose branches bear no thorns are the true *kuko* (wolfberry)."
