In the marine plants section of *Yamato Honzō*, it states: marine algae are recorded in the *Honzō*, and the "nanoriso" mentioned in the Jijie (collective commentary) is not the same as white cogon grass. Nanoriso appears in the *Nihon Shoki* (Chronicles of Japan), in the records of Emperor Ingyō. The Yamato people also called it "shinmesō" (divine horse weed). Because the Japanese name was called "nanoriso," it was written as "shinmekusa" (神馬草) with the meaning of "do not ride a divine horse." The *Kagakushū* states: when Empress Jingū attacked foreign lands, there was no fodder (makusa) for horses on the ship, so she took algae from the sea to feed the horses, and thus it was called "divine horse weed." Raijin (等信) says he has not yet seen the source of this theory. Because it is written "shinmesō," people associate it [with nanoriso], not knowing the original reason why it was named nanoriso in the *Nihon Shoki*, and speak carelessly. In general, the theory of the *Kagakushū* is hard to trust. In Volume 7 of the *Man'yōshū*, nanoriso is composed in poetry. The aged form of this plant is called hodawara. In Japanese custom, this is piled on the spring tray (harunobан) at New Year. Because the *Imyō Bunruishō* records "white cogon" as nanoriso, everyone came to understand shinmesō (divine horse weed) and white cogon as one and the same thing; however, there is a difference between marine algae and mountain grass.

　　　　　　Cogon　The *Taisheikyō* states: cogon, also known by the name "white feather grass"
　　　　　　　《Interlinear: The reading of 茅 is the reverse of Bakō; Japanese name: chi (智)》
　　　　　　　　I say: "chisuge" (智菅) is "chikaya" (官茅), a separate plant. White cogon, sedge cogon,
　　　　　　　　yellow cogon, and fragrant cogon — these four types are all separate species.
　　　　　　　　White cogon grows in fields, meadows, and on embankments; in spring,
　　　　　　　　children and young women (minokusa) pull it out and take the cogon spikes (tsubana)
　　　　　　　　as a pastime of field play. The Japanese name is chi (智).
