## *Senninbō* (仙人帽; "Hermit's Cap" Mushroom)
**Interlinear note: *Bō mōmoku* (cap with net pattern); Kyoto dialect names: *Kinukasa-sō*, *Shiketake*, *Komusō-take***

Its shape is as if wearing a cap (cowl). It is white, with the lower base tinged reddish-brown. Both cap and stem are thin, resembling a mesh of threads. The stem has wrinkled striations. It is said to grow in shaded areas. The reddish-brown coloration at the base is likely characteristic of a mature specimen. Collected from a damp, wooded area of my own garden on the 7th day of the 8th month of the *Hinoto-tori* year (1837), and drawn from life.

---

**[From the *Bencao Gangmu* (本草綱目)]** The *Bencao Gangmu* states: When cooking mushrooms, if ginger shavings or grains of cooked rice are added and they turn black, the mushroom is lethal. If this does not occur, there is no poison. Those that emit light in the night are also toxic. Those that are not well cooked through are also toxic. Those that cast no shadow when illuminated after cooking are also toxic. Those with hairs on top but no markings on the underside are also toxic. Those that curl upward at the edges and turn red are also toxic. All of these are poisonous; for those who have been poisoned by them, *dishang* (地漿; water extracted from the earth) and excrement juice serve as antidotes.

**[From the *Wuzazu* (五雑俎)]** The *Wuzazu* states: In the *jiaqing yihai* year (嘉定乙亥), a monk named Deming went into the mountains and suddenly discovered a remarkable mushroom. He returned and served it to his companions. The poison took effect, and more than ten people died. Deming repeatedly tasted excrement (as an antidote) and barely managed to survive. There was a Japanese monk named Jōshin, who would rather die than defile himself (by eating excrement), and he resisted even as the poison tore through his skin, dying before he would submit. Even now the monastery preserves his Japanese *dochō* (度牒; ordination certificate). The monk's surname was Taira; he was a monk of Genshōji Temple in Kamimori-gō, Yukika County, Sagami Province, Japan. That he would rather die an unnatural death than soil his mouth is reminiscent of the spirit of *Zhen Zhongzi* (陳仲子) and his principles.

## *Tsukiyotake* (月夜蕈; "Moonlit Night Mushroom")
**Interlinear note: Also known as *Hotarubi-shiba* (蛍火芝) and listed in the *Botan-pu*; *Tsukiyo-take*; toxic mushroom**

It is illustrated in Sakamoto Katsuzen's *Kinpu* (菌譜). Its shape resembles a large *Shimeji* mushroom, and it grows abundantly in the 8th month. If someone mistakes it for *Shimeji* and eats it, they will certainly suffer vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain; in severe cases, it is fatal. This mushroom emits light at night, hence the name *Tsukiyotake* ("moonlit-night mushroom"). It is also called *Hayasuke-take*. In former times, a person named Hayasuke ate this and died on the spot.

I (Mototoshi/Genju) was returning from medicinal plant collection in Yashū (Shimotsuke Province) and, together with my friend *Rekisai*, obtained this mushroom. We brought it home and served it as a dish with sake. All who ate it suffered vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain. We decocted birch bark and had them drink it, which effected a cure.

Subsequently, upon obtaining this mushroom again, I asked a local person, who said: "This is called *Tsukiyo-take*; it is poisonous and no one eats it. If you eat it, you will die." He also said: "It grows commonly in flat, grassy fields with reed grasses, or on fallen rotting logs. It is difficult to distinguish (from edible species), so one must be careful."

〇 (Written by) Mototoshi (元寿). On the 29th day of the 8th month of the *Hinoto-tori* year of Tenpō 8 (1837), I set out for mushroom hunting in the mountains of Nerima Village, Itabashi, in Bukō (Edo). I searched for rare specimens for about one *ri* throughout the mountains. In the flat grasslands among the reed grass and dwarf bamboo, I found this mushroom. When I asked our guide, a farmer named Matsugoro, he did not know what it was. After returning from the mountain, I visited Matsugoro's house and asked the name. The local people told me: "This mushroom is a poison mushroom; no one eats it. The name is said to be *Hikari-take* ('glowing mushroom')." Upon reflection, if it is called *Hikari-take* because it glows at night, this would be a corrupted pronunciation of *Tsukiyo-take*, and this is undoubtedly that very mushroom. When immersed in water, the water turns blue-green.

This is a highly poisonous mushroom. In the event of consuming the most poisonous mushrooms, boil them thoroughly; if one or two rushes (燈心; lamp wicks made of rush pith) are placed in the water during boiling, the color of the rushes will change. If the color does not change, there is said to be no poison. It is also said that if a silver *kōgai* (笄; hair pin) is placed in and boiled, the silver will turn black. However—if you know something is a poisonous mushroom, do not eat it.
