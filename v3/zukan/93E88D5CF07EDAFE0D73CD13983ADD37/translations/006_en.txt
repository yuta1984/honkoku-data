【Left Folio】
　　　　Drawn from life on the sixth day of the seventh month of Kinoto-Hitsuji [1835].

The *Gafu* (Picture Manual) states: Reishi is a product of the immortals. One should gather it from the mountains, place it in a basket, set it atop a steaming rice vessel (*eshiki*), steam it until cooked through, then dry it in the sun. Stored in this manner, it will not decay.

In the fourth year of Tenchō (827 CE), in the eighth month, during the reign of Emperor Junna, Ōeda no Ason Fusakuni presented four stalks of sacred fungus (*shisō*). The largest among them was about two *shaku* in length and of a purple-vermilion color. At the base it was a single stalk, but further up it branched repeatedly, frequently having nodes about one *sun* apart. It was bent and not straight; toward the tip it was somewhat whitish. And so forth.

The *Wuzazu* (Five Miscellanies) states: *Zhi* belongs to the same class as common fungi and mushrooms, and is not in itself a difficult thing to obtain. It is only because it occasionally appears growing, unexpectedly, on the beams inside a house that it has come to be regarded as an auspicious omen. As for *zhi* that grow naturally in clusters on rotting logs in mountain valleys, nourished by rain, fumigated by morning and evening mists — there is nothing particularly remarkable about them. During the Jingde era of the Song dynasty, when the *Tianshu* (Heavenly Writings) craze flourished, Ding Wei presented *zhi* offerings that reached over one hundred thousand specimens. During the Zhenghe era, when the *Huashi Gang* (the requisitioning of rare rocks and flowers) was in vogue, provincial officials and Ji Wenzhong gathered as many as three hundred thousand specimens, including some with thousands of fronds in a single plant, bearing the full range of colors. Can such things truly be called auspicious omens?

　　　In our own country, reishi grows abundantly in mountain forests throughout the land, and also grows plentifully on the fir trees (*momi*) in the gardens of private homes. From my own observation, it most commonly grows at the roots of standing trees. Reishi and *Saru-no-koshikake* (Monkey's Seat / bracket fungus) belong to the same family; however, reishi has a stem (stipe), while *Saru-no-koshikake* does not. Their properties are otherwise the same. Among reishi there are additional varieties: Red Zhi, Yellow Zhi, White Zhi, Black Zhi, Purple Zhi, and so forth.
