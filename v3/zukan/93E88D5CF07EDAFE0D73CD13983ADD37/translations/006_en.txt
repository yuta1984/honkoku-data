　　Reishi (霊芝) — also known as:
　　　　　　Saiwaitake (福草, "Lucky Mushroom")
　　　　　　Mannentake (万年蕈, "Ten-Thousand-Year Mushroom")
　　　　　　Kichijoтake (吉祥蕈, "Auspicious Mushroom")
　　　　　　Kadoidetake (門出蕈, "Departure Mushroom") [A regional Tanba dialect term, as the mushroom was customarily given as a gift to celebrate someone's departure on a journey]
　　　　　　Nekoshakushi (猫杓子, "Cat's Ladle")

**Left folio**
　　　　Drawn from life on the sixth day of the seventh month of Kinoto-Hitsuji year [i.e., 1835].

　The *Gafu* (画譜, Illustrated Compendium) states: Reishi is an article of celestial quality. It is gathered from the mountains, placed in a basket, and set upon a steamer (飯甑, *eshiki*); after steaming thoroughly and drying in the sun, it can be stored without decay.

　In the fourth year of the Tenchō era (827 CE) of Emperor Junna's reign, in the eighth month, Ōeda no Ason Fusanari presented four specimens of reishi grass. Among them, the largest was about two *shaku* [approximately 60 cm] in height, of a purple-vermillion color, with a single base that branched as it grew upward, bearing nodes spaced about one *sun* [approximately 3 cm] apart. It was bent and curved rather than straight, and the tips were somewhat whitish — so it was recorded.

　The *Gozatsuso* (五雑俎) states: Reishi belongs to the same category as ordinary fungi and mushrooms; it is not inherently difficult to obtain. It is only considered auspicious because it grows unexpectedly between the rafters of buildings, in places where one would not think to find it. As for those that grow naturally in clusters on rotting logs in mountain valleys, moistened by rain, with morning and evening mists and vapors causing them to develop five colors of their own accord — there is nothing particularly remarkable about this. During the Jingde era of the Song dynasty, when the *Tianshu* (heavenly writings) craze arose, Ding Wei presented reishi to the throne in quantities reaching over one hundred thousand specimens. During the Zhenghe era, when the *Huashigang* (flower and rock tribute) craze arose, the provincial administrator Ji Wenzhong collected as many as three hundred thousand specimens, with some single specimens having thousands of caps displaying every color imaginable — yet can such things truly be called auspicious omens?

　　　In our country, reishi grows abundantly in mountain forests throughout various regions, and often grows in garden borders at private residences, and frequently upon Japanese fir (*momi*, *Abies firma*) trees. From my own observation, it most often grows at the bases of standing trees. Reishi and *sarunokoske* (猿の腰掛, bracket fungus) are of the same type, but reishi has a stalk (茎, *tsuka*), while bracket fungus does not. Their properties are otherwise similar. There are further varieties of reishi, including: Red Reishi (紅芝), Yellow Reishi (黄芝), White Reishi (白芝), Black Reishi (黒芝), and Purple Reishi (紫芝).
