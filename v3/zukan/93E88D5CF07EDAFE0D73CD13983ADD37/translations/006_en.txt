　　　　Drawn from life on the sixth day of the seventh month, year of Kinoto-Hitsuji (乙未壬, 1835)

The *Gafu* (Illustrated Manual) states: "Snow Fungus (雪芝) is a product of the immortals' realm. Collect it from the mountains, place it in a basket (蘿), set it atop a rice steamer (飯甑), steam it until thoroughly dried, then expose it to air to dry further and store it — it will not decay."

In the eighth month of the fourth year of Tenchō (天長4, 827 CE), during the reign of Emperor Junna, Ōeda no Ason Fusanari presented four specimens of sacred fungus (芝草) to the court. Among them, the largest was approximately two *shaku* (about 60 cm) tall, of a purple-vermillion color, with the base joined into one stem, unbranched, with nodes at intervals of approximately one *sun* (about 3 cm) along the stalk, bending flexibly and not growing straight, and appearing somewhat whitish — or so the record states.

The *Gozatsuso* (五雑俎) states: "Fungus (芝) belongs to the same class as mushrooms and reeds, and is not by nature difficult to obtain. It is merely regarded as auspicious because it grows unexpectedly in indoor spaces and is found by chance. Those that grow naturally in clusters on rotting wood and mud in mountain valleys, suffused morning and evening by clouds and mist until they develop five colors of their own accord, are not particularly worthy of wonder. During the Jingde era of the Song dynasty, when the Tianshu (天書, 'Heavenly Writings') craze flourished, Ding Wei presented fungal specimens until they numbered over one hundred thousand. During the Zhenghe era, when the Huashi Gang (花石綱, the imperial transport of garden rocks and flowers) was in vogue, the various provincial governors and Ji Wenzhong amassed and presented three hundred thousand specimens. Among these was said to be one specimen bearing thousands of leaflets arranged in the shape of an elephant. Can such things truly be called auspicious omens?"

　　　In our country (Japan), these fungi grow abundantly in mountain forests throughout the land, and also grow prolifically on Japanese fir trees (樅, *Abies firma*) in garden spaces near residences. From my own observation, they grow most commonly at the base of standing trees. The *Reishi* (芝) and the Monkey's Bench fungus (猿の腰掛, bracket fungus) belong to the same category: the *Reishi* has a stalk, whereas the Monkey's Bench does not. Their properties are otherwise the same. There are further varieties of *Reishi*, including Red *Reishi* (紅芝), Yellow *Reishi* (黄芝), White *Reishi* (白芝), Black *Reishi* (黒芝), and Purple *Reishi* (紫芝), among others.
