**From "Rokubutsu Shinshi" (六物新志)**

**Eburiko (エブリコ / 喧蒲里歌)**

Moshitsu [the author's scholarly name] notes: "Eburiko" (唾蒲里歌) is a product of the island of Ezo, which belongs to our country (Japan). In our country it is called the "tree fungus of the Ezo pine." It has long been used by the world at large as something possessing remarkable efficacy. However, among all the Chinese materia medica, local gazetteers, and similar works, there is not one that has ever recorded an explanation of it. Therefore, its physical properties and principal therapeutic uses could not be clearly determined. Moreover, those who used it merely followed oral traditions passed down among common people, without any reliable basis.

In the spring, the third month, of the year Hinoe-Saru of the An'ei era (安永丙申, 1776), when the Dutch came to present tribute, the medical official Katsuragawa-kun brought this substance and inquired of the Dutch physician surnamed "Kareru-Hitere" (カレルヒテレ / Carolus Thunberg?), given name "Toiniruke" (トイニルケ). The physician replied: "This is what we call 'Agaricus' (亜互里歌斯) in our country." Upon hearing this, I [the author] then cross-referenced it with that country's *Sōmoku-ryu* (草木琉, herbal/botanical encyclopedia) and

[continuing from lower section] the work of "Hiettsu" (窓葉都 / Hiets), whereupon both the illustrations and descriptions matched perfectly with what the physician had said, leaving no room whatsoever for doubt.

The work of "Oettsu" (窩葉都 / Oets) states: In Latin it is called either "Acaricas" (亜互里歌斯 / Agaricus) or "Acarica-müheke-tabiris" (亜互里歌謨敵傑太弼里斯 / Agaricus officinalis?), which in our country is called "Rorukeni-Zuamumei" (魯爾更聚墨). This is the "Zuamume" (聚墨)

[continuing from lower section] that grows on the "Rorukeni" (魯爾更) tree. 《Marginal note: This translates as "tree fungus" (木耳).》 The substance is produced in the mountain valleys and forest foothills of places such as "Teroru" (恒羅児 / Tyrol) and "Swarutosiniel" (斯窓児咄星協爾 / Schwarzenberg?) 《Marginal note: Both are place names belonging to the European continent.》 People from our country and from "Umarusuland" (物角私卵度) 《Marginal note: a place name》 trade for it there and distribute it in all directions. [The rest is omitted.]

Among the Ezo-produced Eburiko, there are individual pieces weighing five or six *kin* (approximately 3–4 kg). I have personally observed these. The Western-produced specimens, even when large, are no bigger than a fist (奉), yet their superior quality is greatly praised. Pharmacies further divide this product into three grades. The coarsest,

[continuing from lower section] and lowest grade, is called "Acarico-mumesanumu" (亜互里歌謨墨紗奴謨); the middle grade is called "Hinumu-Akari

Ki" (虚奴謨亜互里吉); and the finest grade is called "Rōshimuramu-Acariki" (蝋修羅模亜互里吉). Both the middle and upper grades are considered superior quality.

The "Rorukeni" (魯角更) tree is called "Roikisu" (路伊吉斯 / *Larix* / Larch) in Latin. Both the works of "Hibuneru" (吸蒲涅児 / Hübner?) and "Shinemeru" (削墨児 / Schömer?) state: The "Rorukeni" tree grows in the high mountains of "Switsāland" (斯微泄角卵度 / Switzerland) and in "Aruben" (亜児迸 / the Alps) 《Marginal note: a mountain name; this mountain is exceedingly high, lying on the border of France (拂郎) and

《Marginal note: "Serumania" (入尓瑪泥亜 / Germania/Germany)》, "Tausene" (達烏虚涅), "○Raroru" (位羅羅児) 《Marginal note: a place name, belonging to the European continent》, and similar locations. The tree stands tall and straight; its bark is coarse and thick, reddish on the outer surface, and fragrant. From the middle of the trunk upward to the crown, branches grow on all sides, rising vigorously, with their tips drooping downward, exactly like a willow (楊柳). The leaves are slender and grow densely, like hair. They are somewhat yellowish and fragrant. In spring the tree puts forth leaves and flowers; the flowers are reddish with a tinge of pale ivory, and by summer the tree bears fruit. The fruit's shape closely resembles that of a pine cone (松毬), arranged in a fish-scale pattern. The "Ezo pine" (野作松 / Ezo Matsu) is often found at nurseries (植木屋), but only as small bonsai specimens,

[continuing from lower section] making it impossible to observe the tree's full form—which is a pity.

○ The Ezo people universally call Eburiko "Tahoshi" (多色失 / Taboshi). Whenever a local person falls ill, regardless of whether the condition is of the yin or yang type, deficiency or excess,

[continuing from lower section] cold or heat, regardless of which part of the body is affected, and regardless of whether the cause involves *ki* (vital energy), blood, water, fire, internal damage, or external factors—they simply use this substance together with "Ichimato" (伊結麻 / イチマト) 《Marginal note: some say its Chinese name is 牛皮消 (niúpíxiāo); whether this is correct is unknown》

to treat the illness, and reportedly have no need for any other medicine.

○ Thanks to the explanations of Western physicians, I have been able to thoroughly understand the properties and efficacy of this substance. Each time it is used, those who obtain a mild purgative effect and thereby achieve results are beyond counting. When made into a tablet (錠), its effect is particularly rapid. Furthermore, taking the two ingredients Eburiko and dried ginger (乾姜), pulverizing each finely in the quantities described above, mixing with paste, and forming into pills is also effective. Moreover, adding Eburiko to the *Shimotsu-tō* (四物湯, "Four Substance Decoction") is highly effective in treating women who experience intense abdominal pain before menstruation comes earlier than expected. This is a folk remedy passed down among common people; however, having tried it repeatedly, its effect is miraculous. I therefore record it here.

○ Eburiko produced in Nambu (南部, southern Mutsu Province / present-day Iwate Prefecture) is no different from the Ezo product in form, appearance, and flavor. This is perhaps because that region shares the same climate and atmosphere as Matsumae and Ezo.
