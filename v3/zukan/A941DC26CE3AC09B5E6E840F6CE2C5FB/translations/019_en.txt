The "Wakan Sansai Zue" (Illustrated Encyclopedia of Japan and China), section on Water Fruits, states:
　　Jì (Hishi) — [pronunciation: Qi] — also known as: Líng, Water Chestnut, Shayō
　　　　　　　　Japanese name: Hishi (比之)

　　　　　　　　　　Those without horns (corners) are named Mikawa-bishi.
　　　　　　　　　　Wild water chestnuts grow naturally in lakes; cultivated ones
　　　　　　　　　　are grown mainly in embankment ponds.


The "Yamato Honzō" (Great Japanese Herbal) states:
　　Jì-shí (Hishi) — alternate name: Líng — also written as 菱


The "Wamyō Ruijushō" (Japanese Names Classified) states:
　　Hishi (菱子) — The "Shuowen" (Explanatory Dictionary) states: Líng [pronunciation: Líng]
　　　　　　　　　[In some regions] called Xiè [two readings: jiē and hòu],
　　　　　　　　　In Chǔ it is called ■ [pronounced Qí; also written as 芰].
　　　　　　　　　Japanese name: Hishi (比之)


The "Jiuhuang Bencao" (Herbal for Relief of Famine) states:
　　Líng-kē (Hishi)


The "Imyō Bunruishō" (Classified Glossary of Alternative Names) states:
　　Misumogusa (美須毛久佐)


　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　The "Yamato Honzō" states:
　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　Recorded in the section on [Water Plants]


　　　　　The "Kyokutei Saijiki" (Kyokutei's Seasonal Record) states:
　　　　　　　　The water chestnut flower turns away from the sun, [opening/closing] 
　　　　　　　　through day and night, shifting in accordance with the moon. [From the 
　　　　　　　　Bencao.] The leaves of the water chestnut open during the day and close 
　　　　　　　　at night. The yellow flowers close together during the day and [open] at 
　　　　　　　　night. Therefore, the water chestnut is of cold nature, while the Gordon 
　　　　　　　　Euryale (oni-basu) is of warm nature. This is contrary to the explanation 
　　　　　　　　in the Senki Ruisho Bencao. [Baien] Miwа says: Considering this 
　　　　　　　　explanation, the water chestnut flower blooms most vigorously at night. 
　　　　　　　　Its leaves close at night with the stems naturally drawing inward. This 
　　　　　　　　accords with the explanation in the Bencao. This illustration is taken as a 
　　　　　　　　true rendering of the plant at midnight.
