## 【SAKURA (Cherry Blossom / *Prunus* spp.)】

According to *Wamyō Ruijushō*:
　　Sakura (桜) — The *Mojishūryaku* states: the character 桜 〔fanqie reading: "Ūkei no han"; Japanese name: Sakura (佐久良)〕
　　The fruit is about the size of a cypress nut (柏), and comes in red, white, and black varieties.

According to *Zōho Tashiki-hen*, Section on Trees:
　　Sakura 〔Reading: Ō / Ei〕　Japanese name: Sakura (左久羅)

　In China (Tang), it appears that the cherry blossom does not exist, as it is not recorded in the *Honzō* (materia medica), *Sansai Zue* (Encyclopaedia of Three Realms), *Sōmoku Gafu* (Illustrated Album of Plants), or similar works, and Chinese poets also did not compose verses in its praise.

〔Seal: Flowering Tree〕

　　　Copied from life on the twenty-seventh day of the second month, Mizunoe-uma year (1822)

### A poem by Sòng Liǎn (宋景濂) on cherry blossoms:

　　In Japan, the appreciation of cherry blossoms flourishes more than in China;
　　It is as if the glory of the peony combines with the beauty of the crabapple.
　　This is perhaps what even Zhào Chāng could not paint —
　　When the spring breeze just stirs, it blows the snow-like petals and carries their fragrance.

---

### A poem on mountain cherries by Wáng Jīnggōng (Wang Anshi / 王荊公):

　　The mountain cherry clings to the rocks, casting shade upon the pine boughs;
　　Compared to other flowers, it blooms the latest.
　　Only the spring wind, disliking the solitude,
　　Blows its fragrance across the water to let people know it is there.

　○ From these poems, it is evident that China was not entirely without cherry trees.

---

Emperor Heizei composed the first known Japanese imperial poem on cherry blossoms:

　　Dwelling long ago beneath the secluded cliffs,
　　Its brilliant light illuminated the four directions.
　　Suddenly encountering those who would pluck its branches,
　　It smiles through the three months of spring.
　　The fragrance it sends forth — now much, now little —
　　Its drooping shade falling from branches long and short.
　　How is it that this singular thing
　　Holds such beauty sovereign over the spring scene?

---

The *Nihon Kōki* (Later Chronicles of Japan) states: In the second month of the third year of Kōnin (弘仁三年, 812), Emperor Saga made an excursion to Shinsen-en Garden, where he viewed the flowering trees and commanded men of letters to compose poems on the flowers. The banquet of flowers (*hana no en*) is said to have originated from this occasion.

## 【KAWASAKURA (Birch-bark Cherry / *Prunus jamasakura* or similar)】

Kawasakura (樺桜)　〔Reading: Wa; Japanese name: Kawa (加波); also called Kaniwa (加仁波)〕

　This is what is referred to as the single-petaled cherry (hitoe-zakura). Separately,
　there is a tree called "Kaba" (樺, birch), which is a different species from the cherry.
　It is recorded in the miscellaneous trees (*zatsumoku*) section of *Yamato Honzō*.
　The *Jìhuì* (字彙, Chinese character dictionary) states: "Kaba (樺) is a tree name; its bark is said to be suitable for wrapping bows."
　This suggests it may be an entirely separate kind of tree. In the *Wamyō Shō* (Wamyō Ruijushō) it is also treated as a separate species, yet apart from cherry bark, I have yet to see any other tree that could properly be called "Kaba."

---

According to *Yamato Honzō*:
　　In the poem "Setting Out Early from Dìngshān" (早発定山) by Shěn Xiūwén (Shěn Yuē / 沈休文) in the *Wénxuǎn* (Anthology of Literature), the line reads: "The mountain cherry opens, as if about to burst into flame." The annotation explains: "A fruit-tree by name. Its flowers are vermillion, like fire about to ignite."
　　In a poem by Wáng Jīnggōng (Wang Anshi): "The mountain cherry clings to the rock and is reflected in the pine branches."
　　In a poem by Sīmǎ Wēngōng (Sima Guang / 司馬温公): "The red cherry scatters as the apricot blossoms open."
　　From these, it is clear that what China calls "cherry" (桜) refers to a tree with vermillion flowers.
　　The cherry as known in Japan (with white or pale pink flowers) does not exist in China; in the Enpō era (1673–1681), He Qīngfǔ, who came to Nagasaki, reportedly said: "If such a tree existed in China, it would certainly have been recorded in Chinese books and celebrated in poetry." This [observation about *kawasakura*] would appear to be a well-founded account.
　　In Korea, the tree is said to exist and is called "Naki" (奈木).
