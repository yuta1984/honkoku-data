## 【AOKI — Japanese Laurel (Aucuba japonica)】

From the *Zōho Tashiki-hen* (Expanded Encyclopaedia of Plant Names), Trees section:
　　　　　Aoki (阿於幾)

[Seal] Garden Tree

According to *Yamato Honzō* (Great Japanese Herbal):
　　《Ahaki (檍) / Aoki》　Alternate name: Mannenbranch (万年枝, "Ten-thousand-year branch")
　　　　　　　　　Also called: Tōsei (冬青, "Winter-green")
　　　　　　　　　Its flower is called:
　　　　　　　　　　Mannen-hana (万年花, "Ten-thousand-year flower")
　　　　　　　　　　　Also simply: Aoki (青木, "Green tree")

The *Tōyō-sango* (桃葉珊瑚, "peach-leaf coral plant") belongs to the same category
as *Ōba-aoki* (large-leaved aoki), *Koba-aoki* (small-leaved aoki),
and *Nagaba-aoki* (long-leaved aoki).
The *Setsuri-sango* (雪裏珊瑚, "coral-in-snow") is commonly called
*Shimofuri-aoki* (霜降青木, "frost-speckled aoki") in Japan;
its leaves are mostly found on large-leaved varieties,
and it is not a variegated (spotted) variety but grows as a solitary (non-pollinating) plant.

　　Mizunoe-Uma year, Koshin (third month), sixth day.

According to the *Wamyō Ruijū-shō* (Japanese Name Thesaurus):
　　*Ahaki* (檍) — pronounced "oku (億)." The *Nihongi Shiki* records it as "Ahaki."
　　　　　　　　　On reflection, it is also recorded as an alternate name for the *kashi* (oak/holm oak),
　　　　　　　　　as found in the *Erya* commentary; it belongs to the catalpa (*azusa*) family.

---

According to the *Wakan Sansai Zue* (Illustrated Sino-Japanese Encyclopedia):
　Buddhist monks (priests) take its stem and use it as a poker
　to stir and tend the flame of an oil lamp.
　Because it can repel mosquitoes and moths,
　it is called *Gosei-sō* (護生草, "life-protecting herb").
　Its flavor is sweet.
　The ancient Chinese sage *Shi Kuang* (師曠) said:
　　"When the year is about to bring an abundant harvest,
　　the sweet herb (*amakusa*) sprouts first"
　　　　(*amakusa* here refers to *nazuna* / shepherd's purse)
　　"When the year is about to bring hardship,
　　the bitter herb (*nigakusa*) sprouts first"
　　　　(*nigakusa* here refers to *teireki* / pepperwort, *Lepidium* sp.)
　Note: This is one of the seven herbs used in the Seven-Herb Rice Porridge (*nanakusa-gayu*)
　eaten on the seventh day of the first month.
　Its fruit (seed pod) is triangular in shape, not yet large when formed,
　narrowing at the base, and resembles the plectrum (*bachi*) of a three-stringed instrument (*shamisen*).
　Children rub two of its seed pods together, which produces a sound,
　and for this reason it is also called *Sansen-sō* (三線草, "three-string herb").
