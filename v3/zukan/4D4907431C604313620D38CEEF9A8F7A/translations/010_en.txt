## Matsu / Pine (松 — *Pinus* spp.)

From the *Wakan Sansai Zue* (Illustrated Encyclopedia of Japan and China), section on aromatic woods:
　*Matsu* (pine; Tang Chinese pronunciation: *Son*) [fanqie spelling: Shōyō]
　*Shō* (枩, variant character); *Yōboku* (容木, same); Japanese name: *Matsu* (万豆)
　*Shōkō* (松黄, "pine yellow" — pine pollen); commonly called *Midori* (美止利, "green/vitality")
　*Zui* (蕤) [pronounced *zui*]; *Zui* (甤, same character) — the place where ■*zui* grows [i.e., the pollen-bearing stamen/catkin]

　The pine does not change color even after passing through frost and snow; comparing this to a person of steadfast heart, it is called "the tree of fidelity" (*tēiboku*).

From the *Wamyō Ruijushō*:
　*Matsu* (松): The *Hanshu* [Book of Han] mentions "planting green pines."
　The fanqie spelling "Shōyō" is also written as 榕 (*yong*), as seen in the *Tōin* [*Tang Yun*, a Tang dynasty phonological dictionary].
　Japanese name: *Matsu* (万豆).

From the *Nōgyō Zensho* (Complete Book of Agriculture):
　The pine is the chief of all the hundred trees; its character is written with the "tree" radical (*kibhen*) combined with the character for "lord" (*kimi*, 公).
　There are varieties with two, three, and five needles, and none of them change color through the four seasons.
　Both in Japan and China, the pine is admired and treasured.
　Beneath a pine of a thousand years grows *bukuryō* (茯苓, *Poria cocos*, a medicinal fungus);
　likewise, above it grows *nenashi-kazura* (兎糸子, *Cuscuta japonica*, Japanese dodder);
　and at its roots, amber (*kohaku*) can be found. All of these are fine medicines.
　Pine resin (*shōshi*), when refined, is applied to bowstrings; it also has countless other uses in plasters and medicines.

　The word *matsu* derives from *tamotsu* ("to preserve"), with the initial syllable dropped.
　*Moto* and *mato* are interchangeable. It is a tree that long preserves its vitality and longevity.
　The *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*), "Treatise on Divination by Tortoise and Stalks," states: "The pine and cypress are the chiefs of all the hundred trees, and they guard the gates of towns and villages."
　The *Shiji* also records: When the First Emperor of Qin climbed Mount Tai, a sudden violent storm arose, and he took shelter beneath a tree. He thereupon enfeoffed that tree with the title of "Five-Grade Grandee" (*Godaifu*). The historical account originally did not specify what kind of tree it was; it was Ying Shao who first stated it was a pine — and so the pine came to receive the rank of Grandee.

From the *Yamato Honzō*:
　Pine

From the commentary to the *Shokumotsuhonzō* (Food Materia Medica):
　Pine flower (*shōka*)
　Alternative name: *Shōkō* (松黄, "pine yellow")

## Sumire / Violet (菫 — *Viola* spp.)

[Seal] Flowering herbs

From the *Yamato Honzō*:
　*Shikahejitei* (紫花地丁, "purple-flowered earth nail") — Japanese name: *Komahikikusa* (コマヒキクサ)
　Also: *Kinka* / *Sumiregusa* (菫花)
　　　In Kyoto, called *Sumōtori* (スマウトリ, "sumo wrestler")
　　　In Tsukushi (Kyushu), called *Tono-no-uma* (トノノ馬, "lord's horse")

The author's note:
　The names for *sumire* (violet) are recorded in various ways across different texts. The *Jiuhuang Bencao* (救荒本草, *Bencao for Relieving Famine*) lists it as *Kinkinsai* (菫々菜) and *Zentōsō* (箭頭草, "arrowhead herb"). The *Wakan Sansai Zue* identifies this as *Yahazusō* (菫々菜, "arrowhead-leafed herb") [comparing the *Shūsetsu* with *kinka* (菫花)]. The same work also lists *Sumire* (菫) [also written 葵], *Kukink* (苦菫), and *Ku* (苦) as entries.
　The *Yamato Honzō* identifies *Shikahejitei* (紫花地丁, violet) as *Komahikikusa*, with the alternate name *Kinkinsai* (菫々菜).
　The *Wakan Sansai Zue*, however, treats *Shikahejitei*, *Kinkinsai*, and *Kinka* (菫) as three separate species.
　Nevertheless, since the name *Zentōsō* (箭頭草) appears under *Shikahejitei*, all three names refer to the same plant.
　*Kinsai* (菫菜) is a different plant altogether — it is *Nozeri* (野芹, field parsley/wild parsley, *Oenanthe javanica*).
　The various names for *sumire* as recorded across different texts are a source of considerable confusion.
