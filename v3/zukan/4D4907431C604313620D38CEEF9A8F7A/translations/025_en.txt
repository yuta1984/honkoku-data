## 【SUMIRE / VIOLET (Viola mandshurica)】

**Zǐhuā Dìdīng** (Purple Flower Ground Spike)
- Japanese name: Sumire (須美礼)
- Alternative names: Dokkyōko (独行虎) [from Bencao Gangmu / Honzō Kōmoku]
- Heihōtei (米布袋) [from Zōho Imyō]　Sentsu-sō / Arrow-head Grass (箭頭草) [from Bencao Gangmu]
- Yōkakushi / Ram's Horn Seed (羊角子) [from Hiin / Biyun]

**Jiùhuāng Běncǎo (Kyūkō Honzō)** states:
　Sumire (菫菜/violet)
　　Also known as:
　　　Arrow-head Grass (箭頭草)

**Violet flower (菫花)**: Alternative names are recorded in the section where the illustration was previously copied.
　　Two varieties exist.

**Máo Shī (Classic of Poetry)**:
　　Kin (菫) — Tsubosumire (round-leaved violet)

On the 13th day of the third month (Kōshin/姑洗), in the Kinoto-Uma year of Bunsei 7 (1824),
I sketched this from a specimen grown in my garden.

【Flowering Herb】

**Wakan Sansai Zue (Illustrated Encyclopedia of Japan and China)**, section on moisture-loving herbs, states:
　Kin (菫) [pronounced "kin/勤"]　Bitter Violet (苦菫)　Bitter Mallow (苦葵)
　Japanese name / common name: Sumire (須美礼) / Sumō-gusa (相撲草, Sumo Grass)

According to **Bencao Gangmu**, Su Gong states: "Jǐn cài (菫菜) is none other than the sumire (violet)." Liu Yuxi states, and the Shuōwén (etymological dictionary) says: "The jǐn (菫) has a root like shepherd's purse and leaves as fine as willow..." — this refers to the bitter violet (苦菫). The ancients' phrasing was inverted, just as when they called sweet grass (甘草) "greatly bitter" (大苦). [The author notes: "Those who say 'leaves like willow' — this was illustrated previously."]

Children play a game in which they pick two stems, hook them together, and the one whose calyx is cut off and falls loses. For this reason it came to be called **Sumō-gusa** (角力草, Sumo Grass / Wrestling Grass). Among the three plants — Geranium (牻牛児草/Gennoshōko), Violet (菫), and Sword Grass (刀草) — the violet is the most renowned.

## 【HAKOBE / CHICKWEED (Stellaria media)】

**Shokkyō (Food Competition text)** states:
　Arrow-head Grass (箭頭草)

**Wamyō Ruijushō (Japanese Name Thesaurus)** states:
　Fanlu (蘩蔞) [read as Hakubera] — The Bencao states: Fanlu [two pronunciations: Fanlu]
　　Japanese name: Hakubera (八久倍良)
　Táo Yǐnjū (Tao Hongjing) states: This is identical to Chicken-gut Grass (鶏腸草).

　On the first day of the third month (Yayoi), Mizunoto-Hitsuji year (1823)　【Medicinal Herb】【Vegetable Category】
　Sketched casually with both brushes

**Zōho Tashikiden (Expanded Botanical Encyclopedia)**, section on soft and moist plants, states:
　Fanlu (繁■) [Hanro]
　Japanese name: Hakubera (波久倍良); also called Hakobe (波古倍)
　Supplementary name: Tsuruhakobe (豆留波古売, Trailing Chickweed)
　Alternative names: Shigusa (滋草) [from Zōho Imyō]; Sōro (■縷) [from Erya]; 
　Gō (蔜) [pronounced "kyō/教"]; Ryōro (寮縷) [from Guoya]; Gachōsai (鵞腸菜, Goose-gut Vegetable)

It is listed among the seven herbs of Jinjitsu (人日, the seventh day of the first month / Spring's Seven Herbs) in: the Engishiki, Prince Ribu's Records, Chōmei's Shiki Monogatari (Tale of the Four Seasons), and various other texts.

## 【KAKIDŌSHI / GROUND IVY (Glechoma hederacea var. grandis)】

**Yamato Honzō (Japanese Materia Medica)**:
　Sekkyetsusō (積雪草, Snow-accumulation Grass) — the Kakidōshi
　In the Bencao: Kohakka (胡薄荷, Foreign Mint); also called Tsuruhatsuka

**Kyūkō Yafu (Wild Plant Famine Guide)**:
　Jiqian'er (地銭児, Ground Coin Plant) — Kakidōshi　【Climbing/Vining Herb】
　Also known as: Sekkyetsusō (積雪草)

**Zōho Tashikiden**, section on fragrant herbs, records:
　Sekkyetsusō (積雪草/Shakketsusō)
　Japanese name: Tsubokusa (豆保久佐). Upon examination, also called Tsuruhaka (豆留波加).
　Alternative names: Kaiso (海蘓) [from Zōho Imyō]; Kohakka (胡薄荷) [from Taihōhō/Daiboufang];
　Jiqian-cao / Ground Coin Grass (地銭草) [from Tangben]; Lianqian-cao / Linked Coin Grass (連銭草) [from Yakuzu/Medicine Illustrations]

**Wakan Sansai Zue** states: The Sekkyetsusō is commonly called "Kakidōshi" (籬通し, Fence-passer). The illustration in Bencao Xinxu (本草心続) shows a branching specimen, but this is incorrect. The Wamyō Shō identifies it as Tsubokusa (豆保久佐), but this too is incorrect — Tsubokusa is listed separately. In the Bencao Gangmu, this has yet to be fully investigated; regarding the flower, further examination is warranted.

　In the Bencao commentary, Táo Yǐnjū states: "The Sekkyetsusō, Japanese name Tsubokusa (豆保久佐), is so named because of its cold nature." Su Jingjing states: "Its leaves resemble coins (錢), therefore it is also called Renkensō (連銭草, Linked-Coin Grass)."
