　Fukujusō — also called: Fukuzuku-gusa
　　　　　　　　and: Ganjitsu-sō (New Year's Day plant)

## 【UME (Japanese Plum / Prunus mume)】

Recorded in the "Five Fragrant Fruits" section of Wakan Sansai Zue:
　Ume (梅; Tang Chinese pronunciation: Mui) — phonetic reading: "Shi"; ancient character form: 槑
　　Japanese name: U-me (宇女)
　　Now also written/read as "Mu-me" (牟女)


According to Sansai Zue:
　There are four things to be prized in plum (trees): rarity is prized over abundance; old age is prized over youth; leanness is prized over fatness; the bud is prized over the fully-opened flower.

○ According to Kakurin Gyokuro: In ancient times, people spoke only of the plum's fruit and its growth; no one spoke of the beauty or fragrance of its flowers. It was not until the Song dynasty that plum blossoms began to be celebrated in poetry and prose.

○ In our country (Japan), in ancient times, when people said "hana" (flower), they meant plum. Since the middle ages, when people say "hana," they refer exclusively to cherry blossoms.

○ According to Shoku Nihongi: In the seventh month of the tenth year of Emperor Shōmu's Tenpyō era (738 CE), the emperor pointed to the plum tree in front of the palace hall and said: "Since last spring I have wished to admire this tree, and yet I have not had the occasion to do so." He then commanded thirty men of letters each to compose a poem on the plum tree, and all composed verses on the theme of spring. Among the poems:

　　Haru no yo no　yami wa ayanashi　ume no hana
　　　　iro koso miene　ka ya wa kaeruru

　*(The darkness of a spring night is meaningless — though the color of the plum blossom cannot be seen, can its fragrance be hidden?)*

〔Category: Flowering tree〕

Wani of Baekje praised the plum, calling it "this flower." Lord Sugawara no Michizane loved plum trees throughout his life, leaving behind the legendary name "Tobiume" (Flying Plum). From the words of Jin dynasty's Jūkyochū came the epithet "Kōbunboku" (Tree that loves literature). From a poem by Tachibana no Naokimi came the name "Ōjaku-bai" (鶯宿梅, Plum where the warbler sleeps). From a poem by Saigyō came the name "Tomekokaishi no ume" (the plum one longs to have come to one). Such examples cannot be fully enumerated.


According to Yamato Honzō, in the section on plum records (Baifutsuki):
　Those growing in the wild, not yet having undergone grafting (saisetsu),
　are called "Kōbai" (江梅, River plum). They are also
　named "Chokkyakubai" (直脚梅, Straight-legged plum).
　Or, these are sometimes simply called "No(bai)" (野, Wild plum).
