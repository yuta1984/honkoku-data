## 【*Shikimi* (樒; Japanese Star Anise, *Illicium anisatum*)】

### According to the *Zoho Tashiki-hen* (増補多識編), section on Poisonous Plants (*Dokusō-rui*):

　　*Mōsō* (莽草)　[Japanese name:] *Shikimi* (志幾美). Note also that in Suruga Province, it is called *Nezumikōroshi* (祢須美古呂志; "Mouse-Killer"). Its alternate name is *Nabewari* (那倍和利; "Pot-Breaker").　[Alternate names:] *Somō* (鼠莽; "Rat-Weed"). [Additional alternate names in the supplemented edition:] *Mōsō* (䒽草) [sound: *oka*], *Bōsō* (芒草) [cited from the *Shanhaijing* (山海経)].

In the *Sansai Zue* (三才図会), two varieties are listed under *Mōsō* (莽草) and *Mokumitsu* (木密) respectively. Regarding *Mōsō*, it is said to have no flowers or fruit — this requires further investigation.
Furthermore, the name *Nabewari* (那倍和利) is of uncertain identification. "*Kōfun*" (鉤吻; *Gelsemium elegans*), which bears the reading *Nahewari*, is a different plant altogether — see later volumes for clarification.

### According to the *Yamato Honzō* (大和本草):

　　The flower of *Mōsō* (莽草の花; *Shikimi* blossom)

From the same work, in the section *Yakumei Bengi* (薬名弁疑; "Critique of Medicinal Names"):
　　*Somō* (鼠莽; "Rat-Weed")

　　The character 樒 has been transmitted in popular Japanese usage as "*Shikimi*" from generation to generation.
　　In the *Honzō Wamyō* (本草和名), it is likewise read as *Shikimi*.
　　In the *Imyō Bunruishō* (異名分類抄), *Mōsō* (莽草) is given the Japanese name *Hanaki*.
　　In the *Wamyō Ruijushō* (和名類聚抄): *Shikimi*.
　　In the *Shinsen Jikyō* (新撰字鏡): *Mohone* (モホネ).
　　In the *Wakan Sansai Zue* (和漢三才図会):

　　　　*Mokumitsu* (木蜜; Japanese reading: *Shikimi*; Tang Chinese reading: *Momitsu*).
　　　　[Alternate names:] *Mitsukō* (蜜香), *Mokkō* (没香), *Takōboku* (多香木), *A-■* [character unclear].
　　　　The character 櫁: [Reading: The *Tōin* (唐韻) says "it is a fragrant wood"; Japanese name: *Shikimi* (シキミ)].
　　　　Note also that the *Kenpōnashi* (枳椇; Japanese Raisin Tree, *Hovenia dulcis*) is likewise called "*Mokumitsu*" —
　　　　　but this is a different plant from *Shikimi*.

　　　　　　In common Japanese practice, this plant is offered as a Buddhist flower.
　　　　　　Alternatively, it is burned as powdered incense (*makkō*) before the Buddha.
　　　　　　Both the bark and the leaves are used.

　　　　　　Sketched from life on a day around the end of *Kyōshō* (夾鐘; second lunar month),
　　　　　　year *Kinoe-Saru* (甲申; Bunsei 7, 1824).

### According to the *Wamyō Ruijushō* (和名類聚抄):

　*Shikimi* (樒): The *Tōin* (唐韻) and *Kangoshō* (漢語抄) both state: "*Shikimi* (之岐美) — it is a fragrant wood."

The same work further states:
　*Mōsō* (莽草; *Shikimi*): The commentary to the *Shanhaijing* (山海経注) cites *Mōsō*;
　　　the *Honzō Wamyō* (本草和名) gives the reading *Shikimi* (之木美).
　　　It is a plant that can be used to poison and catch fish.

　*Shikimi* is found in abundance in Musashi, Izu, Awaji, Tanba, and Harima Provinces.
　Branches are cut and offered to the Buddha; the branches and leaves are referred to collectively as "flowers."
　The bark and leaves are harvested, dried, and burned as aromatic incense, which is called *makkō* (抹香; powdered incense).

　　A poem composed in Oki Province:

　　*Sanagara ya*　*hotoke no hana ni*　*ori semaji*
　　　　　　　　*Shikimi ga e ni*　*tsumoru shirayuki*　— Emperor Go-Toba

　　(Would that the white snow piling upon the *shikimi* branch
　　　　not be broken off to serve as a flower before the Buddha.)
