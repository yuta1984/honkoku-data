【Preface】

"The field mouse transforms into a grain-snatching bird; the well-frog divides into a ladle."

It was around the fine season of Yayoi (the Third Month), when everyone spread their cotton-padded sleeves along the shore, eager to pluck up nori seaweed, pick up shells, and gather what they could. They hauled their boats up onto the tidal flats and sat in a row offshore. And who were these people assembled there? Beginning with Kurabe Yukizumi, they placed a great sake cup in the center. While strolling along the rocky shore in search of delicacies, they obtained razor clams (matégai) gotten from a diving woman (ama). Whether drawn out by the previous night's drinking, Matsuhidenari's face was faintly flushed; he fanned himself with a round fan, saying he could not hold his liquor — whereupon the others cried, "No, no — more, more!" and retrieved a sake cup that had been lying in the shadow of a bamboo joint, urging him to drink. Even this gentle, refined man eventually succumbed to the sake, his drawn eyebrow-ink of wisteria vine becoming disheveled.

With the vigor of a brave whale, our lord's belly seemed to suck up water like the Peach River current; he was proclaimed "Momo no Tsū" (Master of Peaches), cheered on with cries of "Here, here!" The poet Hakuju, who composed under the name of bamboo-joint, together with the seven hosts and all the sake-loving company, numbered thirty-six persons in all. These were by no means ordinary companions, but men of refined elegance, having shed their outer husks. When the third round of sake had been reached, this gathering suddenly transformed into the great Peng bird (大鵬), soaring up into the hanging clouds, its wings becoming fans. Responding to the beckoning of those wing-fans, even as far as the imperial city's castle town, journeying on foot as though crossing the thousand-fathom depths, they gathered a thousand varieties of small shells. The pleasures encountered along the way were utterly indescribable — though one might tell a thousand years' worth of tales to people back in one's hometown, they could never be conveyed in ten thousand words. The belly full of triumphant sake never emptied, and to put it in words: "One should not speak of shells to a well-frog." As for our heated sake pot at Futami-ga-ura — likening it to the shells of the poetic immortals, each person has added one kyōka verse. Just as the place changes and one gathers green seaweed (aosa) from the shores of Shinagawa and Miyake-ura, so too have we assembled these poems.
