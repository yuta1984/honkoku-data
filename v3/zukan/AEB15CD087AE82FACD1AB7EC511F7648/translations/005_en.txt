　　If the eggs are obtained, the mother will fly to the spot. When boiled, [the insect] is edible and is said to be quite pungent and delicious.

　　*Kōmoku Yakusetsu* states: There is no Japanese variety. The alternate name "qīngfú" is given to coins (currency), but this is merely a borrowing of the name.

　　There is a certain species of moth that appears in great numbers around the autumnal equinox. It does not fly by day, nor does it come to lamplight at night. It attaches itself to the leaves of plants and trees, neither drinking nor eating, remaining motionless for several days. When one catches the insect and examines it closely, beneath its abdomen there is something resembling stitched cotton, roughly equal in size to the insect's own body. If one brushes away this cotton-like covering, eggs are revealed—several hundred clustered together, resembling shrimp eggs. Some are clay-yellow in color; others are white. In form, the insect closely resembles a cicada. Once it has laid its eggs, the mother disappears from sight. The young (larvae) are yellowish-red and covered in hair; at first, several hundred cluster together, spinning thread and weaving the leaves together. As autumn deepens and the leaves turn pale (wither), the insects grow to about three or four *bu* in length and bear black hairs. When the season of frost descent (*sōkō*, approximately late October) arrives, they burrow beneath tree roots, into the soil, or among debris and fallen matter. Around the season of spring rains, they again attach to grass and leaves and begin to feed. By the end of the third month, they become hairy caterpillars over two *sun* long, black-haired and mottled. The depth of this moth's love for its offspring resembles that of the qīngfú,
