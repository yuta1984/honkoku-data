**From fish merchants: On Anago (conger eel)**
Conger eels from Kanagawa do not shrink when broiled. Those from the opposite shore (other producing regions) lose flesh when grilled. "Anago-munagi" — the Edo variety is the best.

From a natural history record: "Shiro-unagi (white eel)" — specimens from Kumano are white in color.

**From Geishū (Aki Province, modern Hiroshima Prefecture):**
Conger eels dwell in mud. They are a fish caught by angling. After Setsubun (early February), they taste good, and by summer the flavor is particularly fine; they are also at their most plentiful during this season.

**[Middle section]**

**Geishū: Katanakiri**
It dwells among rocks. Those with hard fin-bones are scooped up with small nets. When broiled and eaten, the flavor is extremely fine. In the third month it bears eggs. When spawning, they gather in the sand of the beach (hanata) and deposit their eggs there. It is also known as "Kamisori-uo" (razor fish).

Someone called "Ise no Tadashi" says that "Umi-dojō" (sea loach) comes in two varieties. One type is round and short in shape, has many spines on its back, and will prick people.

　　Kinpo (Ginpo): Those with black spots are most numerous from September onward.
　　In Shinshū (Shinano Province), it is called "Kamisori." Its shape resembles a loach but is flattened, with two spines on the dorsal fin, and is said to cut people's hands.

**From *Haicuoshu* (Treatise on Sea Creatures):**
The qiu (loach-like creature) resembles the shàn (swamp eel) but is shorter; its head is pointed and sharp. It is yellow in color, scaleless, covers itself with mucus (making it) difficult to grasp.

[Sticky note] Alternate name: Ginpo (Ginbo)

**[Lower section]**

**Ginpo (Stringer/Gunnel)**
In the lower reaches of Horie (a canal/waterway area), it enters dark recesses and grows fat and large. It is slightly firmer than conger eel but easy to fillet. The bones are hard, it has fine speckles, and the area around the dorsal spines tends to be darker in color. There are also specimens that are slightly less reddish.

　　Ginpo lurks inside bundles of brushwood (soda) and beneath sea lettuce (aosa). It has teeth and bites people. Its fins can also injure people; when pierced with a hook, it becomes entangled in the fishing line.

Those that enter freshwater grow longer. Those dwelling in seaweed in spring are called "Hama-dojō" (beach loach).

**From *Butsuppin Shikimei Keimon* (Illustrated Encyclopedia of Natural Products):**
Those produced in Kumano, Kishū (Kii Province), are entirely white in color, and in the local dialect are called "Shirahasu" (white eel). They are also known as "Shiro-unagi" and by the name "Reisuke."

**From *Sansai Zue* (Illustrated Compendium of the Three Realms):**
The anago (阿奈古) resembles the hamo (sea pike conger) in shape, but its color is less lustrous than the hamo. From the nape to the tail it has small white dots like stars, running in rows on both sides, numbering over a hundred on each side. Its flavor is [*nijūhei*? — meaning unclear]. It has little fat and is not particularly tasty. Fishermen broil it and fraudulently sell it as eel (unagi/manrei).

**[Lower section]**

**From *Sansai Zue*:**
The shàn (taunagi, swamp eel) is commonly called "Kitako" (木太古) and in the western provinces is called "Aburago" (阿布良古).

Upon investigation: The swamp eel is found in abundance in the muddy banks of waterways in the western provinces. It resembles the eel in shape but is somewhat flattened; large specimens reach two to three shaku (approx. 60–90 cm) in length. The mouth is pointed, the teeth fine, and the eyes small — it also resembles a snake, but has no fins. It is deep yellow with small black markings and is very mucousy. It is skinned from the tail; the flesh is white, but contains hair-like spines (bones), so it is eaten boiled/cooked.

*Sansai Zue* further states: "The swamp eel is fond of sleeping. Sellers today invariably place two loaches inside the container with them, using one to keep the swamp eel moving and stirring; if this is not done, the eel will often sleep and die."

**Kidako (swamp eel, large variety)**
Large specimens can reach three to four shaku (approx. 90–120 cm) in length.
