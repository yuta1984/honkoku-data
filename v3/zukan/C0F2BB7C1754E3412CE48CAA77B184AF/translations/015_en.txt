**Lower Section**
*Ukiki* (Ocean Sunfish): In Mito (present-day Ibaraki Prefecture), it is called "Namakori." The taste of *ukiki* resembles that of *kushikahi* (a type of shellfish) with a slight flavor of whale meat.

When catching *ukiki* in Mito, the creature's shape resembles a winnowing basket (*mi*). It is killed by being pierced with a harpoon (*mori*), then the belly is cut open, the large intestine alone is removed, and the flesh is discarded. The intestine is split down the middle and flattened into a strip like an obi (sash), then brought back to market. Wholesalers then cut it into lengths of about one *shaku* (approximately 30 cm) for sale.

As food, it is blanched and eaten as sashimi, or marinated in miso and soy sauce. Inside the belly there is a part called *miwata* (internal organ), which is white in color and edible. It is said to be remarkably effective when used medicinally for postpartum blood disorders and dysentery. The creature can range in size from three to four *ken* up to six to seven *ken* (approximately 5 to 13 meters).

In Geshū (present-day Hiroshima Prefecture), it is called "*okiuo*" or "*ookiuo*." Because it is a weak creature by nature, it sometimes beaches itself on waves. Fishermen do not bother to catch it deliberately. Since its flesh contains no fat, it invariably melts away when boiled.

The item called "*yukiuo*" is the offspring (juvenile) of the *ukiki*. Its flesh is pure white in color; when boiled, the flesh dissolves into water. The bones are soft, and both bones and flesh are eaten together.

**Left Page**

**Upper Section**
According to the *Sansai Zue* (Illustrated Compendium of the Three Powers), the fish called *sagyō* (楂魚) is commonly known as "*ukiki*," and is also called "*manbō*" (満方魚, Manta/Mola fish).

Upon investigation, the *sagyō* inhabits the offshore waters of Ōshū (present-day Tōhoku region) and Jōshū (Hitachi Province, present-day Ibaraki Prefecture). Its shape resembles that of a ray (*ei*) and is square, hence the name "Manpō-gyo" (Square Fish). Large specimens measure one to two *jō* (approximately 3–6 meters) on each side and one to two *shaku* (30–60 cm) in thickness. The edges around its perimeter are somewhat thinner. It is grayish-white in color, with white flesh and hard bones. Its taste is not particularly good. It is sluggish and dull-witted (*rodon*), floating and swimming as if unaware of danger. When a person holds it back with a long-handled *sarae* (a rake-like tool), it simply stays put — hence the comparison to a floating log (*sha*/raft) and the resulting name. The back is split open to extract the white intestines, which measure over one *jō* in length (also called "hyakuhiro," meaning "a hundred fathoms"). These are made into *hishio* (salted fermented paste), or alternatively preserved in sake lees (*kasozuke*), or dried and eaten.

**Lower Section**
According to the *Ningbo Fuzhi* (Gazetteer of Ningbo Prefecture): The *kōton* (江豚, finless porpoise) resembles a pig and is also called "*daihaku*" (大白, Great White). Its body contains much oil; when used as lamp oil, it is suitable for illuminating spinning and weaving work, but it is said to burn brighter when illuminating gambling. It has long been said in tradition that it is the transformation of a lazy woman.

According to the *Huangyan Xianzhi* (Gazetteer of Huangyan County): The "*hakutai*" (白袋, White Bag), also known as "*hakukyō*" (白距), has a shape resembling a cow. When it enters rivers from the sea, it is an omen of flooding.
