**From "Sansai Zue" (Illustrated Compendium of the Three Realms):**
The Japanese name is "Hamu," commonly called "Hamo" (possibly an abbreviation of the Chinese pronunciation).

Upon investigation, the sea eel (hamo, *Muraenesox cinereus*) is abundant in the southwestern seas (the Chinese and East China seas) but is entirely absent in the northeastern waters. Its shape resembles that of the eel (*unagi*) but is larger; it has a dorsal fin running to the tail, is blue-black in color but lighter than the eel, has no scales, a white belly, and teeth of alternating lengths arranged in rows, with dozens of small teeth also aligned along the central ridge. It generally measures one and a half to two shaku (approx. 45–60 cm) in length. The flesh is white and not brittle. It is split open, basted with soy sauce with the skin on, and broiled. It has less fat than eel, but the flavor is excellent.

**Wind-dried Sea Eel (Kon-kiri)** 〈commonly called "Gomugiri"〉
Ten heads of sea eel are strung together and dried white, forming a shape like a flat board. This is enjoyed in summer and autumn. When finely chopped and mixed with sake and soy sauce, it makes an excellent substitute for namasu (vinegared dish). The annotation on eel in *Bencao Gangmu* (Compendium of Materia Medica) states that the dried form is called "Fengman (wind eel)," which likely refers to this preparation.

**Tōhei (large sea eel)**
A large variety of sea eel, measuring four to five shaku (approx. 120–150 cm), found in Sanshū (Sanuki Province, modern Kagawa Prefecture). Its flavor is inferior to ordinary specimens.

Generally, the liver and intestines of the sea eel can also be boiled and eaten, or made into a salted paste (*hishio*), which is excellent. The gallbladder is too bitter to eat. A white sac-like organ (the swim bladder), when dried, can be used to polish the string of a *karaumi* (cotton-batting bow; a bow used for fluffing cotton).

**Ise (Province, product):**

Among hamo, there is a variety called "Gishibari." Its eyes are located at the tip of its snout. This is likely the "Kuro-hamo" (black pike conger). It has a fierce nature, and is said to come close to shore alive during the summer months.

**Sagi-anago (heron conger):** In Bōshū (Awa Province, modern Chiba Prefecture), it is called "Hōzu" and used as a remedy for *shaku* (abdominal spasms/colic).

"Hamu" dwelling at a depth of fifty *hiro* (fathoms) in the sea off Iki Island — 〈this is an anago (conger eel)〉

**Hamo from Sendai:** No small bones. Large specimens measure around three shaku, four or five sun (approx. 100–105 cm). Has fine teeth.

Hamo coming from Kyoto and Hyogo are the most prized. Very large specimens are not highly regarded. Those with small heads and gills, with a dark navy back, white belly, and fine flavor are preferred. Hamo produced in Edo are fat and short, with large heads, and are a pale blue-gray color.

**Hamo bone-cutting technique (honegiri):**
Open the hamo and place it face-up on the cutting board, then place one board beneath (as a guide), using it as a reference so that the kitchen knife does not cut all the way through the skin. This is a secret technique passed on when learning the art of bone-cutting. (The hamo) dwells among seaweed.
