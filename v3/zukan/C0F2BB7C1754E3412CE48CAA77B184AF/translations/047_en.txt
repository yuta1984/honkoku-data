**Right folio, lower section:**
*Gatabō*: The body is shorter than an eel (*unagi*), approximately one shaku (≈30 cm) in length. The back is pale black. There are fins above and below the body. It resembles an eel and has a rough texture. People eat it. It lives among rocks and oysters. In Tosa Province (present-day Kōchi), it is called *Utsubo* (moray eel). It resembles *Haki*, and is brown in color.
　*Umi-hebi* (Sea Snake): Thinner and longer than an eel. Brown in color. The snout is pointed, and the eyes are at the sides of the head. People eat it.

From the *Kanzan-ō Gaikoku-ki* (Kanzan's Record of Foreign Lands):
On Hachijō Island, there are three
things of remarkable size: one is the loach (*dojō*), one is the muskmelon (*makuwa-uri*),
and one is the turnip (*kabu*). A loach of two shaku (≈60 cm) in length
and over seven sun (≈21 cm) in girth
was observed. It was cut into rounds,
simmered in miso, and tasted —
it was supremely delicious.

**Left folio, upper section:**
(Classical Chinese entry:) *Namada* — found in abundance in the sea off Bōshū Province (present-day Chiba). Length over three shaku (≈90 cm),
circumference approximately eight sun (≈24 cm). No scales. Roughly resembles a catfish (*namazuo*). The eyes are very small. The upper jaw is
pointed, with barbels; the mouth has fine teeth, and the upper palate has fangs. Between the throat there is
something like a tongue. The back has a fleshy dorsal fin; the ventral fins are greenish-yellow-white. No gills;
in their place there may be a blowhole connecting to the throat. The fishermen say: the flesh is tender,
sticky and slippery, and inedible. Sometimes it is made into dried fish and eaten.

*Namada*: a dialect term from Katsuta in Bōshū Province. In Izu Province it is called *Torazaya*.
There are two varieties. It is like an eel but larger. The flesh is yellow with tiger-stripe markings. Teeth are sharp. It is nearsighted and cannot see far. The tail is wide.
The tail has many fine bones. It is edible as far as the *emochi* (stomach) region. The skin resembles the skirt-flesh of a soft-shelled turtle (*suppon*). It lives among the rocks. It is fished using a five-sun (≈15 cm) hook attached to the end of a bamboo pole, baited with rotten sardines (*iwashi*).
One variety is darkly colored and not attractive.

*Umi-unagi* (Sea Eel) from Niigata in Echigo Province: The body is flat, yellow with black spots, with teeth. Small ones are eaten by people. Very slimy.
The *Sansai-zukai* (Encyclopaedia of the Three Powers) says: *Kidako*, also known as *Aburago*.
The *Yamato Honzō* (Japanese Materia Medica) says: *Kidako*.

**Left folio, middle section:**
*Watamuki* ("Gut-peeler"): dialect of Odawara region.
Also called *Umi-mamushi* (Sea Viper). The shape resembles a snake,
black in color. The flesh is white and delicious. When prepared as *kabayaki* (grilled),
there are no bones. There are three sturdy joints (*fushi*) in the body.
Even if these joints are removed, and the head and tail
are cut off, the entrails removed, and the skin peeled,
it will still thrash about. The joints are like *tegus* (silkworm gut/monofilament).
No scales. It preys on *mebaru* (rockfish) and generally small fish.
The thickness is about that of a thumb. Length: one shaku five or six sun (≈45–50 cm).

**Left folio, lower section:**
*Umi-unagi* (Sea Eel): In that area it is called *Namada*.
Some reach the thickness of a tea bowl and a length of one or two jō (≈3–6 meters).
Those living along the rocky shore do not exceed three shaku (≈90 cm). Looking closely at the skin's coloration, there is
a woven-pattern (*aya*) design within it, making it quite beautiful. Because it is lean
(lacking fat), people do not particularly go out of their way to catch it; however, when one is
occasionally caught on a fishing line, fishermen boil and eat it, only to find it has small bones
and is of inferior quality. Nevertheless, it is said that in Edo,
when making *kamaboko* (fish cake), a considerable amount of *namada* flesh is
mixed in to make it. Those that lurk
among the rocks along the shore are particularly feared by people.
At night, women and others who regularly go out to the shore
to gather fish may accidentally
step on a *namada*, and it will bite their toes.
If in surprise they try to pull away,
it will bite the toe clean off.
Therefore, if one simply stays still
and remains calm, when the *namada* opens its mouth,
using that as a signal to
withdraw one's foot — one will escape unharmed.
