When officials accompanied us to more distant places, they would ride in a box-like conveyance pulled by two horses, with one person who managed the horses riding on one of them. When we became hungry, we would stop at a large house nearby and request a meal through the officials' directions. The houses were universally roofed with tiles, the pillars were entirely of stone, with benches placed on the earthen floors, and they were built with two or even three stories, fitted with glass-paned screens — their splendor was beyond description. The main streets were seven to eight ken wide, while the side streets were four to five ken wide, and the rows of houses were neat and clean. Wherever there were bridges in town, they were all made of stone, built up in a sturdy arched fashion. The land was fertile and abundant in produce, including medicinal goods, coral beads,

woolen cloth, felt goods, and other items such as white taffeta, rice, soy sauce, sugar, and distilled spirits made from millet. Fish were plentiful, and they appeared to produce all manner of curious and intricate crafted goods. During our stay, eight guards were posted during the day and sixteen at night, changing shifts each night, and for a period of one hundred and twenty days, without fail, they kept watch, and when the night had grown quiet they would come around to check on us one by one as we slept — the same every night. Perhaps owing to having been exposed to rain and wind for several days while adrift, and having drunk poor water, everyone had developed edema (swelling) and remained indisposed. Saijirō's swelling was not so severe, but he was in distress from being unable to eat. The Luzon authorities therefore sent a doctor who came to examine him twice daily, checking his pulse and providing various things, though we could not understand anything at all (of what was said).
