There were more than a few who were exposed to public view for three days at a time. Even to the eyes of one who was no better than a chained monkey himself, the sight was truly pitiable.

During their stay here, one of the four fell ill, and as his condition appeared to be serious, four or five physicians attended him, administered decoctions without cease, and some thirty nurses were assigned to watch over him day and night. He received treatment of the most devoted care, yet medicine and stone needles proved of no avail, and he finally passed away. He was buried in that land with the utmost ceremony. To describe the funeral arrangements: both the inner and outer coffins were exceedingly beautiful. After the body had been placed in the coffin, it was filled to the brim with vermilion. For the funeral procession, in addition to a great number of officials of all ranks, several thousand commoners of the city participated. As guides for the deceased, some hundred and fifty monks together with one great high priest stood in solemn ranks, their chanting voices unceasing. The three remaining men rode in their customary palanquins for the procession. In the grounds of a great temple that appeared to be the memorial site of the king himself, the burial rites were conducted with the greatest care, and at the gate, a large board was erected with "Japanese Person" written in large characters in vermilion ink,

serving as a marker. Thereafter, for the period of one week, men and women, young and old, gathered in crowds, and the number of those who came to pay their respects was immense. It seemed to them that even the state funeral held for a great minister of that country upon his death could not surpass this, and the three who remained behind felt a considerable sense of honor.

When the forty-ninth day after the death of the departed had passed, the three set out from that place, staying two days here and three days there as they went, and spent over a hundred days traversing the length of the country before finally leaving its borders. From there they traveled about twenty ri by horse until they reached a steep and rugged mountain path, and again boarded their plank palanquins and proceeded for over fifty days, at the end of which they at last emerged onto a plain. After passing through a number of castle towns, they came to a great river called the Niruwei River. The river was about five and a half ri in width, with a dry riverbed on the southern bank some five or six ri wide. On the day they crossed this river, they set out before dawn, and it was past the fifth hour of the night of the following day (8 o'clock in the evening by modern reckoning) before they finally arrived at a post station on the far bank.
