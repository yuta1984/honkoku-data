Around the third quarter of the Hour of the Ox that night, Magoshichi saw in a dream a nobly born lady, appearing just as he had seen the portrait of Princess Shikishi Naishinnō in the Hyakunin Isshu,¹ dressed in a magnificent twelve-layered jūni-hitoe court robe,² who appeared in the space at the bow of the ship and declared, "I shall be waiting at Kobuchi," then flew away pointing toward the shore. An extraordinary, ethereal fragrance spread in all directions, and he awoke from the dream. Magoshichi was filled with a sense of wonder, thinking it must be some kind of auspicious omen, and considered telling the others about it. However, being only nineteen years old and the most junior among the crew, he felt embarrassed at the thought of speaking up only to be ridiculed, and so he said nothing of it at that time. Now, sailing from Hakodate back toward Edo, a distance of roughly three hundred ri by sea, and with the weather holding favorable, they set sail from Kobuchi harbor at six o'clock in the morning (the sixth hour of the morning by the old reckoning) on the 15th of October.³ After running some ten-odd ri, the wind began to pick up a little, and so they turned the helm toward a bay called Hahakigi to take shelter there. This place was about one ri by land from Kobuchi harbor; to enter the bay at Kobuchi, one would pass by Kinkasan⁴ close by to the east. Kinkasan is an island of about five ri in circumference, rising up at a distance of some three ri

from the shore. Seen from the ship, it could be mistaken for a carpet of gold and silver sand, and the shrine at its foot presented a magnificent structure of consummate beauty. Thus, on the night of the 19th of October, watching the weather conditions, they found the wind had somewhat abated, and set sail from Hahakigi bay at the seventh hour of the morning (four o'clock in the morning by the present reckoning). They ran for more than fifty ri to a place called the Cape of Shioya, on the border between the provinces of Ōshū and Hitachi.⁵ At that point the wind grew fierce again, the sky took on a terrifying color, and the surface of the sea was in an ominous state, so the crew all consulted together, saying they should moor the ship somewhere suitable and wait out the weather. The helmsman Jūemon was a young man of only eighteen that year, and was in no position to offer his opinion, but the head man Nihei said: "There is no doubt that this wind will later shift to an easterly and gradually calm down. We should simply drop anchor here for a while and wait." The navigator Shinjichi, apparently finding this hard to accept, called out to a neighboring vessel among the more than forty coastal trading ships that had gathered there to wait for better weather, asking how they thought the wind would turn. The reply came back: "It is difficult to say either way—if the wind should worsen through the night..."
