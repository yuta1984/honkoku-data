Day after day the wind remained fierce, and as they drifted across the vast expanse of ocean, there was no trace of anything that might be an island, no cloud that could be mistaken for land; their only tenuous guide was the position of the moon and sun by which they could tell east from west—and so they had no way of knowing whatsoever on which sea they were adrift. They managed somehow to fashion a replacement for the rudder that had been snapped by the waves, and used it for six or seven days, but then the rough seas grew fierce again and that rudder too was broken. Left with no other option, they beat the spears and halberds they happened to have on board into nails and made yet another replacement rudder, which they used for more than ten days. Even after sailing so many days, not a single other vessel could be seen crossing their path—what kind of ocean in the world could this be? With the waves as their pillow, unable to find even peaceful sleep, passing each day into the next, the state of their hearts can well be imagined, and it is deeply pitiable. Moreover, as their water supply had dwindled, they waited for rainy days, but the season being that of the cold drizzling rains, it never fell in any satisfying quantity, and so with water buckets, carrying pots, mortars, and every vessel they could find, they caught what few drops of rain they could

—barely enough to wet their throats. November came, yet the west wind abated not at all. They found themselves caught in a stretch where the currents were particularly unusual, and the newly fashioned rudder was once again broken and rendered useless. With all means now exhausted, they lashed a board to the anchor and, pulling on the rope suspended from the rudder housing, managed to keep moving. By this time it was already the fourteenth day of Shimotsuki (the eleventh month). Several days of rain had preceded this, and when it finally cleared, the fierce west wind too gradually calmed to a gentle breeze. The entire crew gathered together at the bow, looked at one another, and said: "Twenty-five days have now passed. Since the wind has blown continuously in the same direction ever since we left Shioya no Hana, reckoning from our day-and-night running before the wind, we calculate that we have been driven a distance of roughly two thousand eight hundred *ri* or more. At that rate, we should surely have washed ashore somewhere by now—yet we have encountered neither passing ships nor islands at all. This must be truly the limit of our miserable fortune."
