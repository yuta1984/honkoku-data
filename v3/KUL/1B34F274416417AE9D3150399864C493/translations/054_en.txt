...they headed toward the northern road of Ōshū. Thus, by the 20th of July, they had finished loading the timber and set sail from a place called Kotomari in Tatebi. With a gentle favorable wind filling the sails, they ran along smoothly, but when they had rowed out some fourteen or fifteen ri offshore, for some reason, Genzō the cook was struck by the sail and fell from the lookout platform into the swirling waves below. "This is dreadful!" the crew cried out in alarm, and immediately they lowered the sails, dropped anchor, threw out straw mats and poles, crying "Swim! Swim!"—but there was no sign of him surfacing. Alarmed, everyone launched the small boat and searched here and there, but found not even the slightest clue. Pitiful Genzō, nineteen years old that autumn, his life as fleeting as the morning dew, dissolved into the seaweed at the ocean floor. With nothing to be done, they raised the sails and set the ship in order again. Parting from the white waves left in their wake, every one of them wrung their sleeves with tears. From there they arrived at a place called Sai-no-ura in Nanbu, stayed for more than ten days, arranged for a replacement for Genzō, hired a man called Sadagorō, and then arrived at a place called Hakodate within the domain of Matsumae.¹ Since this was a fine harbor,

they stayed, waiting for good weather for a while. Here, among the sailors, a young man named Chōsaku had been meeting secretly with a girl called Okin, the daughter of their lodgings, and on the night before their scheduled departure, he ran away taking the girl with him. This would later be understood as the thread of fate that saved his life. For one or two days they searched for Chōsaku's whereabouts, but found no trace of him at all. First Genzō had met his sudden end, and now again they had encountered such misfortune; everyone feared these might be ill omens for the journey ahead. Dejected, they nevertheless prepared the ship and departed. Thus, past the 20th of August, they arrived at the harbor of Kobuchi at the cape of Sendai² in the interior, lodged at the shipping agent's house, summoned a Buddhist priest, and held memorial services on the thirty-fifth day after Genzō's death. They also asked people around to find a replacement for Chōsaku, and hired a man known in those parts for his great strength, called Kanazuchi Chōta. With repairs made to the ship's equipment, and hoping to avoid the early autumn storms, they watched the weather for a while longer.
