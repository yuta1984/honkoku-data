They had agreed: if a ship turned back to Kobuchi, it would signal with two fires; if it remained offshore, each ship would show one fire. As the weather continued to deteriorate, many ships were already turning back. When the whole crew clamored to turn the ship around, Nihei all the more stubbornly held the ship in place—being no more than a mortal man, he could do nothing but watch the retreating ships and linger for a while, hoping perhaps the wind might yet improve. By now the sun had already sunk into the western sea, and the landscape on all sides, growing ever darker, was increasingly ominous. By that time, the only large vessel that remained in the area was the Shimamura Maru from Chikuzen.¹ When it was about half past the sixth hour (seven o'clock in the evening by modern reckoning), clouds black as spilled ink rose up, lightning flashed at intervals, and the wind from the west howled ever more fiercely. Now resigned to their fate, just as they were each preparing to row toward the lights of the distant shoreline, a single fierce squall came sweeping in with such force that the ship was on the verge of capsizing. With the addition of thunderous roaring and brilliant flashes of lightning, their spirits were utterly crushed, and they made to

furl the sails and cut down the mast—their hearts were willing, but what could they do? The ship was tossed and battered by mountainous raging waves, and they could not stand or move about at all. All the while, their bodies were soaked through with rain and seawater, and every limb of every man was frozen numb. The entire crew, utterly exhausted, cut off their hair-ties,² cast their "life substitutes"³ into the sea, and with fervent prayers to all the gods and buddhas, beseeched: "Return us to our home port, or if not, to Kobuchi, Saiura, Ezo, Matsumae⁴—anywhere on Japanese soil, we implore you." There was nothing left to do but pray. Yet they reasoned that the gods and buddhas would only help those who helped themselves, and that praying without effort would be of no avail—so the entire crew worked with every last ounce of strength they possessed. Whether the gods and buddhas took pity on them, after a time the thunder ceased, even the rain began to let up, and the moon of the twentieth night began to filter through the breaks in the clouds. Thus the night gradually brightened into dawn, and as the clouds torn apart by the wind rose with the climbing sun, the sky cleared completely.
