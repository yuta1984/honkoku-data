The official system has nine ranks, both principal and associate. The principal first rank is the Ōji (pronounced "Haansu"), the associate first rank is the Aji (pronounced "Ansu"), the principal second rank is the Sanshikan (pronounced "Sanzu") and Oyakata (pronounced "Oyakata"), and the associate second rank is also Oyakata. From the third to the seventh rank is the Peechin (pronounced "Baakishi"), the principal and associate eighth rank is the Satonushi (pronounced "Satonoshi"), and the principal and associate ninth rank is the Chikudun (pronounced "Jikutoshi").

Ōji is an official title; regardless of whether they share the king's surname or not, all those who have received a territorial fief are called "such-and-such Ōji." Even the king's own brothers, if they have not yet received a fief, may not call themselves Ōji.

Aji is equivalent to a regional lord. Baikumi (pronounced "Baikume") and Baikin (pronounced "Baikin") are both of the same rank as Peechin.

The royal府 uses a mountain as its castle, with each side measuring one ri, stone foundations stacked up, and surrounded by flowing water. The main hall is on the mountain's ridge, with the palace buildings in two stories, eight columns spanning north to south, facing west. The upper level is dedicated to the gods, the middle serves as the court hall, and the lower level is for the king to sit and stand with his retainers. The main hall broadly follows Chinese architectural conventions, while the residential quarters follow the style of this land (Japan). The conduct of the royal family also follows our (Japanese) customs.

Both men and women wear their hair exposed. Men cut their hair and tie a topknot on the right side. What the national history calls "cut hair and grass skirt" — the right topknot is called "Katakashira." According to tradition, people of ancient times all wore horns, and long ago a god's left horn broke off; subsequent generations imitated this. Women have lustrous hair like clouds, tied up into a high topknot, with hairpins unadorned. They tattoo their hands with ink, creating patterns of various flowers and plants. (Colloquially called "Haritsuki" [needle-piercing].)

It is the common custom to revere the gods, and the gods too are spiritually efficacious. Their god is called "Kinmamon." The women through whom the gods speak are called "Kimi-sha," numbering thirty-three, all daughters of chieftains. Their leader is called "Kikufu-kimi." The remaining shamans and spirit mediums throughout the land number in the hundreds and thousands. When the gods descend, drums beat and all sing together to delight the gods. When one person sings, a hundred respond in harmony, and the sound is mournful and melodious.

There are also sacred serpents, which the people of the country fear as they would a god.

The islands are mountainous and precipitous, with little flat land. The fields are sandy and gravelly, lean and poor, and farming is extremely difficult. The climate is always warm, and grains ripen early, but abundant harvests are not seen. Chūzan and Hokuzan have many paddy fields suited to cultivating non-glutinous rice. The area of Nanzan consists of dry fields suited to legumes and wheat. The current tax quota of the country is: Chūzan's grain rice — 71,787 koku; Hokuzan — 32,828 koku and 7 to; Nanzan — 19,096 koku and 8 to and a fraction. Other levies and taxes are proportional to these figures.

Among its products, horses, conch shells, cowrie shells, ramie cloth, cowhide, ebony, sulfur, and whetstones serve as tribute items. Grains include rice, millet, wheat, and soybeans; vegetables include melons, eggplant, ginger, garlic, scallions, and chives, and all such items are available.
