**[Continuation of the inscription (kanbun)]**

Divine beauty of a thousand years; once played, the melody rises in noble dignity. Countless birds sing winding songs in the trees; insects chirp in chorus from the thickets. Even wandering spirits weep together; lonely widows grieve all the more. Though the sound scatters, lingering echoes remain; the music enters a realm without end.
The resonance travels along the rafters and hums secretly in the mountain stream. Upon the high peaks, solemn tones of a flute echo through the forest; over flowing waters, a crystalline melody rings out. Grandly it accords with the Six Rites; it proclaims the Eight Winds. Nature and ethics, temperament and morality—all blend together in harmony.

> Written with reverence on the fifteenth day of the fifth month, in the year Kinomi (Kyūshi, 1713), the third year of Shōtoku, by Chūdaibu Ni Naokuni.

**[Habu Ryōfū Shōki (Record of the Cool Breeze at Habu)]**
*(Authored by the household of Tanaka Kaго; collated by Yamazaki Shibatari)*

In a certain year, the heat of the *sanpuku* (the Three Dog Days of midsummer) was extreme, and sweat flowed like liquid gruel. Then the peak of Mount Tagi suddenly grew overcast, and with a tremendous sound a summer shower came down. Before the rain even reached the village near where Ieтоshi lived, a man appeared at the eaves, gasping for breath, muttering "I'm all wet." Asking if he might have a cup of tea, he entered the house. Looking at him, one saw that he was enormously fat and dark-skinned—a man resembling a mountain ascetic (*yamabushi*). He wore a large-sleeved unlined robe (*katabira*), had what appeared to be a straw rope (*hayonawa*) around his waist, and carried a copper-hilted sword. Since the rain showed no sign of stopping, he came up into the kitchen and chatted about this and that. *(Abridged)*

The man said: "The Yōrō Waterfall is not far from here, so surely you must know its founding legend (*engi*). I would very much like to hear it." So the host (*Ietoshi*) recounted what he knew.

To begin with: the Yōrō Waterfall dates to the reign of Emperor Yūryaku, the twenty-second human emperor. In that place there lived a commoner called Genjōnai, who had an aged mother. His mother greatly enjoyed drinking sake. The family was poor—as scarce as the famous parable of "four in the morning, three at night"—yet he was filially devoted to his mother. He would rise early, go into the mountains, gather firewood, sell it at the market, and use the proceeds to buy sake to sustain his mother. On one occasion, Genjōnai was obliged to go to the capital on official business for the locality. Before leaving home, he told his wife: "In my absence, be as filially devoted as I am." His wife consented and stayed behind, never neglecting her duties for a moment.

One evening, he had a dream in which an old man appeared and told him: "Deep in the mountains there is a waterfall and a spring that flows from it. This is a divine healing water (*senyu*) fit for your aged mother. If she drinks it, her body will grow young again and she will live for a thousand years."
