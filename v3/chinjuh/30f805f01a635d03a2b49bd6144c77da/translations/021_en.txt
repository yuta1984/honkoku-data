...we had spread out a straw mat to enjoy the cool of the evening. The master of the house was a man fond of sake, and he laid out wine and food there. As I myself do not drink, I was sipping tea, when an old man came by, caught sight of the master, bowed with his hands lowered in greeting, and was about to head toward the back of the garden. The master called him back, pointed to the old man, and said: "This old fellow, when he was young, was saved by a bear. Having escaped with his life in peril, he has lived healthily and vigorously to this very year of eighty-two—a most auspicious old man indeed. Please do make his acquaintance." The old man smiled pleasantly and again made to leave. I called him back and said, "Being saved by a bear—that is a remarkable story. Please, do tell it to us." At this, the master took the teacup that had been in front of me, said "First, have a drink," and poured the sake to the brim. The old man sat on the edge of the straw mat, looked at the sake with a smile on his lips, drank three cups one after another, smacked his lips in great delight, and said: "Very well then, I shall tell my tale."

"In my twentieth year, at the beginning of the second month, I set out pulling a sled to gather firewood in the mountains. The places close to the village had all been stripped bare, and what little remained was in spots with treacherous footing. So I crossed over one ridge and found an abundance of brushwood suitable for firewood. I cut to my heart's content, and singing as I went, I quietly bundled the wood and loaded it onto the sled, lashed it down, tucked my mountain knife into the bundle, and began to ride the sled downhill along the way I had come. Then one bundle of wood tumbled off the sled and became wedged in a crack in the snow that filled the valley below.

(※ It is quite common for frozen snow to crack open when warmed by the weather.)

It seemed a waste to leave it behind and go home, so I went to the spot, took hold of the branches of the bundle, and tried to pull it up—but it would not budge at all. It must have been driven in by the force of its fall. I thought then to pull from the heavier side, and so I lay face down, stretched out both hands, and with a shout tried to haul it up. But as my feet had no firm footing, my own strength worked against me, and I tumbled over, plunging from the crack in the snow far down to the bottom of the valley. As I had slid down over the snow's surface, I fortunately suffered no injury, and for a time I was as in a dream. When I finally came to my senses and looked up, it was as if a great folding screen of snow had been set up all around me, and it seemed as though an avalanche might come at any moment.

(※ The terror of avalanches is described below.)

I felt no sense of being alive. The darkness was deep, but at the very least I resolved to make my way toward the lighter direction, and I followed along the narrow, snow-buried valley floor until I reached a place where I could see the sky. Yet the cold at the bottom of that snowy valley was fierce, my hands and feet were numb and stiffened, and I could barely take a single step. Knowing I would freeze to death if I stayed, I steeled my heart and pressed on, wondering if there might yet be a way out, and after going about half a *chō* [roughly 55 meters], I reached a spot where a waterfall stood. Looking all around me, I saw it was a dead end in the valley. I was like a mouse that has fallen into a jar, utterly without recourse. I stood dazed, my chest tightening, and could not even muster the thought of what to do.
