There must be many places with underground fire veins that have yet to ignite because they have not yet obtained a yang fire (an actual flame).

Momotoki writes: When I was staying in Ojiya, Iwai offered to show me the fire of Jigoku Valley and brought along five fellow companions. Two servants were loaded with food and sake prepared for the occasion, and I traveled together with Kyōsui, making a party of ten in all. We left Ojiya and headed westward, passing through villages called Shinbo Village and Yabukawa Shinden, until we reached a village called Ichinomiya. The path wound along narrow ridgeway trails through the mountain valleys, and the distance to our destination was about one and a half ri. That day the weather was exceptionally clear, and the autumn scenery of the village in all its varied splendor captivated the eye. We crossed over a low, flat hill and came to a slope — this was the path leading down to Jigoku Valley. Looking down from the top of the slope, we could see a single thatched hut below. This was the bathhouse mentioned in the main text. When our party had reached halfway down the slope, four or five beautiful women appeared on the upper floor of the thatched building, each leaning against the railing. Some pointed at our group from afar, some laughed, some called out names, some clapped their hands, and some raised their hands and beckoned to us. Surrounded on all four sides by mountains, with old trees growing thick and dense, to see these beauties in such a setting gave us quite a start, and I said, "These must be tanuki (raccoon dogs) if not foxes!" At this, Iwai and the friends exchanged glances and burst out laughing. These were in fact geisha girls from a restaurant in the lower part of town in Ojiya. Iwai had conspired with his companions to secretly arrange for them to be brought here in advance, intending to treat me to a pleasant surprise. It was not they who were foxes — it was I who had been bewitched by Iwai. We descended to Jigoku Valley and everyone went up to the upper floor. Iwai accompanied me and Kyōsui to show us the fire.

Now, this valley had many mountain cherry trees and was originally called Sakura Valley (Cherry Valley), but because of the earth fire, an area of forty to fifty paces on each side (a "pace" being six shaku) was cleared and leveled, and using the earth fire to heat a bathing room, it was made into a place for people to enjoy leisure. That a place once called Sakura Valley (Cherry Valley) should now be called Jigoku (Hell) on account of the earth fire — the cherry blossoms must surely find this most lamentable.

Now, upon observing the fire, there was a shallow well that had been dug, and the fire burning from within that well was more vigorous than the fire of an ordinary bathhouse.
