○ Fire-Resistant Cloth (Kakanfu / 火浣布)

During the Hōreki era (1751–1764), Hiraga Kyūkei (Gennai) first produced fire-washing cloth and wrote the *Kakanfu-kō* (Study on Fire-Resistant Cloth), citing ancient texts from Japan and China, and boasted that it was an unprecedented marvel of craftsmanship in our country. After his death, his technique was not passed down, and this became a source of regret among enthusiasts of curious arts.

However, our province (Echigo) has long produced the very stone needed to make fire-resistant cloth. Its sources include Mount Kinjō, Mount Makihata, Mount Naebasan, Mount Hakkai, and others. The stone is soft and weak — so soft that it can be scratched with a fingernail. Its color is a bluish-black, and when broken apart, it yields stone-cotton (asbestos). Having obtained this stone and tested it, I found that the asbestos within it resembles finely spun cotton cut into pieces of about two to three *bu* (approximately 6–9 mm) in length. There is a secret technique for spinning this into thread and weaving fire-resistant cloth. If one masters that secret technique, even a young girl could weave fire-resistant cloth.

○ Now, in our post-town (Shiozawa), a man named Inariya Kiuemon devoted enormous thought and effort to the spinning of asbestos, and at last mastered the technique himself, successfully weaving fire-resistant cloth. Around the same time, the physician Kuroda Genkaku of the nearby village of Ōsawa also independently mastered the technique for weaving fire-resistant cloth. Both kept their methods secret and did not teach them to others — and it is itself a remarkable thing that in the same period, in neighboring villages, two people independently achieved the same remarkable craft of fire-cloth. This occurred sometime during the Bunsei 4th and 5th years (1821–1822).

Having heard the accounts of both men, I learned that if one exerts full effort, it is possible to weave a piece of over one *jō* (approximately 3 meters) in length — though the process of weaving on the loom is by no means easy, they said. Hiraga Gennai stated in the *Kakanfu-kō* that he never succeeded in weaving more than five or six *shaku* (approximately 1.5–1.8 meters). Furthermore, one respect in which Genkaku surpassed Gennai is that in addition to fire-resistant cloth, Genkaku also produced two further inventions: fire-resistant paper (*kakanshi*) and fire-resistant ink (*kakanboku*). Writing on fire-resistant paper with fire-resistant ink, and then placing it in a roaring fire until it burst into flames, one could carefully retrieve it, and once the heat had cooled, both the paper and the writing remained exactly as before.
