(Continuation of Ishiuchi Myōjin) ...are filled to the brim with rounded stones. ○ Hyakuju remarks: When I too visited Ojiya, I saw these stones and thought to take one back with me as a conversation piece. But when I heard the local people say, "It is passed down that this deity holds these stones precious," I returned the stone I had taken to its original place. Looking carefully, I saw that the countless thousands of stones had each become perfectly round, as if polished by human hands into jewels. In all things, the mysterious workings of the divine cannot be measured by the knowledge of mortal flesh.

### ○ A Beautiful Woman

Hyakuju remarks: I write the following in connection with Ojiya. When I was lodging at the house of Gankyo in Ojiya (eighth month of Tenpō 7 [1836]), one day, grown weary of taking up the brush, I thought I would go and view the autumn scenery of the mountains and river. I set out alone, climbed the hill overlooking the river that flows before Ojiya, spread out the felt mat I had brought on which to write, and as I puffed on my pipe and gazed about, I saw: upstream, a boat being hauled against the current, barely seeming to move; downstream, another boat following the current, seeming almost to fly. Migrating geese aligned themselves in the sky like characters in a row; woodcutters returning home unfolded the scene like a painting. The trees in the grove were lightly touched by frost, glowing red; the ranges of mountains barely wore their caps of snow, gleaming white. The autumn scenery of this snow country was a fresh vision for eyes accustomed to Edo, and unexpectedly I composed a *zekku* verse — and so I had been gazing for some time, when three girls of about sixteen or seventeen, each carrying a bundle of brushwood on her back, came climbing up the mountain and paused to rest here. I could hear them exchanging words and laughing among themselves. My eyes had been wholly captivated by the scenery, when one of them drew near with her pipe and said, "Could you give us a light?" — and looking at her face, I saw that beneath disheveled hair and without a trace of make-up, her natural allure was such that she might be called a flower, or likened to a jewel. Rags of a hundred patches clothed this priceless gem. I was so startled that I forgot the scenery entirely and could only stare at her. She gave a bow and withdrew, then sat down on the grass beneath a tree, stretching out her legs. She passed the light from my pipe, and all three girls smoked together. Two of them were unremarkable in appearance, but one alone was a very Xishi — when she spoke, the others beside her were like reeds next to a jade tree; when her white teeth gleamed and she laughed, it was like a white lotus rising from the water and swaying in a gentle breeze. Ah, what a pity! Such a beauty, born in this remote and rustic place, will become the wife of some dull and boorish lout; a clever wife perpetually yoked to an incompetent husband, sleeping her life away, rotting together with the brambles and thorns — it is beyond enduring to contemplate. Were she to be brought to Edo, she could become in some great mansion a "flower that speaks" [i.e., a prized beauty], or perhaps in the Yoshiwara she would bring forth the glory of a "money-yielding tree."
