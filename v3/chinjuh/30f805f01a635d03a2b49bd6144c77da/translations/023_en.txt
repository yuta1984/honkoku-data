(After the old man had been smoking his pipe for a while,) the listener asked, "And what happened next?" The old father replied: "Well, looking to my side, I found a rocky cave just large enough to crawl into. There was no snow inside, so I crept in and found it was a little warm. At that moment I came to my senses and felt around my waist, only to find that I had somewhere along the way dropped my packed rice-ball lunch. At this rate I would surely starve to death. Even so, I thought, if I ate snow, I could survive five or ten days. If during that time I could hear the sound of sledge-drivers' songs, they would be villagers, and if I shouted loudly enough they would come to my rescue. Above all, I had no recourse but to pray to Lord Ise and Lord Zenkoji, and I chanted nenbutsu prayers fervently and prayed to the Grand Shrine. As evening was drawing near, I resolved to make this my sleeping place, and groping through the darkness I crawled further in, finding it growing gradually warmer. Groping still further, what my outstretched hand touched was unmistakably a bear. I was startled so greatly it was as though my chest would split open—but there was no way to flee, and my life was at its limit regardless. I steeled myself to leave life and death to the mercy of the gods and Buddha, and trembling with fear, I stroked the bear, saying: 'Please, Master Bear—I am someone who came to gather firewood and fell into the valley. I have no road by which to return, and no food to keep me alive. My life is forfeit anyway. If you wish to tear me apart and kill me, then kill me. But if you have any compassion, please help me.' At this, the bear seemed to sit itself up, and after a short while it moved forward and pushed me aside with its rump. When I sat in the spot where the bear had been lying, the warmth was just like sitting beside a kotatsu—my whole body warmed through, and I forgot the cold. I gave the bear all manner of thanks and begged it further to save me, telling it various pitiful things. The bear then raised its paw and pressed it softly against my mouth several times. Remembering what I had heard about ants, I licked it, and found it sweet with a slight bitterness. I kept licking away at it, and my spirits lifted, and my throat was moistened. The bear then began to breathe heavily through its nose, as if falling asleep. 'So then, it truly means to help me,' I thought, and my heart settled greatly. After that, I lay down side by side, back to back, with the bear, but my thoughts were only of home and I could not sleep. I lay there lost in thought, until at some point I must have drifted off. I was awakened by the bear stirring, and looking up I could see the mouth of the cave—so I knew dawn had come. I crawled out of the cave and looked here and there to see if there might be a path home, or some wisteria vine I could use to climb the mountain, but there was none. The bear too emerged from the cave and went to the basin of the waterfall to drink. It was at this moment that I saw the bear clearly for the first time: it was a great bear as large as seven dogs put together. It went back into the cave, so I sat at the mouth of the cave, straining my ears for the sound of sledge-drivers' songs, but there was nothing but the sound of dripping water—not even the sound of birds. That day too passed in vain, and I spent another night in the cave, staving off hunger with licks of the bear's paw. No matter how many days passed, I heard no songs, and the desolation I felt was beyond words. Yet the bear was gradually growing accustomed (to me—)"
