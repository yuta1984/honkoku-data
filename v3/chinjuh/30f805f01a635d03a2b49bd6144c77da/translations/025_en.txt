...this is not so remarkable. Flies are born from ash. Ash is the powdery residue left after fire burns out; therefore, the fly is the creature of fire. If you kill a fly and, while its form still remains, place it in ash, it will revive. Likewise, lice are born from the heat of the human body. Heat belongs to fire. Being creatures born of fire, both flies and lice prefer warmth. The creatures within metal are so minute—like invisible dust—that no human eye can perceive them, which is why people do not know of their existence. Generally speaking, when copper or iron begins to corrode, it is because creatures are being generated. The places where these creatures appear change color. If one wipes the spot frequently, the creatures are killed, and so that place corrodes. Rust is the beginning of corrosion, and within rust there are always creatures. Because they are beyond the reach of the naked eye, people do not know of them. [This is according to the account of the Dutch.] If there are creatures even within metal, why should there not be creatures within snow? However, since it is not an everyday occurrence, it was recorded as a curiosity in the books of China. The snow-maggots (*setsujo*) of our Echigo are small as mosquitoes. There are two varieties: one has wings and flies about; the other has wings but keeps them folded and crawls. Both have six legs. Their color resembles that of a fly, but paler [one variety is black]. Their habitat—open towns and fields—is the same as the mosquito's. However, they are not creatures that sting people. I have illustrated here what I observed through a magnifying glass (*mushimegane*), and await the judgment of naturalists.

　　　　　○Blizzards (*Fubuki*)

"Blizzard" (*fubuki*) refers to snow that has accumulated on trees and other surfaces being scattered by the wind. Because this sight is graceful and beautiful, people compare the scattering of blossoms to it, calling it *hana-fubuki* ("flower blizzard"), and it appears in many ancient poems. But that is the matter of lands to the east and south, where only an inch or so of snow falls. In the northern lands where snow falls fathoms deep—in our Echigo, in places of great snow depth—a blizzard is a violent storm within the snow, a whirlwind that sweeps the snow up and swirls it into the sky. It is the greatest hardship amidst snow, and every year people die because of it. I shall record one example here, to show those who have seen only the gentle blizzards of shallow-snow lands the terrifying nature of the blizzards of deep-snow lands.

Not far from Shiozawa, where I live, there was a young man, the son of a farmer. He was earnest and devoted to his parents. In the winter of his twenty-second year, he took as his bride a nineteen-year-old girl from a village more than two *ri* away. She was not unpleasant to look at; by nature she was gentle, and she was skilled at spinning and weaving—so her parents-in-law doted on her, the couple lived in harmony, and the household was a happy one as they welcomed the new year. Early in the ninth month of that year, she had an easy delivery, and it was moreover a boy—the family felt as though they had found a jewel in the palm of their hand. The whole household rejoiced and celebrated, the mother recovered her health vigorously, her milk was more than enough for one child, and the infant grew plump and thriving. A felicitous name was bestowed upon the child, and a long life of a thousand years was wished for him. All the members of this household were earnest and diligent, devoted to farming and weaving; though they were a small farming family——
