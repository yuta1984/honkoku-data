Whether a person freezes to death or their limbs become numb and contracted, it is all because the "yin poison" (the toxic cold) blocks the blood vessels. If one hastily warms the person with hot water or fire, the vital energy and blood of the body are stimulated, and the yin poison appears to dissolve momentarily, but it does not depart completely. Since yin cannot overcome yang, when yang energy arrives, the yin poison seeps into the flesh and causes it to rot. One must never use hot water or fire to warm a person who has grown cold from walking through snow in the dead of winter. One should wait until the person's own body heat warms them naturally before applying such remedies. This is one technique for long life.

　　　　　○Fire in the Snow

Among what are known in the world as the "Seven Wonders of Echigo," one is the fire that emerges from the hole of a stone mortar in the corner of the hearth of a farmhouse in Myōhōji Village, Kambara District. People have long regarded this as wondrous, passing it down through oral tradition and noting it in various writings. The old records show that this fire first appeared during the Kanbun era (1661–1673), so the fact that it has not ceased even now, more than three hundred years later, is a wonder among wonders. Heaven does not limit itself to producing just one wonder: in Uonuma District of the same province, yet another mysterious fire has appeared. Heaven's mechanism is the same as that of the fire at Myōhōji Village. The Myōhōji fire is well known, but this one is unknown to people from other provinces, so I record it here as a topic of conversation.

In Echigo Province, Uonuma District, near a post town called Itsukamachi, there is a low mountain to the west. At the foot of the mountain runs a small ditch. During the Tenmei era (1781–1789), around the second month, children had gathered near this spot and were playing various games. Growing tired of play, they collected tree branches and lit a fire to warm themselves, when suddenly, at a spot a little away from where they were, another fire blazed up and rose into the air on its own. The children were greatly frightened and scattered in all directions. One of the children returned home and described the details of the incident to a parent, who happened to be a person of discernment. This parent went to the spot and examined the fire's appearance. The fire had not yet gone out. A hole had formed in the snow — just large enough to put one's hand into — and fire was burning three or four *sun* (approximately 9–12 cm) above the hole. After careful observation, the parent concluded: "This must certainly be the same kind of thing as the fire at Myōhōji Village." The parent placed a stone into the mouth of the fire to extinguish it, then returned home and said nothing to anyone. After the snow melted, returning again to the spot, the parent found that the fire had burned on the bank of that small ditch. Striking flint to light a *tsukegi* (tinder stick) and tossing it experimentally into the pool, fire sprang from within the pool just like a garden bonfire. Since fire burning upon water was even more wondrous than the Myōhōji Village fire, people from the post town came to see it. Afterward, an enterprising and clever person built a bathhouse by the pool, drew the subterranean fire through bamboo pipes as if conducting water, and used it to heat the furnace under the bathtub...
