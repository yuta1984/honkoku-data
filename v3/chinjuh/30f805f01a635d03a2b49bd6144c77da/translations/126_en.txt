**Waragutsu (Straw Boots)**
Weaving begins with a single bundle of straw. At the start, the base of the straw is rounded and weaving begins; as it progresses toward the ends, more straw is added, split into two strands, and folded back. The finish is tied and secured in the middle. These are the foremost footwear for walking in snow. Even children wear them. The finer ones use white paper at the start of the weaving, and have pieces of tatami facing inserted at the tread area.

**Fukagutsu (Deep Boots)**
These are made by weaving straw on the inside. One can wear these over regular tabi (split-toe socks) and walk through snow, and there is no need to wash one's feet when entering someone else's sitting room. The weaving technique is extremely difficult. Here in this province (Echigo), only a general outline is recorded. In other provinces, leather versions have been seen. These would be convenient for muddy roads, but in our province's snow, there is no mud on the roads. Therefore, footwear other than wooden clogs (geta) is made from straw. The clogs have various names such as "Koma no Tsume" (horse's nail) and "Ushi no Tsume" (ox's nail), and their shapes differ according to use by men and women, but as they are not so remarkable, no illustration is provided.

**Hatsuhaki (Straw Leg Guards)**
"Hatsuhaki" is a local vernacular term; it is essentially a *habaki* (leg gaiter/guard). It is made from soft straw scraps or from cattail (gama). It is always used when walking in snow. Those who work in the mountains use it regularly. The method of making it should be understood in general outline from the illustration. Simply put, it is a *kyahan* (legging) made of straw. Since straw protects against cold, most snow footwear is made from straw.

**Munéate (Chest Protector)**
This is made from bark known as "Shina-kawa," taken from a tree (the Japanese linden/shinanotki) found in deep mountain forests. The dimensions are tailored to the body, but are generally about 2 shaku 3 sun in length and about 2 shaku in width. It is also called a "chest guard." It is used to protect against snow blowing from the front. It is also used regularly during agricultural work. It can be found in other provinces as well.

**Shibugárami (Straw Ankle Wrap)**
The *Shibugárami* is made by placing the starting end of the weave against the shin and wrapping the straw strands on both sides around the ankle. In local vernacular, the soft straw scraps are called "*Shibi*." Because this item is made from *shibi* and wrapped around the foot, it should properly be called "*Shibigárami*," but through dialectal corruption it is called "*Shibugárami*."

**Kajiki (Snowshoes)**
"Kanjiki" is the classical reading. The local vernacular term is "kajiki." They are 1 shaku 2 to 3 sun in length and 7 sun 5 to 6 bu in width, shaped as shown in the illustration. They are made from branches of a tree called "Jagara." The tip (nose) is curved upward, and a vine called "Kumaibu" or another vine called "Kazura" is used. They are reinforced by wrapping with the thick bark of mountain lacquer tree (yamaürushi). These are worn underneath the boots illustrated earlier, to prevent sinking into the snow.

**Sugari (Large Snowshoes)**
"Sugari" range from 2 shaku 5 to 6 sun to over 3 shaku in length, and 1 shaku 2 to 3 sun in width, and are made by bending mountain bamboo. The two items — kajiki and sugari — are used in winter when the snow is soft, to prevent sinking in. Those unaccustomed to wearing them find them difficult to walk in. Those accustomed to them wear them while chasing wild animals.
