"(The bear had gradually grown accustomed to me and) had become quite dear to me," the old man was saying, when the host, now pleasantly drunk, turned to the old man and said, "Was that bear a female, by any chance?" At this all three burst into great laughter. They drank more wine together, and for a while the story disappeared amid the exchange of cups. When pressed to continue, the old man spoke:

"A person's heart changes according to what they encounter. When I first met the bear, I had resigned myself to dying then and there, and I had no attachment to life. But after being helped by the bear, I gradually grew more and more reluctant to die. Even if no one came to rescue me, I thought, as long as the snow melted I would cling to tree roots and rock ledges if need be and make my way home—so I waited desperately for the snow to melt. I lost track of even how many days had passed, drifting along in a daze, but the bear had become like a domestic dog to me, and for the first time I understood how precious the company of a human being is. Being in a valley, the snow there melted even more slowly than in the village, and I had nothing to look forward to but the passing of days. Then one day, as I sat at the mouth of the cave in a patch of sunlight picking lice off myself, the bear came out of the cave and took hold of my sleeve in its mouth and pulled. I had no idea what it intended, but I let myself be led along until we came near the spot where I had first slipped and fallen. The bear went ahead, digging through the snow freely, opening up a single path. Wherever it led, I followed, and the bear kept opening up path after path until we came to a place where there were human footprints. The bear looked all around in every direction, then ran off and disappeared from sight. So then—it had been guiding me all along! I prostrated myself in the direction the bear had gone, bowing again and again in gratitude, declaring that this was entirely through the grace of the gods and Buddha. I bowed in worship toward Lord Ise and Lord Zenkoji. I was so overjoyed I scarcely knew where to set my feet, and I made it back to the inn just as the evening lights were being lit.

At that time, the neighbors had all gathered there and were reciting nenbutsu prayers for me. My parents and everyone else were startled out of their wits, crying out that surely I must be a ghost. And no wonder—my stubble had grown out like a straw raincoat, and my face had withered like a fox's. The commotion about my being a ghost eventually turned to laughter, and my parents above all, and everyone around, rejoiced. The funeral rites being held that very night—it being the forty-ninth day since I had gone out to gather firewood, and they were holding the memorial service—were suddenly transformed into a joyful drinking party."

This detailed account was told by a farmer named Kuuemon from Komai. That same night, by lamplight, I took up my brush and recorded the story exactly as it was told. But now it has all become a thing of the past.

　　　　　○Insects in Snow

In the land of China, in the region of Shu, Mount Emei bears snow even in summer, and it is recorded in the *Shanhaijing* (Classic of Mountains and Seas) that within that snow lives a creature called the *setsujo* (snow maggot). This account is not without foundation—in the snows of Echigo too, there are *setsujo*. These creatures appear in the snow from early spring onward, and when the snow has completely melted, the creatures too disappear completely. Their beginning and end, their life and death, are the same as the snow's. Consulting the character dictionary, we find that *jo* (蛆) is defined as the fly found in rotting matter—that is, the maggot-fly. *Tatsu* (䖧) is described as being of the *tai* (蠆) family, which stings people, making it a type of bee. For insects found in snow, the character *jo* (蛆) seems most appropriate. Thus "snow-*jo*" (*setsujo*) would mean the maggot-fly of the snow.
