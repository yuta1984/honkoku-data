The force of the frozen snow weakens near the water, the flow is narrowed by the snow blocking it, and thus the force of the water grows ever more violent. Fed by the warm air, it burrows beneath the soft underside of the snow — much like a levee breaking — bringing a disaster that strikes, as the saying goes, "like water in a sleeping ear" (completely without warning). Flooding amidst snow is a hardship unique to cold countries. May those who live in warmer regions take pity on this suffering. The above describes but one example. Floods in the snow vary greatly depending on the terrain and cannot easily be explained in detail.

　　　　　○Bear Hunting

To the northwest, Echigo faces the open sea with no high mountains. To the southeast, ranges of mountains stretch majestically across five provinces — Etchū, Shinano, Etsu, and U羽 — where peak upon peak stands shoulder to shoulder for tens of leagues, and as a result great and small beasts are exceedingly numerous. Among these animals, some flee the snow and leave for other provinces, while others do not. The only creature that remains still and dwells in holes beneath the snow is the bear. Bear bile (kumanoi) from Echigo is considered of the finest quality. Bear bile obtained in the snow season is especially valuable, and in hopes of obtaining its high price, hunters from the region of Dewa — around five to seven men — join forces when spring warmth arrives and the snowfall has ceased. They lead three or four fierce dogs and provision themselves with rice, salt, and a pot, making use of water and firewood found in the mountains as needed. They travel from mountain to mountain, hunting animals for food during the day,

and at night they make their beds at the roots of trees or in rocky caverns, burning green wood to ward off the cold and to provide light, sleeping in the very clothes they wear. From head to foot, everything they wear is made from the hides of animals. Seen from a distance, they resemble monkeys, yet their faces are human. The saying "to use armor as one's bedding" might well describe such men. Their purpose in entering our mountains is the bear. They identify a suitable location, construct a temporary shelter from tree branches and wisteria vines, and use it as their base. Each man leads his dog and splits off in different directions to search for bears. When they discover where a bear has holed up, they leave a marker and return to the shelter, then combine their full strength to capture it. Their tools include hand-spears with shafts of about four shaku in length, mountain blades fashioned like naginata (halberd-style blades), firearms, mountain swords, axes, and the like. When the blades dull, they sharpen them with whetstones they carry with them. These tools too are kept in sheaths made of animal hide. These hunters sometimes enter the mountains not only in spring but even from winter.

Now, the bear is the king of Japan's wild animals — fierce, yet possessed of righteousness. It eats nuts, tree bark, and wood-boring insects, and does not prey upon animals of its own kind, nor does it destroy fields and paddies. On the rare occasions it does cause damage, it is only when its food supply has run out. In the Book of Odes (Shijing), the bear is taken as an auspicious omen for the birth of a son, and perhaps the Six Brave Generals who obtained it did so because of its righteous nature.
