(Continuation of the White Crow entry) As it gradually grew, its call was no different from that of an ordinary crow. Since it was in my neighborhood, I observed it morning and evening. Being a rare bird, many people wished to have it, and there were those who said they wanted to send it to Edo to exhibit as a curiosity, but the master was reluctant to part with it and refused. As things stood, that winter brought the snowy season, and as mountain weasels and foxes, short of food, commonly come to people's homes and steal food during the snowy months, it may have been the work of such a creature — the cage was found torn apart, and only the feathers of the white crow remained beneath the eaves, I was told. Since I had recorded the matter of a white bear in the first volume, I have set down the account of the white crow here as well.

### ○ A Two-Headed Snake

On the twentieth day of the eighth month in the Year of the Boar, Bunsei 10 (1827), a two-headed snake appeared at the eaves of the house of a farmer named Tazaemon in Yokawa Village, in the countryside of the neighboring post town of Muikamachi, and was captured. It was less than one *shaku* (approximately 30 cm) in length; its two heads simply branched side by side, while its color and form were otherwise no different from an ordinary snake. It was placed as it was into an old box with food left inside, but within two or three days it had escaped without anyone noticing. A search was made in the vicinity, but it was never found.

### ○ Floating Islands

About one *ri* (approximately 4 km) to the west of Ojiya, there is a village called Yoshitani. Here there is a pond called the "Kōri-dono Pond," roughly two to three *chō* (approximately 220–330 meters) on each side, in which there are thirteen floating islands. On clear, windless days when the sun rises, the thirteen small islands each separate and drift about the pond as if wandering freely. When the sun sets, they gather at the center of the pond and become one single island. There are various other wonders associated with this pond, but to write of them all would make for too lengthy a text, so I will leave them out. The floating islands of Ushū (Dewa Province) are recorded in various writings and are well known to people; but these floating islands are known to few.

### ○ Ishiuchi Myōjin

Within the area of Ojiya, on the land of a certain farmer, there is a small shrine called Ishiuchi Myōjin, which has been worshipped there since ancient times. I missed the chance to hear its founding legend (*engi*). Those who have warts pray to this deity, stroke their warts with a small stone, and then toss the stone inside the latticed space beneath the shrine's veranda. In no time at all, the wart falls away — a remarkable and mysterious effect. Furthermore, whatever shape the tossed stone may have been, it eventually becomes round, as if someone had deliberately rounded it — yet another strange and wondrous thing.
