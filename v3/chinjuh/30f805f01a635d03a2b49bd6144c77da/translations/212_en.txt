After Gennai's death, his remarkable art was lost — and yet when the two aforementioned men appeared and the technique of weaving fire-resistant cloth once again emerged into the world, alas, how regrettable — these two men also died without passing on their art, and so fire-resistant cloth again vanished from the world. Gennai wove his fire-resistant cloth in the bustling metropolis of Edo, and thus his fame was great; these two men wove theirs in the remote backcountry of Echigo, and thus their names remain obscure. It is for this reason that I record it here, to offer it as a topic of interest for lovers of curious things.

○ The Mummified Monk Kōchi Hōin

Kōchi Hōin was of the Kodama clan, a native of Yamakuwa Village in Shimōsa Province. He studied esoteric Buddhism (*mikkyō*) at Mount Kōya, later returned to his homeland, took up residence at Rengeji Temple in Ōura, and then traveled on pilgrimage to Echigo. In Mishima District, at a place called Iwasaka to the east of Saikōji Temple (of Mount Kaiun) in Nozumi Village (locally also pronounced "Nozomi"), he set down his pilgrim's staff and built a small hermitage. On the second day of the tenth month of Jōji 2 (1363), he passed away in this hermitage. As a parting verse, there is an oral tradition of the following poem:

　*"Should anyone ask who is the master of Iwasaka,*
*tell them: the sound of the wind through pines, painted in ink."*

As a final testament, he left instructions that his remains should not be buried. Now, as of Tenpō 9 (1838), four hundred and seventy-seven years have passed since that time, yet his dried remains appear as though alive. This is counted as one of the Twenty-Four Wonders of Echigo. Accounts of this appear scattered throughout various writings, but none include an illustration — and so I present one here. This illustration is based on what I myself witnessed when I visited lower Echigo in earlier years. What one can see is only the face; the hands and feet are not visible. By temple rule, one is not permitted to view it up close. His eyes are closed, his face is wrinkled, and he appears to be sleeping. The hood and robes are likely not the originals from long ago. This is a marvel of Echigo heard of nowhere else in the land.

Momoki (the author's collaborator Santō Kyōden's pen-name in this work) remarks — In China, too, there are cases similar to that of Kōchi. After the monk Gison of the Tang dynasty passed away, his body was placed in a box, and each month his disciples would take it out and trim the nails and hair that had grown — this was their customary practice. It continued for over a hundred years without ceasing, but later, due to the turmoil of war in the land, the body was cremated.
