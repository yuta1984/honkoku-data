○Upon reflection: Within the earth, there exist water veins and fire veins. Since the earth is the great Yin, water veins account for nine parts and fire veins for one part. Fire veins are therefore exceedingly rare. Where fire veins in the earth congeal and concentrate, they invariably emit a breath of vapor — much like a person's breath — invisible to the naked eye. When the everyday yang fire (ordinary flame) used by people is applied to this vapor from the fire veins, it ignites and produces a flame. This is called *inка* (yin fire) or *kanка* (cold fire). The reason bamboo pipes used to conduct this cold fire do not become scorched is that the vaporous breath of the fire vein has not yet received yang fire to become actual flame — it is nothing more than vapor at that stage. Once it receives yang fire, it produces a flame one or two *sun* (roughly 3–6 cm) above the mouth of the pipe. From this, one can understand that the vapor of the fire vein is what burns. The fire at Myōhōji Village is the same phenomenon. This is not my own original discovery; it is what I have worked out by consulting ancient texts.

　　　　　○Waremeki-yama (Cracked Mountain)

Deep in the mountains behind Shimizu Village in Uonuma District, there is a mountain. Its height is a little over one *ri* (approximately 4 km), and its circumference is also a little over one *ri*. Because there are crevices and cracks of various sizes throughout the mountain, this name was given to it. The lower half of the mountain is covered with old trees whose branches intertwine, while above the midpoint, rocks and boulders pile upon one another. Their shapes are like dancing dragons and raging tigers — so strange and fantastical as to defy description. On either side of the mountain's foot flow valley streams, which join together to form a waterfall. The spectacular scenery is likewise beyond words. During droughts, when people pray for rain at the base of this waterfall, there is invariably a response. One year, around the middle of the fourth month when the snow had melted, more than twenty farmers from Shimizu Village gathered and climbed this mountain intending to hunt bears. Believing that the crevices forming caves in the mountain were certain to be bears' dens, they mixed stems of *tōgarashi* (red pepper) and tobacco into their firewood — a customary method — and lit fires at the mouths of the caves. But no bears emerged whatsoever. Thinking the caves must be too deep for the smoke to penetrate to the back, the following day they increased the firewood and stoked the fires as if to burn the whole mountain down, yet still no bears came out. Instead, smoke began rising from crevices here and there all over the mountain, swelling up like clouds. Filled with a sense of wonder, the farmers gave up on their hunt and returned home empty-handed — or so the farmers of Shimizu Village told me. In my estimation, above the midpoint of this mountain, the rock forms its bones while the layer of earthen flesh is thin, and the earth's veins allow vapors to circulate, thereby creating these crevices. The wondrous workings of heaven and earth are truly beyond calculation.

　　　　　○Nadare (Avalanche)

The collapse and tumbling of snow down a mountain is called *nadare* in the local dialect. It is also called *nade*. Upon reflection, *nadare* derives from *nade-oriru* (to slide down smoothly). Saying *re* instead of *ru* is a form of conjugation. The term is also used for mountains themselves. Here I borrow the character 頽 (*tai*, meaning "collapse") to write it.
