## The Crane's Repayment of Grace

In the spring of the seventh year of the Tenpō era (1836), the year of Hinoe-saru, a merchant of Ojiya chijimi cloth from our district, Yoshizawaya Tōgorō (known by his haiku pen name Nishō), traveled to the western provinces for business. While lodging in a certain castle town, he heard the following story from his innkeeper.

A farmer in the vicinity had discovered a sick crane in his fields, on the verge of death. He nursed the crane back to health using ginseng root from his stores, and before long the crane recovered and flew away.

The following October, two cranes descended near the farmer's garden and dropped two stalks of rice, each giving a single cry before flying off again. The farmer picked them up and examined them: the stalks measured over six shaku (approximately 180 cm) in height, with correspondingly long ears of grain, each bearing four to five hundred grains per panicle. The farmer thought to himself: "Surely these must have been brought in the beaks of last year's sick crane from a foreign land, as repayment for the kindness shown. Whatever the case, this is a most remarkable rice," and presented it to the local lord. After being kept for a time, the stalks were returned to the farmer with the instruction to cultivate them carefully. When the planting season came, he tended them with great care, and the rice grew just as magnificently as the crane-gifted stalks had suggested. It was said that some was also presented to the provincial lord.

Tōgorō inquired further about the village and the person involved, and discovered that the man who had saved the crane was the very same household where he had sold his chijimi cloth. He went immediately to the house, heard the full account in detail, and asked: "Would you give me one or two grains of unhulled rice as a souvenir for my home country?" The owner replied, "Echigo is said to be a fine country for rice, so it should grow well there," and gave him fifty or sixty grains of unhulled rice. Tōgorō brought these back to his province, explained the circumstances, and presented them to his lord. The rice was planted within the castle grounds, and Tōgorō was duly rewarded — or so the people of Ojiya told the story at that time.

I reflect that even a humble farmer such as myself was born into such a blessed and peaceful age, and it is precisely because of this that I can live in peace and take up my brush in this way. And so, praying for a thousand years of prosperity and tranquility, I set down my brush with this story of the crane. There remain many more wondrous tales of snow and other curious matters that I have not been able to include here; I shall therefore continue with another volume when my livelihood permits.
