Then, at a place called Kōzaki, all the Chinese disembark, and the memorial boat is drawn up to the shore, set alight, and burned entirely to ash. At this moment, each of the Chinese bows three times before the memorial boat before withdrawing. There are also two men known as "stick handlers" (bō-tsukai), who wear black garments and wrap black cloth around their heads. Keeping time with the beat of the metal gongs, they continue to wield their sticks in various ways until the memorial boat has finished burning.

## On the Chinese Practice of Slaughtering Living Creatures

The ancients said: "If one dwells long in a room of芝蘭 (orchids and thoroughworts), one ceases to notice their fragrance." In general, whether good or bad, that which is habitually practiced becomes one's nature. Compared to the people of our country, the Chinese seem at first glance to be of a rather timid and delicate temperament. When they see blood flowing from a person's body, they grimace and hurry away. And if they happen to witness the death of a close friend or someone they have grown familiar with, they grieve loudly with wailing and weeping.

Yet when it comes to killing chickens, pigs, or any living creature whatsoever, they treat it as casually as cutting vegetables. This is something the people of our country find distressing to witness, but the Chinese, on the contrary, bear it with perfect composure. Furthermore, when performing Buddhist services, the people of our country take special care to prohibit the taking of life. The Chinese are not so. Even in the course of conducting memorial services and offerings, they place live chicks and piglets aboard the memorial boat and burn them. When the fire and smoke reach the animals, the cries of their death screams are unbearable to hear — yet the Chinese remain perfectly calm. This is nothing other than the fact that habit has become their nature. The Master (Confucius) said: "In choosing a place to live, regard benevolence as the finest quality." One should always cultivate the habit of goodness, and must not, through familiarity, draw near to evil.
