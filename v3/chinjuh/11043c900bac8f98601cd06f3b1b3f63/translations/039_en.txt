## On the Ghosts of the Chinese Residents

The Chinese residents (Tōjin) are much given to talking about ghosts. Inside the Chinese Quarter (Tōjin-yashiki), there is even a ghost hall (Yūrei-dō). The ghosts prevalent in their homeland differ from the ghosts known in Japan, which are portrayed as lacking the lower half of their bodies. Chinese ghosts are complete in bodily form and even make the sound of footsteps. Moreover, even in broad daylight, people speak of encountering them: "So-and-so met one at that place," or "Such-and-such a person saw one here." Of course, none of this can be confirmed with certainty, yet since many people die in vain after crossing ten thousand leagues of the great sea, it is likely that their lingering grudges become ghosts.

Long ago, a learned and widely knowledgeable scholar came among the Chinese visitors. His name has been forgotten. The people of Nagasaki (崎陽, Kiyō) petitioned the authorities for permission to study under this scholar, and he was made to lodge at the home of a certain interpreter (tsūji). After several months had passed, the daughter of the neighboring house happened to die. The learned scholar, in his spare moments from his hired work, would peer through a gap in the window into the neighboring house every evening. When the master of the house found this suspicious and inquired about it, he replied, "I wish to see a Japanese ghost." The master explained that in Japan, ghosts do not appear so readily, to which the scholar seemed to find this quite astonishing. Considering this, one might wonder whether ghosts are truly abundant in China. It is indeed a curious matter.

## The Chinese Flowing Kanjō (Memorial Rite)

Since many of the Chinese who come to this country die here, the residents of the Quarter are prone to believing that ghosts wander within, and so memorial Buddhist services are held from time to time. For an ordinary kanjō (memorial rite), a boat of about one ken in length is constructed, and all manner of cargo and utensils are fashioned entirely from paper. Monks from the Chinese temple (Tōjin-dera) are invited into the Quarter to recite sutras, after which the boat is taken outside the Quarter to the seaside and burned. If the rite is to be performed with greater care and solemnity, two boats of four to five ken in length are built — one designated as a "departing boat" (de-fune) and one as an "arriving boat" (iri-fune). Those who died on a departing voyage are mourned with the departing boat, and those who died on an arriving voyage are mourned with the arriving boat. Everything that would be loaded onto a real ship — all cargo, utensils, and even pigs and chickens — is recreated without omission. As a result, the expense runs to as much as twenty or thirty kanme.
