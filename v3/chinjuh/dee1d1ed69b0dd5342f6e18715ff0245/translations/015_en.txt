The face was in no way different from that terrifying novice monk. "So this, too, is a monster!" he thought, and reached for the sword at his hip — but the horse was also a shape-shifting creature, and it reared up violently, throwing him headlong to the ground. "So I have been deceived after all," he thought, and his mind was thrown into confusion — or so it was later told.

Meanwhile, the servants who had been sent as a welcoming party went to check on the others who had been left behind, and found them collapsed and unconscious. They administered medicines and tended to them before returning home.

Afterwards, the affair became the subject of much gossip and speculation, and as the outcome reflected poorly on the samurai, he perhaps felt bitter about it all — for he submitted his resignation and disappeared, whereabouts unknown. This is a story that is by now widely known among people.

It is said that "the wise are not led astray, and the brave do not fear" — yet one who does not fear what ought to be feared deserves no praise. In general, the courage of a commander and the courage of a foot soldier are entirely different things in terms of what should be embraced and what discarded. What was shown here was nothing more than the courage of a common soldier — and what glory can there be in standing up to a monster? Even if one were to throw oneself at a tiger or plunge into a river and die…
