Upon inspecting the miscellaneous taxes, it was found that there were tens of thousands of kan of Eiraku Tsūhō copper coins. This matter was brought before the shogun of Kyoto, the monk Ashikaga Yoshimitsu. It was declared that since the Chinese ship had arrived at a port in the Kantō region, the coins should rightfully be the profit of the kanrei (deputy shogun), and accordingly all the coins were received as such. From this point on, the Eiraku coins circulated in the Kantō region for many years.

After that, around the Tenbun era (1532–1555), Eiraku coins were mixed with bita coins (inferior copper coins) and traded at the same value, causing merchants to despise the bita coins and disputes became rampant.

Thereupon, Yamagaku Shinano-no-kami Sadanobu and Kasahara Echizen-no-kami Moriyasu, retainers of Hōjō Ujiyasu, were ordered to issue directives to villages and hamlets throughout the region, stating: "Henceforth in the Kantō, only Eiraku coins shall be used, and no other coins shall be mixed into circulation." From that time on, the bita coins gradually fell out of use, flowing up to the capital, while only Eiraku coins remained in the Kantō. At that time, bita coins were called "Kyō-sen" (Kyoto coins) or "nobori-sen" (coins going up [to the capital]).

At the end of the Tenshō era (1573–1592), as the Hōjō clan declined and the Toyotomi clan came to power, other coins were again mixed into circulation; however, four mon of other coins were equated to one mon of Eiraku coins. Around that time, gold koban coins came into existence, and it became the customary practice to exchange one ryō of koban for one kan of Eiraku coins.

When exchanging Eiraku coins for koban, one mon was deducted for every hundred mon of Eiraku coins, and this deduction was called "kōsen" (commission fee). Converting this into bita coins: since one mon of Eiraku was worth four mon, one mon (four bita) was deducted, leaving 96 bita mon in circulation per hundred. This is said to be the origin of the term "96-hyaku" (96 per hundred).
