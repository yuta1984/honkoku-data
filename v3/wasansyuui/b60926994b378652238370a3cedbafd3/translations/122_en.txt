Upon inspecting the miscellaneous revenues, it was found that there were tens of thousands of kan of Eiraku Tsūhō copper coins. This matter was reported to the shogun in Kyoto, the lay monk Yoshimitsu. Declaring that "since the Tang ship has arrived in Kantō, the proceeds should rightfully belong to the kanrei (deputy shogun)," he received all of those coins. From this point onward, Eiraku coins circulated in the Kantō region for many years.

Later, around the Tenbun era, Eiraku coins were mixed with bita coins (inferior coins) and traded at the same value, causing merchants to despise the bita coins, leading to constant disputes and uproar.

Thereupon, Yamakado Shinano-no-kami Sadanobu and Kasahara Echizen-no-kami Moriyasu, retainers of Hōjō Ujiyasu, were ordered to issue a decree to villages and hamlets throughout the region: "Henceforth in Kantō, only Eiraku coins shall be used, and no other coins shall be mixed into circulation." From this point on, bita coins gradually fell out of use and drifted up to the capital, while Eiraku coins alone remained in Kantō. At the time, bita coins were called "Kyō-sen" (京銭, capital coins) or "nobori-sen" (上り銭, ascending coins). At the end of the Tenshō era, the Hōjō clan declined, and during the Toyotomi era, other coins came to be mixed back into circulation, with four coins of other currency being equated to one Eiraku coin. At this time, gold koban coins were introduced, and it became standard practice to exchange one ryō of koban for one kan of Eiraku coins. When exchanging Eiraku coins for koban, one coin per hundred Eiraku coins was deducted as a commission fee, called "kōsen." Converting this to bita coins, with the value of one Eiraku coin being four bita coins, the deducted amount was removed, and 96 bita coins were made to pass as the equivalent of 100. This is said to be the origin of the "96-per-hundred" (kurokuhyaku) system.
