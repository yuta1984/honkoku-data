The naming of one thousand *mon* as a *kan* (*貫*) likely comes from the meaning of the character itself. The character dictionary states: "Threading coins through [a string] is called *kan*." I would ask a person of wisdom and discernment to clarify whether this is correct or not.

(Question) Korenaga asks: "In our country, one *kanmon* of coins is sometimes called one hundred *hiki*. What is the reason for this? Please explain."

(Answer) In response: "This matter does not appear in national histories, nor have I heard from which text it originates. It appears to be simply a custom handed down in Japanese popular usage. There is a saying: 'When casting coins, one *komahiki* coin is cast; for every such-and-such number of coins cast, one coin goes to the *komahiki* [horse-leading fee].' The name is said to derive from this ratio." Whether the method of casting coins in Japan was indeed like this, I cannot say in detail, as I have never been involved in the official minting offices.

In my younger days, I once studied *kanrei* (the study of epistolary and ritual writing), and that text states: "Around the Kenkyū and Bunji eras [1190–1199; 1185–1190], the Right General, Lord Yoritomo, was frequently fond of hunting. His ordinance on field hunting (*denryō*) stated: 'Those who catch one animal shall be rewarded with ten *mon*, and those who catch one hundred animals shall be rewarded with one *kanmon*.' It is said that from this practice, the common people of that time came to call one *kan* 'one hundred *hiki*,' which was the parlance of that era." Whether this is truly the case, I cannot say with certainty.
