In the middle ages, a man called Heki Danjō invented this way and achieved great skill in archery. His tradition split into four schools: Yoshida, Sekka, Chikurin, and Sekidō. Those who shoot today all belong to these schools; the old school no longer exists. I surmise that perhaps the old school was not so precise in hitting the mark. The Heki school balances the bow hand (oshite) and releasing hand (katte), composes oneself properly, and releases with intention, firmly controlling the releasing hand. As a result, the arrow's flight is strong, and the shaft strikes deep—this should be double that of the old school.

In the spring of the eleventh year of Keichō (1606), a student of the Chikurin school named Asaoka Heibei Moritsugu began shooting chestnut-wood arrows in the tōshiya (distance archery) competition, and with merely fifty-one arrows passed through, he gained fame throughout the land. (These arrows were arrows with arrowheads attached.) From that time on, there were many who practiced this in successive generations. Shooting within the inner hall of the Rengeo-in (Sanjūsangen-dō) Temple's Kannon Hall, they were proclaimed "the best under heaven."

In the first year of Jōkyō, the year of Kinoe-Ne (1684), Wasa Daihachi, a disciple of Yoshimi Daemon of Kii Province, passed more than 8,100 arrows out of 11,000 total shots in the tōshiya (long-distance corridor archery), and thereby gained renown throughout the land. A hall was also constructed in the Eastern Capital (Edo), and people practiced this shooting in successive generations.

As for the equipment: in former times, the yugake (archer's glove) was made of leather alone, but now half of it is carved from horn and inserted, so that the fingers no longer hurt; and a method was devised of trimming the tip of the bow (hazu) to make the arrows spring off more effectively.
