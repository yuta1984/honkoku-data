After the Datong era, iron coins proliferated ever further and commodity prices soared. In the eastern regions, 80 coins were counted as one *mò* (hundred); in the western regions, 70 coins were counted as a hundred; and in the capital, 90 coins were counted as a hundred — this was called "long coins" (*chángqián*). Thereafter, an imperial edict was issued ordering the uniform use of *zú mò* (full hundreds), but the people did not comply. By the final years, it had come to the point that 35 coins were counted as a hundred.

After this, during the Sui dynasty and the height of the Tang dynasty, such practices were no longer heard of. However, when Emperor Xianzong came to power, the shortage of coins gave rise to the system of "flying money" (*fēiqián*). From the 4th year of the Yuanhe era (809), coins in circulation in the capital fell 20 short per *mǐn* (string of 1,000 coins). By the reign of Emperor Muzong, even when buying and selling gold and silver in the capital, one liang was added on top of every ten liang; and when buying rice and salt, 7 or 8 coins were added for every 100 coins. Thus the exchange in use both inside and outside the capital came ultimately to count 80 coins as a hundred. This was called *diàn mò* ("padded hundred").

After this, during the Later Tang of the Five Dynasties period, 80 coins were counted as a hundred. This was called *duǎn mò* ("short hundred") or *duǎn qián* ("short coins"). During the reign of Emperor Yindi of the Later Han dynasty, 80 coins were received but only 77 paid out. This was called *shěng mò* ("abbreviated hundred"). In the Song dynasty as well, at the founding of the dynasty, following the system of the Five Dynasties, 80 or 85 coins were counted as a hundred, and in some cases 48 coins were counted as a hundred. From the 5th year of the Qiande era of Emperor Taizu (967), 77 coins were counted as a hundred. The *shěng mò* is also called *chú bǎi* ("deducted hundred") or *zhé bǎi* ("discounted hundred"), it is said.

These matters are recorded in the *Tongdian* (Comprehensive Institutions) and in Shen Cunzhong's (Shen Kuo's) *Bitan* (*Mengxi Bitan* / Dream Pool Essays). As for the Jin, Yuan, Ming, and Qing dynasties, this author has not yet acquired knowledge of them.
