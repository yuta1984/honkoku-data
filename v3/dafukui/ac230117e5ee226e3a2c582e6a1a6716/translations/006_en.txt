【Right folio】
He had been instructed to report [what he had learned]. Thinking that if he fell ill along the way he would be delayed, he took two servants named Tasuke and Yasuké with him, and under the pretext of having urgent private official business in Kyoto, he departed Osaka late that same night. Along the way, he gradually revealed that he was in fact heading to [Fukui], and while hurrying along, at the time of the Imagiri crossing, word came that fire had broken out from the aforementioned Heihachirō's residence and that Osaka was in an uproar. Having no choice, he gave his servants a rough account of the man's [Sukenojirō's] intentions, and pressed on along the road in haste. However, due to flooding at the Ōigawa River, they were detained by a river closure, and finally arrived [in Fukui] last night.

【Left folio】
Having explained all of the above, [the report concludes that] there is no doubt that Sukenojirō acted entirely out of loyalty to the domain and did not fall into Heihachirō's scheme—there is nothing suspicious about his true intentions. However, since he is a person who disclosed such important intelligence, it cannot be said with certainty that there are no individuals who harbor resentment and might make accusations against him. Beyond this, there is concern for his safety, and while it would be most appropriate to place him in a detention house (ageya), if he were to fall ill and an important line of inquiry were lost as a result, not only would Sukenojirō's efforts be in vain, but Yamashiro-no-kami's worries would also come to nothing. Furthermore, given that Sukenojirō and his servants have shown themselves to be persons who understand the importance of such critical matters, it is difficult to order them to be placed in an ageya.
