(Continued from the previous page) ...upon inspection, it was reported to him that no matter how formidable the enemy forces—"even the most diabolical of opponents"—they would be impossible to overcome in a short time. The warriors from Tango and Tajima provinces had boarded over one hundred warships＃1 and came racing in by sea, but the ikki forces had laid in wait with bows and firearms, ready to engage the approaching enemy. Completely unmoved and firmly entrenched, they did not stir in the least. Since there was no means of breaking through—whether at the Nakakawauchi-guchi, the Kinome-guchi, or along the coastal shores—Lord Nobunaga too grew somewhat vexed and at a loss. As the attacking forces directed their firearms toward the enemy positions at each of the various passes, the ikki defenders shot back over the ridgelines and hilltops, resulting in a number of casualties and deaths among Nobunaga's own men.

[Left folio]
Lord Nobunaga, upon hearing of the state of the ikki forces, took with him some forty to fifty horsemen from his close attendants and page guards, and stealthily climbed Kinome-yama himself to survey the enemy's fortifications. Upon returning, he declared: "That such a paltry affair as this ikki should cause such a large force to spend so many days in the field, expending their energy and spirit—this is a great waste. We too shall construct fortified positions (tsukeshiro) and station troops within them to keep the enemy in check." Accordingly, fortifications were built within Kinome-yama, and Isono Tanba no Kami, Aobe Awaji no Kami, and Higuchi Saburōhyōe—these three commanders—were to take turns guarding the position in monthly rotations.
