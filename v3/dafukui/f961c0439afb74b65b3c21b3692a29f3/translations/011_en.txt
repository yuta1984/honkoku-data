(Continued from the previous page) ...upon reconnoitering, it was reported: "Even the most formidable of demon-kings (tennma hajun) could not break through in a short time." The warriors of Tango and Tajima Provinces boarded over a hundred warships and came rushing in from the sea, but the ikki forces had laid their bows and firearms in wait, readied themselves against the approaching enemy, and held their positions without yielding in the least. The passes at Nakakawauchi-guchi and Kinome-guchi, as well as the sea and beach approaches, were all alike—there was no means of breaking through—and Lord Nobunaga too was somewhat at a loss. The besieging forces arrayed at every pass were fired upon from the ridges and summits of the mountains, and a number of Nobunaga's men were wounded or killed.

[Left folio]
Lord Nobunaga, having heard of the state of the ikki, took his personal attendants of forty or fifty mounted men and secretly climbed up to Kinome-yama, where he personally surveyed the enemy's fortifications. Upon his return, he declared: "To spend so many days dispatching such a large number of troops against an ikki of so trifling a matter as this, and to expend their energy thus, is a great waste. We too shall construct siege forts (tsukishiro), station troops within them to contain the enemy." Accordingly, fortifications were constructed within Kinome-yama, and Isono Tanba no Kami, Aobe Awaji no Kami, and Higuchi Saburōbei—these three men—were to take turns garrisoning the position on a monthly rotation.
