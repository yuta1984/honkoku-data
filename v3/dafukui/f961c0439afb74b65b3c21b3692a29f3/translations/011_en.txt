(Continued from the previous page) ...he was informed that even the mightiest of demon forces (tenma hajun) would find it impossible to break through in a short time. The warriors of Tango and Tajima boarded over a hundred warships and came rushing in from the sea, but the defenders had positioned firearms (jūbō), bows, and arquebuses, waiting for the oncoming enemy, holding their positions without the slightest disorder. The Nakakawauchi-guchi, the Kinome-guchi, and the coastal approaches were all in the same state — there was no means of forcing an entry. Lord Nobunaga, too, was somewhat frustrated. The attacking forces positioned at each of the passes were being fired upon from over the ridgelines of the mountains, and a number of casualties — wounded and dead — occurred among his allies.

[Left folio]
Lord Nobunaga, having been informed of the state of the ikki, took with him forty or fifty mounted personal attendants (o-koshō), stealthily climbed Mount Kinome, inspected the enemy's fortifications for himself, and upon returning declared: "It is a great waste to have sent such a large number of troops for so many days and expended their energy on a matter as trifling as this ikki. We shall also construct siege outposts (tsukeshiro), garrison them with troops, and contain the enemy." Thereupon, he had fortifications constructed within Mount Kinome, and ordered three men — Isono Tanba no Kami, Aobe Awaji no Kami, and Higuchi Saburōbee — to take turns guarding the position, rotating on a monthly basis.
