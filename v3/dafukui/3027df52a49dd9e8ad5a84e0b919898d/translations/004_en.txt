　Among (Sōshuku-nyūdō's) relatives was Akechi Jūbee-no-jō Mitsuhide, who was also about to die in battle. However, the nyūdō (Sōshuku) admonished Mitsuhide, saying: "Mitsuhide is the legitimate grandchild of this house. I (Sōshuku) shall repay the grace of our fallen lord (by dying here), but you must revive our family name for the sake of our ancestors. Rely upon my son Yahei-ji Mitsuharu and my nephew Jirō Mitsutada." Mitsuhide accepted this reasoning, and accompanied by his kinsmen, departed from the castle, passed through Gujō District, then through a place in Echizen called Anama, and wandered through various provinces. Thereafter, he settled in Echizen and entered the service of the lord of that domain, Asakura Saemon-no-kami Yoshikage, from whom he received a domain assessed at five hundred kan.

The Eiroku-era Uprising in Kaga Province

　In the autumn of Eiroku 5 (1562), the village people (peasants) of Kaga Province raised an uprising and defied the orders of the lord of Echizen, Asakura Yoshikage. As Kaga, Noto, and Etchū had long been in a state of uprising, following the Ikkō-shū's head temple, the Osaka Honganji, and refusing to follow (Asakura's) governance, in Kōji 1 (1555), Echizen dispatched Kin'go Norikage (nyūdō name: Sōteki) as

[Left folio]
general, sending a force of tens of thousands of horsemen. After months of battle, (Asakura) came to control half of Kaga up to the Tedori River as a border, absorbing it into Echizen. Due to this upheaval, rice and grain could no longer be transported to Kyoto, causing hardship in the capital. Consulting the will of the Emperor, Shogun Yoshiteru dispatched Ōdate Saemon-no-suke and Takeda Jibu-no-shō bearing a shogunal letter (mikyōsho) to Echizen, where they mediated the dispute between Asakura and the Honganji. It was reported that a marriage alliance had been arranged between Yoshikage's daughter and Kyōnyo, the head of the Honganji, and both parties agreed to a reconciliation in accordance with the shogun's wishes. Accordingly, Kaga Province was returned to the Honganji. Thereafter, however, (troublemakers) in Kaga again failed to forward the rice tribute to Osaka for the Honganji, and behaved as they pleased. As a result, Kyōnyo's father Kennyo sent word to Asakura about the matter, who in turn sent word to Kaga — but they refused to comply. Moreover, they constructed fortifications at places called Kashiwano, Sugiyama, Kurahashi, and Sendai, blocked the roads, and seized goods by force. As a result, many people came to the lord (Asakura Yoshikage) with complaints and lamentations, and (Yoshikage) determined that the situation could not be left unaddressed.
