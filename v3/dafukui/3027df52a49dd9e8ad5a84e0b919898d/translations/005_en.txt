　　With Asakura Tosa-no-kami Kageharu, castellan of Kitanoshō, as commander, [the forces comprising] Shōrenga Ōmi-no-kami, Nojiri Shume-no-suke, Kurosaka Bichū-no-kami, Mizoe Ōi-no-suke, Buso Uneme, Fukamachi Zusho, Hosorogiki Satsuma-no-kami, and others — in total more than three thousand eight hundred horsemen — advanced into Kaga Province and encamped at Tsukitsu, Miyukizuka, Shō, Anata, and Shikichi. A messenger was sent to the ikki forces, declaring: "Since you have broken the agreement of prior years, we shall carry out a swift punitive expedition." In response, the ikki commander of Oyama in Kawakita district, a man called Tsubosaka Hōki, sent word saying: "We acknowledge that there has been a transgression, and after consultation among the people, we intend to comply. We ask that you postpone [your action] in the meantime." Though [the Asakura commanders] found this reply unreliable, they each maintained their positions firmly. At this time, the encampment of Shōrenga Ōmi-no-kami Kagemoto lay to the east of Miyukizuka, and a certain miasma [ki] was drifting southward. Akechi Jūbei Mitsuhide, who had been assigned to serve under Shōrenga [Kagemoto], observed this omen and reported to Kagemoto: "A vapor is rising to the east, and it bears the appearance of threatening the south." Accordingly, their defenses were further strengthened. And then,

[Left folio]
　　on the evening of the twentieth day of the ninth month, the ikki forces drew near. Shōrenga [Kagemoto], having prepared ditches and palisades in the fortified camp from the outset, awaited them. The ikki vanguard of more than two thousand men beat their drums and attacked from the south. With Akechi Jūbei and Yahei-no-ji [Mitsuharu] at their head, more than fifty skilled arquebusiers unleashed a relentless volley [tsurubeuchi], and although the ikki forces had heard the name of firearms, they encountered such a thing for the first time, and more than three hundred of their leading peasant soldiers were struck down. The ikki then pressed toward the eastern encampment, whereupon three mounted warriors who had been holding that flank — Magara Jūrō-zaemon Naotaka, his son Jūrō-tarō Takamoto, and the monk Zuiden-bō — rode out to meet them. The Magara father and son were at that time the peerless wielders of great swords [daitō] in all of Japan. The blade that Naotaka habitually favored had been fashioned — through deliberation among the swordsmiths of Echizen Province, known as Chiyotsuru, Arikunī, and Kanenori — to a length of seven shaku eight sun, and was called the "Tarō-tachi." Though four servants were required to carry this sword, Naotaka bore it with effortless ease.
