　　"This is the vapor that foretells battle. There must be no negligence," and so on. Ōmi-no-kami took Akechi with him and climbed a high hill to observe the martial omen, and was in the process of further strengthening his defenses when, on the evening of the twentieth day of the ninth month, the Ikko-ikki forces came pressing forward. The Shōrenga forces had already constructed trenches and palisades outside their encampment in anticipation, and Akechi Jūbei, with his comrade Yahei-ji at the vanguard, had over fifty skilled gunners fire in continuous volley. The Ikko-ikki forces had only ever heard the name "arquebus" but had never before encountered such a thing in battle, and were utterly routed. At that moment, Magara Jūrōzaemon Naotaka, his son Jūrotarō Takamoto, and Zuiden-bō—all men of prodigious strength and valor—sallied forth from the gates and threw the enemy into confusion. Akechi saw this at a glance and reported to the commander, "Now is the moment!" Ōmi-no-kami issued orders, and with Akatsuka, Yasuhara, Nosaka, Tatsuta, Kawasaki, and Isobe at the vanguard, they charged forward. The Ikko-ikki forces fled without offering any resistance; the pursuing force cut them down, taking over seven hundred and fifty heads. Defeated at the Battle of Gokōzuka, the Ikko-ikki forces surrendered to the commander-in-chief, Kitanoshō Tosa-no-kami Kagetaka, at the port of Tsukitsu,

[Left folio]
　　and submitted hostages to the lord (Yoshikage). After this, the various forces withdrew to Echizen, and rewards were distributed among those who had rendered meritorious service in Kaga. Magara and his son were granted an additional one thousand kan of lands in Konbokutō District. Akechi was praised for his discernment of the martial omen, his meritorious use of firearms, and his counsel in the deployment of forces, and received a letter of commendation from Yoshikage along with a horse of pale yellowish-grey (tsukige) coloring, presented to him saddled.

　　Magara and his son were men of matchless physical strength. The great swords they habitually preferred were made in consultation with swordsmiths of Echizen (Etchū-fu) known as Chiyotsuru, Arikuni, and Kanenori, and were fashioned to a length of seven shaku and eight sun. The sword was named "Tarō-tachi," and required four servants to carry it. His son Takamoto likewise had a sword of six shaku and five sun, named "Jirō-tachi," which he carried lightly with ease. Zuiden-bō used an oak staff of one jō and two shaku, reinforced with iron bands in a hexagonal pattern, with the grip end fashioned round, and so on.
