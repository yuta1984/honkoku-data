Before long, Oyamada Takake came forward, presented his horse to Yoshisada, fought and died in his place, and Yoshisada relied upon this to make his escape and return.

The Emperor Go-Daigo once again hastily took refuge on Mount Hiei, fleeing the leading rebel force. Takauji entered and occupied Tō-ji, and attacked from three directions. Yoshisada and his generals defended the eastern slope, and their trenches and stockades gradually took shape; but for the western slope, they relied upon its natural steepness, leaving only court nobles and monks to defend it. The enemy discovered this and attacked under cover of the dawn mist. For a time neither side pressed forward decisively, but the monks descended and struck, driving the enemy off. Several days later the enemy attacked again, and when the imperial army found itself at a disadvantage, they urgently rang the great lecture hall bell. Yoshisada led his troops up to Shimei-no-take and, together with Kōtsuna, struck down at the enemy from high ground; the enemy broke and fled, tumbling into the valleys and dying.

From this point, Yoshisada garrisoned the great ravine and conducted defensive fighting for more than ten days. They then first agreed that if an emergency arose on either the east or west slopes, the bell would be rung to notify one another. One morning, a troop of monkeys gathered and struck the bell, causing all the armies to believe the enemy had arrived, and they rushed together to the east and west slopes. The enemy, watching this from a distance, startled and fell into confusion among themselves. The imperial forces then descended and struck, routing the enemy, capturing the enemy commander Kō Moreshige, and beheading him.

About ten days later, hearing that enemy forces in the capital were few, they advanced to attack Tō-ji. But the enemy had hidden troops who rose up from all four directions, and our army suffered a great defeat. In autumn, the Senior Counselor Fujiwara no Morimoto led forces from the northern provinces to come to their relief, and the imperial army was reinvigorated. They then deliberated on attacking Tō-ji again, but at this point a traitor appeared and informed the enemy of their plan through a stratagem, so the enemy prepared accordingly. Our army, unaware of this, advanced — Morimoto from Kawara and Yoshisada from Uchino — but was defeated again. By this time repeated setbacks had mounted, and the Emperor, wishing to gratify the monks, granted many temple estates and villages to Mount Hiei. The monks, thus pleased, sent letters to Kōfuku-ji in Nanto (Nara) requesting reinforcement. As a result, forces from the Kinai region successively submitted their allegiance and requested to serve under the imperial banner. Fujiwara no Takasuke and others were therefore dispatched to lead them, with the purpose of cutting off the enemy's supply routes. The enemy fell into great distress.

Yoshisada then sent the forces of Awa and Awaji to light torches every night on Amida-no-mine to intimidate the enemy encampment, and together with Takasuke and the others set a date to attack the capital. When the appointed day arrived, Yoshisada led forty-three members of his clan into the palace to take their leave of the Emperor. The Emperor's expression softened as he offered them words of comfort and encouragement.
