(The various armies) collapsed and routed at dawn. Funada Yoshimasa, seeing that (Yoshisada's) main camp was empty and deserted, rode at speed to report to Yoshisada. Yoshisada, with barely a hundred men about him, fled to Owari. Masayoshi, Kikuchi Takeshige, and others arrived one after another to join him, and they fought a rearguard action as they withdrew, reaching Ukishima-ga-hara. Along the way, they encountered troops of the Kai Genji and struck them into submission. Gathering the scattered soldiers—more than seven thousand in all—they reached the Tenryū River, but it was in violent flood and could not be crossed. They therefore halted for three days to rest the troops and horses, dismantled civilian dwellings, and constructed a pontoon bridge to ferry the various armies across. Only after every unit had crossed did (Yoshisada) himself cross.

Someone suggested cutting the bridge to guard against an enemy assault. Yoshisada replied: "Even if we cut the bridge, the enemy will simply come and build another. That is a simple matter for them. If we cut the bridge in advance, we will only gain the reputation of fearing the enemy, and it will be of no benefit whatsoever." He ordered the ferryman to guard the bridge with care, not to damage it, and to await those who would come later. When Takauji's forces subsequently arrived and heard of this, they were all moved to admiration, saying that (Yoshisada) possessed the qualities of a true commander.

Upon returning to the post-town of Yahagi, Utsunomiya Kōtsuna said: "Defeats and routs are following one upon another. If enemies were to rise up on the road ahead and block our path, we would be trapped with no way forward or back—as if fallen into a valley. It would be better to withdraw and use the mountain strongholds of several eastern provinces to defend against them." The various commanders also feared that a sudden crisis might arise in the imperial court. They therefore withdrew to Owari.

At this time, troops throughout the various provinces had risen in revolt and rallied to Takauji, so the court urgently recalled Yoshisada to return and guard the capital. In the spring of the first year of Engen (1336), Takauji's great army bore down on Kyōto; (the capital) was thrown into chaos and the common people fled in confusion. Yoshisada deployed his generals to defend the strategic points, and himself proceeded to Ōwatari, where he constructed watchtowers atop the bridge, connected the bridge-girders while removing their planking, and taunted the enemy to lure them in. The enemy attempted to cross by lashing together rafts, but was impeded by obstructing materials (sunk in the water) and could not advance. Our army unleashed a continuous volley of arrows; the turbulent, surging water smashed the rafts, and five hundred men drowned. Enemy soldiers of exceptional valor climbed over the bridge-girders and reached the base of the tower, intending to pull down the pillars. The remaining troops took advantage of this and rushed forward to press in. Before long the bridge-girders gave way and collapsed; more than a thousand men fell into the water.

The next day, Yamazaki fell and could no longer be held, and the enemy entered the capital in full. Yoshisada rushed back and, attending upon the Emperor, escorted him to Mount Hiei. Takauji dispatched Hosokawa Jōzen to seize Miidera (Onjō-ji) as a base for a coming attack. Just then, Minamoto (Kitabatake) Akiie led troops from the Ōu region (Mutsu and Dewa) to come to their aid.
