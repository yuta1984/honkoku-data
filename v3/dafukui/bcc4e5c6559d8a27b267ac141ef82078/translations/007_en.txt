It happened that just at this time, the Prince's consort-attendant returned to the capital and made a confession, and furthermore the western domains (the Kyushu lords) presented several dozen copies of Takauji's proclamation calling for the raising of troops. At this, the Emperor was seized with fury and issued orders to attack Takauji. Prince Takanaga, Minister of the Imperial Household (Nakatsukasa-kyō), was appointed Governor-General of the Eastern Provinces, while Yoshisada was made Commander-in-Chief to command military affairs, leading a force of sixty-seven thousand troops along the Tōkaidō. The Danjō-in (Inspector-General) Prince Tadafusa and the Saemon-no-kami Fujiwara Sanesue advanced via the Tōsandō.

As Yoshisada prepared to depart, he donned his armor and had a farewell audience (with the Emperor). The ceremonial implements were immaculately arranged. According to the old regulations, when a Commander-in-Chief went on campaign, a formal ceremony of intermediate rank was held at which the commander's baton (節刀, *setsutō*) and the ceremonial bells (鐸鈴, *takurei*) were presented. However, when Taira no Koremori went on his eastern campaign during the Jishō era, only the bells were presented and the baton was not given (an ill-omened precedent). Taking heed of that inauspicious precedent, additional ceremonial gifts were prepared and presented (to Yoshisada).

Yoshisada, in accordance with the precedent set when Taira no Masamori suppressed Minamoto no Yoshichika during the Kaishō era, ordered Funada Yoshimasa to surround Takauji's mansion in Takakura. Three battle cries were raised, arrows were shot in pairs, and the mansion's gate-posts were hewn down, after which the army set out.

When (the army) reached the Yahagi River, tens of thousands of Takauji's troops had blocked the river crossing, arrayed in a defensive line. Wishing to lure the enemy into crossing the river and then strike them while they were fatigued, Yoshisada drew back his position away from the river. The enemy, as anticipated, crossed the river straight away and advanced. Yoshisada divided his forces and struck them, breaking through. As night fell, the enemy withdrew to Sagisaka. Utsunomiya Kōtsuna and others came and submitted (joined Yoshisada's army). Pressing the attack, Yoshisada's forces routed them.

Tadayoshi led a relief force of twenty thousand and took up positions at Tegoshi-kawara. Yoshisada ordered Yoshisuke (Wakiya Yoshisuke) to press upon the enemy encampment under cover of night and to open fire in a fusillade. The enemy was startled and collapsed in rout, withdrawing in their entirety to Kamakura. Yoshisada achieved a string of victories, with several tens of thousands surrendering and submitting to him. He advanced into Izu and remained at the provincial headquarters for several days, awaiting the arrival of the army from the Tōsandō route.

Takauji was hard-pressed and at a loss as to what to do; however, once Tadayoshi had gathered and reorganized the remnant forces, within the space of ten days their military strength was restored to vigor. Styling their force as three hundred thousand, they marched out to defend the strategic strongpoints at Takeshita and Hakone. Yoshisada fought against Tadayoshi at Hakone; taking advantage of the high ground and directing (his troops), his soldiers all fought with great valor and broke through. However, the army at Takeshita fared poorly, and the various forces were thrown into alarm and disarray (continues on the next page).
