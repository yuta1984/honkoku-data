At dawn, (Yoshisada's forces) set fire to Ōtsu and advanced. The vanguard failed to gain the advantage, and Jōzen, pressing his victory, came in pursuit. The fires were burning at their height, and moreover the naval forces on the lake kept up their fire, preventing any advance. Yoshisada unleashed his troops to strike (Jōzen's army) and pushed forward to close in on Miidera under its banners. Crack soldiers took the lead, broke through the palisade, set fire to the halls and shrines, and charging through the smoke, took seven thousand three hundred heads. Jōzen fled back to the capital. (Kitabatake) Akiie withdrew his troops and retired. Yoshisada alone pursued the enemy to Yamashina and routed them there as well.

He then entered the capital and divided his forces into three. One division was sent toward Shōgunzuka, one toward Shinnyo-dō, and one toward the Nijō riverbed.

Prior to this, Takauji had marched out with troops to aid Jōzen, and these forces happened to run directly into our army. Yoshisada climbed Mount Kachō and surveyed the enemy's great host, then addressed his men: "The enemy outnumbers us and we cannot match them in a straight fight. We must use surprise tactics to overcome them." He thereupon selected fifty seasoned veterans as a separate force, to which a further thousand were added, had them furl their banners and remove their identifying insignia, and sent them into the enemy ranks disguised as the routed survivors of Miidera, with the order: "Wait until the main armies are engaged, then strike." He then advanced to give battle. The enemy was numerous but their formation was disordered. Our army's advances and retreats were perfectly coordinated and uniform, and they gained the advantage in every engagement. Before long the hidden force suddenly rose up among Takauji's immediate retinue; the enemy fell into mutual suspicion, panicked, and routed. The various armies pursued them as far as the Katsura River.

That very night, Jōzen rushed forward to reconnoitre our army's condition and, finding our troops scattered, turned back and came to attack. Funada Yoshimasa and others were killed by the unruly soldiers. Yoshisada was defeated and withdrew to Sakamoto. Takauji re-entered the capital.

Before long, reinforcements arrived from the Higashiyama forces, and Yoshisada made plans together with his generals to advance along separate routes and attack the capital. The monks of Mount Hiei first seized Kaguraoka; Kusunoki Masashige defeated Ashikaga Takatsune (Moronao) at Izumo-ji; Minamoto (Kitabatake) Akiie fought Takauji at the Shijō riverbed; and Yoshisada led his troops in a flanking charge. The enemy, startled, turned and cried out: "The Nakaguro is upon us!"—and fled without giving battle. "Nakaguro" was Yoshisada's banner device. Yoshisada changed his appearance and mingled with ordinary soldiers, penetrating deep (into enemy lines) with the intent of targeting Takauji, but was unable to find him. As dusk fell, he withdrew his army.
