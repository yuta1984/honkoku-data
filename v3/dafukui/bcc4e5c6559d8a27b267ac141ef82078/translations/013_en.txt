"I shall fire at least one arrow into the enemy encampment, and dare not return alive." He divided his army into three columns. Yoshisada advanced through Ōmiya, fighting as he went; the enemy gave way at every point, and he finally reached Tō-ji. Halting his horse and glaring at Takauji's encampment, he called out in a loud voice: "The turmoil under heaven is owing to none other than the two of us. Rather than continuing to afflict the people and cause harm to the populace through this conflict, would it not be better to settle matters in single combat?" When he had finished speaking, he released an arrow that struck the watchtower and pierced a pillar of the enemy's encampment. Takauji wished to ride out and fight, but his subordinates restrained him.

Before long, the enemy converged from all four directions. Yoshisada broke through the encirclement and reached the Sanjō riverbank, but the enemy swarmed together again, and a flying arrow struck him in the forehead; blood flowed down to his helmet. Yet his fighting spirit grew only fiercer, and he wheeled his horse westward and continued fighting.

When the army had first set out, the Emperor had cut strips from the red hakama he himself was wearing and bestowed them upon his generals and soldiers. All were deeply moved, and they stitched them onto their armor as a distinguishing mark before setting out. At this point, those eight hundred soldiers all fought with all their might and rescued Yoshisada from layer upon layer of encirclement.

On this day, because Fujiwara no Takasuke had mistimed his advance and moved out too early, all the other armies were defeated.

From the time they had first accompanied the Emperor's御幸 to Mount Hiei, exactly four months had passed. Food and provisions for the entire army of two hundred thousand had all been drawn from the monks. The monks had exhausted their resources to supply them, but could no longer sustain the effort. Moreover, the enemy cut off the northeastern supply routes, and though the imperial army repeatedly went out to contest them, they could not prevail. Soldiers, starving and in desperate straits, deserted in a continuous stream.

In winter, the Emperor believed Takauji's false overtures of peace and was about to return to the palace. Fujiwara no Saneyō dispatched a messenger at full speed to inform Yoshisada. Yoshisada did not believe it, saying, "The messenger is just talking nonsense." Horiguchi Sadamitsu rushed over and saw for himself that the imperial palanquin was already prepared. Sadamitsu wept and remonstrated, pleading with the Emperor to remain; the Emperor, moved by this, was persuaded for the time being to stay.

Presently, Yoshisada, together with his younger kinsmen and his troops, was granted an audience. All harbored inward resentment, yet maintained proper decorum in their bearing. The Emperor addressed Yoshisada, Yoshisuke, and the others: "My initial thought was that since you and Takauji are of the same clan, you would surely side with his rebellion. Yet you alone severed ties with your own kinsmen and devoted your loyalty to the imperial house. This I deeply admire. I have always wished to rely upon you to sweep away the rebels and pacify the realm. But Heaven's fortune has gone against us; our army is exhausted and our provisions are spent. It is for this reason that I intend to seek a provisional truce with the enemy in order to prepare for the future. The reason I did not inform you before my departure was my fear that the matter might leak out."
