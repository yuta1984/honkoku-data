(Regarding the manner of writing bound volumes,) there is the style of Middle Counselor Sadayori (Fujiwara no Sadayori). In our household, we place one sheet (the first leaf) aside and begin writing from the middle (of the next sheet). This is the common style. As for Sadayori's style, I (the author) also find it to have merit in certain respects. However, the style to be adopted should ultimately be determined by the preference of the master (the writer or the patron).

Now, concerning the practice of copying and transmitting imperial poetry anthologies (chokusen-shū) without releasing them to outside parties — this is a matter of established precedent (kojitsu): there has been an official directive stating that "one should inquire of the relevant house (in cases of doubt)," and whenever questions of doubt have arisen, our house has been consulted on numerous occasions, and we have offered direct testimony.

When Minister of the Right Kan (Sugawara no Michizane) was making a fair copy of the Kokinshū, he came across poems marked "composer unknown" (yomibito shirazu). Thinking to himself, "What sort of person could this be? How regrettable," he nonetheless left the notation "yomibito shirazu" as it was, but appended a note stating "this is an error (ayamari)." This was truly a compassionate act,

— the feeling of which would be evident to any reader. It is said that both lords and their retainers were moved with admiration upon contemplating this act of consideration. Perhaps it was upon seeing this (annotation) that Tōfū (Ono no Tōfū) and Yukinari (Fujiwara no Yukinari) were inspired in their own writing. This matter does not serve as the mirror and model (kikagami) for our house per se, but I set it down here for the time being — it is, however, a matter that people keep confidential (and should not be divulged).

One: There is something called the "Method of Entering Wood" (nyūboku no hō). This refers to an achievement attained only through deep mastery and long practice.

The household retainers of Hōshōji-dono (Fujiwara no Tadamichi) summoned the author's great-grandfather, the Court Noble Sadanobu (Fujiwara no Sadanobu). Perhaps because Tadamichi believed his own handwriting to be quite accomplished, he had Sadanobu look at a sample of his writing and compare it (with Sadanobu's). At that point, Sadanobu — perhaps wishing to demonstrate the merit of his own hand — called for a square tray (oshiki) made of cypress wood (hinoki) and wrote upon it.
