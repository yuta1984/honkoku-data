From this time forth, when speaking of the degree to which the skill (of calligraphy) had penetrated deeply into wood, it has been transmitted as "the achievement of entering wood" (nyūboku no kō). In the writings of Lord Yukinari (Fujiwara no Yukinari) as well, it is precisely this matter upon which he himself offered personal testimony.

One: Concerning the manner of writing poetry. If it is a five-character verse (gogen), it should be written in two lines. However, this depends on the paper to be used. If the paper is tall (long in the vertical dimension), it should be written consecutively (in a continuous descending flow). If it is a seven-character verse (shichigen), four characters should be written on one line and three on the next. This is the standard method, and it is something people (of learning) know.

One: When copying by lamplight, one should write slightly smaller than one would during the daytime. (Writing by lamplight) tends to make the characters grow large.

One: The order and categories of formal correspondence (shōrei) — this is a matter of the utmost secrecy. It must be kept confidential.

What is issued from the direction of the Emperor is called a "Rinji" (論旨, Imperial Decree). What is issued from a Prince or the Crown Prince (Miya) is called a "Ryōji" (令旨, Princely Decree). What is issued from the Retired Emperor or Cloistered Emperor (In) is called an "Inzen" (院宣). What is issued from a Provincial Governor (kokushi) is called a "Kokusen" (国宣). What is issued from the Shogunal House is called a "Migyōsho" (御教書, Shogunal Letter of Directive). The Migyōsho carries a year-era name. What is called an "Onsho" (御書) refers to a private letter (on'uchisho, 御内書) written by the lord himself. What is called a "Hōsho" (奉書, Letter of Transmittal) is (a document) issued on behalf of a superior's wishes, from the hand of officials of the time…
