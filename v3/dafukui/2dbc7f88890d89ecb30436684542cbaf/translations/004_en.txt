One: On the manner of practicing calligraphy. First, one should look carefully at the model (tehon) and thoroughly grasp the meaning of each stroke and dot of the characters before practicing. One must not be overly eager to merely imitate the model. If one is too fixated on imitation, the characters will lose their vitality and will not become truly one's own. One should practice writing characters boldly and with an open spirit. If one holds the brush shaft (laid somewhat flat) and writes so that the right side of the characters is slightly elevated, the characters will appear somewhat transparent (i.e., they will have an airy, luminous quality). Conversely, if one writes with too much force, the characters will become ugly and appear unskilled. One should carefully study old calligraphic works and practice whichever model one finds most appealing.

As for this style — among those who practice it, there are said to be many throughout the world who can write it out with a certain effortless ease (shidokenaku) after about four attempts. Also, writing that merely resembles the model but has no soul (spirit) is called "dead characters" (shiji). Furthermore, writing in which one has departed from the old calligraphic traditions and one's own personal quirks have gone too far is called "broken characters" (haji). In one's youth, one should not practice only large characters. Nor should one be overly devoted to writing only small, fine characters. Likewise, one should not be exclusively devoted to writing only large characters. One should regularly practice and train in the styles and elegant qualities of large and small characters, formal (shin), semi-cursive (gyō), cursive (sō), and kana.

One: The imperial folding screens for the Daijō-e (Great Thanksgiving Ceremony) are a matter of the utmost importance. They are divided into left and right, called Yuki and Suki respectively, comprising six folios (chō) of five-shaku size [with marginal notation] and six folios of four-shaku size [with marginal notation], totaling twenty-four folios on front and back. On the five-shaku screens, the main text (Chinese verse) is composed and written; on the four-shaku screens, the kana (Japanese verse) is written. This is the role of the Hakase (learned scholar-calligrapher).
