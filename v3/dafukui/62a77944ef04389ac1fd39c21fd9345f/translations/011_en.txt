Akamatsu Kazusa-no-suke Yoshinori did not retreat a single step, holding his position before Nijō-Inokuma and the Iwagami shrine. At this juncture, Yamana Kunai-shōyū Tokihiro declared: "Since this battle originated from His Lordship's patronage of us, we cannot afford to give less effort than the other forces," and — rather than engaging the lesser enemies — he resolved to charge directly into the ranks of his uncle Mutsu-no-kami, and if an opening arose, to grapple Ujikiyo to the ground and earn the lord's commendation. With his force of fifty-three horsemen, he sallied forth from Reizei Ōmiya, planted a single banner bearing the three-lined-lozenge (mihikiryō) crest within the torii gate of the Iwagami shrine, and waited in readiness for the approach of Mutsu-no-kami's troops. When Mutsu-no-kami's soldiers, having been locked in combat with the Akamatsu forces, separated to both sides and were resting their men and horses, Tokihiro charged out toward Nijō Ōmiya, plunged into the midst of the five hundred-plus horsemen, threw them into a brief melee, and then dashed back out toward Nijō — of his fifty-three horsemen, only nine, master and retainers together, remained. Mutsu-no-kami, seeing this, called out: "That banner with the three-lined-lozenge crest bearing a cicada-base

with bamboo leaves attached — that must be Kunai-shōyū! Take him, every last horseman!" He had his eyes fixed on Kunai-shōyū and surrounded him within a ring of eight horsemen; it appeared as though he was about to be cut down — but at that moment, Kakiya Danjō and a certain Naめreyoshi charged in to block the way and fell in battle, and in that opening, Kunai-shōyū escaped the peril of that desperate pass, rode to rejoin the forces at the Jingikan encampment, and after the battle had dispersed, went to the encampment at the palace and reported the full account of the fighting at Ōmiya.

○ Meanwhile, as Ōuchi Sakyō-no-daibu was requesting reinforcements from the side of Akamatsu Kazusa-no-suke, Isshiki Sakyō-no-daibu Akinori declared his intention to "join the battle," whereupon the Shogun's expression brightened with satisfaction, and he issued orders to the effect that Isshiki should "hasten at once and observe how matters stand, and that Jibu-no-taifu should also be sent." Sakyō-no-daibu then rode eastward along Nijō with his force of more than three hundred horsemen.

○ Kageyukōji Jibu-no-taifu Yoshishige also followed with more than five hundred horsemen.
