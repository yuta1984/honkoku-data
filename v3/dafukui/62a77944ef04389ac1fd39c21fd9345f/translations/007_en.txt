(Continuing from the previous folio) ...Seeing this, the soldiers recovered their spirits somewhat. Shōya Jōkei Nyūdō rode up to him and said harshly, grinding his teeth: "You undertook so great an enterprise, yet you missed the agreed hour for the signal — where in the world have you been?" Mitsuyuki replied: "And that is precisely why I say that my fortune is spent. Such a strange thing has occurred: around midnight I left the encampment and let my horse take me along, thinking I was heading down into the eastern valley — but I was in fact going southward. I passed through the area of Kawashima and came out at Tambaguchi, and when dawn broke faintly and I looked around, I found myself at the pass in the middle of Ōeyama. I thought, 'What on earth has happened? Who was guiding the way?' and when I asked, they told me: 'A warrior in hi-odoshi armor riding a grey horse called out "The Katsuragawa is this way, this way!" and led the way at the very front, but at the moment dawn broke, he vanished without a trace.' From there I turned back, and it was at the Katsuragawa that I first heard the battle in Kyōto had begun." Shōya Nyūdō said: "And that is precisely why I say your fortune is spent. Word has come that our side has been defeated at the Battle of Ōmiya, and that Kazusa-no-suke and Kobayashi have been killed." At that, (they resolved): "Then dividing our forces among the various directions is futile. Let us join into a single force, press upon the end of Nijō, and die in battle." Thus they decided upon the Ichijō approach. The Tsuchiya party also joined together as one.

**[Left folio]**

〇 Now, on the evening of the twenty-ninth day, the members of the Tsuchiya clan had gathered together and spoken among themselves. Heiji Saemon-no-jō said: "There is not one chance in a thousand that we can win this battle. Let all of this clan die in tomorrow's battle — only then can the resolve of true warriors be left on the lips of men for posterity." They all made a pact, declaring: "Let it be known to all that so many tens of men of the Tsuchiya party died in battle," so as to spread their fame and pass it down to their descendants. Everyone agreed this was the right course, and so all fifty-three members of the Tsuchiya party uniformly attached finger-guards to their left hands and bound their ring fingers with crimson thread.
