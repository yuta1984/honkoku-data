"So this is the end," Kojirō said, throwing his sword down from horseback, drawing his short blade, and clinging to Ōshū's body, about to cut open his belly — when one of Sakyō-no-daibu's warriors, a man called Kawasaki Tachi-no-tachi, threw himself on top of Kojirō, tore off his helmet and cast it aside, thrust his blade twice through the gap where the cheek-guard had come loose, and then seized Kojirō's hair and pulled his face upward to look at him. There before him was a young warrior of about sixteen years — strikingly beautiful and of an elegant, refined appearance. Not knowing where he could bring himself to strike, the warrior said, "By any means I would like to save you. Please tell me your name." Kojirō replied, "Even if I were to give my name, no one would know me. Simply take my head and, when presenting it alongside those who died in battle in the service of the general, ask those around you, saying, 'Whose head is this?' Perhaps there will be someone who recognizes it." With that, Kojirō recited the nembutsu, and after that spoke no more. The warrior thought, "Ah, how I wish I could save this person!" — yet he had already thrust his blade twice, and even were he to save Kojirō, there was no way Kojirō could escape. Feeling as though a flowering branch had snapped and its blossoms were scattering before the storm could even arrive, he cut off Kojirō's head. When he later inquired of others, he learned that this was none other than Tatsufusa-dono —

**[Left folio]**

— a man who, though Ōshū's nephew by blood, had been taken as his adopted son (*yūshi*) and cherished with deep affection throughout all these years — and so the grief was all the more overwhelming.

Yet Ōshū had possessed more than forty children, and though he had resolved upon this great undertaking and gone to his death in battle, not a single one of those children had accompanied him to the end. Only this one Kojirō had died with him, and his name remained on the lips of ten thousand in praise and lamentation alike. He had lived but sixteen springs and autumns — still so young — yet he understood the principles of the bow and arrow and in the end went forth to meet his death; how noble and admirable was that spirit. The path of filial devotion, it seems, is not determined by whether one is a blood son or an adopted son — and at this everyone shed tears of deep emotion.

Moreover, as Ōshū had been the *shugo* (military governor) of four provinces, there must have been some twenty thousand retainers and family dependants who owed him their allegiance. Yet when the battle turned desperate, each and every one fled in their own direction, and at the very spot where Ujikiyo fell in battle, there were no more than ten-odd men remaining. How utterly shameful a thing it was. While watching their lord — to whom generations of their families owed deep and abiding obligation — die in battle right before their eyes, they cast aside the armor, *haramaki* (belly armor), swords, and daggers they had adorned with inlaid gold and silver, fled into temples in the capital and its outskirts in groups of five or ten, and shaved their heads — their hearts not yet even moved to true religious awakening —
