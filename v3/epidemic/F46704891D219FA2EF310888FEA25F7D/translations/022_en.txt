No. 8

"What is there in this world to dig for?" — In Horie 3-chōme (the "hori" of "digging"), there lived one Yamakawa Tasuke, upon whose house sorrows piled up like a mountain (*yama*). His wife Setsu, driven — as her very name suggests — by feelings that were *setsu* (agonizing), grew weary of the world, and on the twenty‑eighth day of the fourth month last she set out to visit an acquaintance who lived on the bank of the Uji River ("Uji" echoing *ushi*, "bitter, wearisome").

Just then the rains had fallen for days on end and the river was swollen. Gazing out over the surface of the Uji, she thought: in this floating world, which the Buddha himself preaches to be but foam, a dream, a phantom, how long should one go on living? And so, all hope cut off, sad as the water itself, she threw herself into the Uji River. For what reason no one knew — like the "white waves" of the stream — she was seen bobbing and sinking in the current, when by good fortune a man named Horiguchi Jisuke, of the Fourth Ward of Fushimi, happened to pass by. Astonished at the sight, he leapt at once into the Uji, caught hold of the woman as she was being swept along, and with great difficulty dragged her out; but by then she had already stopped breathing. Seeing, however, that there was still a faint warmth about the pit of her stomach, he built a fire, warmed her, and tended her by every means he could think of — and, her allotted span not yet being spent, she at last revived.

He then inquired where she lived and reported the matter immediately to the Kyoto Prefectural Office, which communicated with the Osaka Prefectural Office, and the woman was handed back to her husband. Horiguchi Jisuke, for having risked his own life to save the woman — conduct deemed admirable — received a commendation from the Kyoto Prefecture together with a grant of seventy‑five *sen*.
