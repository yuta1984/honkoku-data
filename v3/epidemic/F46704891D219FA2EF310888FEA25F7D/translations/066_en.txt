Meiji 8 (1875). Nishiki-e Shinbun (Colour-print News). Published by Ishiwa.

On the night of the sixth day of the third month, in the middle of Yodo no Kobashi bridge, the clothes of a man and a woman were found cast off, with a single farewell letter laid on top of them. It looked for all the world like a love suicide, yet the two themselves were nowhere to be seen. Wondering what could have happened, we inquired into the origins of the affair, and learned the following:

The woman was Wakana of the teahouse Yamaasa-uchi in Kamishichiken, in Saikyō (Kyoto) — a courtesan of striking beauty. The playboy who had set his heart on being united with her was a man who had lavished "several thousand blossoms" (i.e., money) upon her in his visits, a poultry dealer of Sasai-chō, close by Kitano. Wakana, a "bird in a cage," and a bird-seller: a "crossbred fowl" match of well-suited temperaments — or was it an ill-starred bond? Each "liked" (suki) the other — like a "suki-nabe" (sukiyaki pot) — and they burned with longing until at last their plans, too, boiled dry; with nowhere left to turn, they sank themselves in the water and left only their names afloat as gossip. Far too shallow a folly, people say — and we have here drawn the rumour just as it is told.

Not only did they expose (sarasu) their bodies to the water — they exposed their names as well, and their conduct too, so people will surely say.

Recorded by Yoshitaki.
