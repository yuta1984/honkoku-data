To an inn called Tsujimotoya in Ichiba village, Sano, in Senshū (Izumi Province), a small tradesman of Sakai Prefecture named Matsubei came to stay one night in mid-third-month of Meiji 8 (1875). There he was so struck by the good looks of the maidservant O-Ume that a single lustful thought at once sprang up in him. Like the sedge (suge) of a sedge hat, she seemed at first cold (sugenai); yet like the cords of leggings she proved easy enough to untie; and like the sole of a foot-cover, the true bottom of her heart remained unknown to him — until, with a single two-shu note, she at last "folded," and the two tied the bond of soiled sleeves.

But the inn was cramped and crowded with many lodgers, so as a token they swore that O-Ume's hairpin should be stuck in Matsubei's hair, and that by this sign she would find him and steal to his side in the dark. Unluckily, a fellow lodger named Chōbei overheard the arrangement. When Matsubei, worn out by the road, fell into a deep traveller's sleep, Chōbei hit upon a scheme: he moved the hairpin into his own hair and waited, all fidgets. Presently came O-Ume's footsteps — he caught her in the darkness, and for a while they exchanged sweet words.

At length, about the time the dawn cock crowed, Matsubei awoke to find the hairpin of the evening's promise gone. He called O-Ume and questioned her, and only then did it emerge that the arrow of love had missed its mark. O-Ume, bristling with fury, railed at him; the affair ended in a shouting quarrel, a one-night dispute in which neither side could be proved — truly a curious tale, and one to laugh at without end.
