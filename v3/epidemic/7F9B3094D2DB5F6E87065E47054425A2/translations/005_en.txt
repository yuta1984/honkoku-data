…If (fetal toxin and blood impurity) were latently concealed without manifesting, then by what means would they provoke illness and cause suffering (this question necessarily arises)? Furthermore, even in high antiquity, it is inconceivable that the "fire of desire" of parents was entirely absent, nor that a fetus could fail to receive the impurity of the mother's blood. To account for this, a theory has been constructed which states: "The reason smallpox did not exist in high antiquity is that people's nature was then pure and simple; the reason smallpox exists in the middle ages is that desires and passions ran unchecked." However, it cannot be said that the people of high antiquity were without exception all pure and simple, nor that the people of the middle ages were without exception all given over to unchecked desire. Having already designated these as the source of "residual toxin" (*idoku*), various prescriptions such as *shōsei*, *rensha*, *tokketsu* (*rabbit blood*), and *kidō* ("thinning the pox") are devised, yet not one in a hundred proves effective. All of these are forced and far-fetched theories and do not constitute a proper general account. Allow me to attempt to argue this point. Consider a case today where children are born of the same mother, sharing the same fetal membrane — that is, twins.

[Left folio]

When such persons encounter an epidemic (*tenkō*) and come to develop the pox (*shutō*), one may have a mild course with no ill effects, while the other takes a severe turn and dies. Furthermore, when an epidemic is in progress, it may happen that one village experiences uniformly mild and easy cases, while another village experiences uniformly severe and dangerous ones. How could it be that the fire of desire and blood impurity are more abundant in one place and less in another?

Above all, one must understand that this disease originally arose as a kind of noxious pestilential *qi* (*akurei no chinki*) — a rare and strange *qi* — endemic to a remote land in a foreign country, which spread through contagion as merchants' ships and fishermen came into contact with one another, gradually multiplying and spreading until it settled and became a single transmissible disease. It is disseminated according to the seasonal *qi* (*jiki*), and its severity — whether mild or grave, whether it runs a favorable or fatal course — varies according to the severity of the epidemic at a given time, the strength or weakness of an individual's inborn constitution (*hinshitsu*), and the presence or absence of other illness. Because it comes from without, by being cautious and avoiding it…
