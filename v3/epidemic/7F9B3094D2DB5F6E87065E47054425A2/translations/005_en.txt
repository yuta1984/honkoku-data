…If [fetal toxin and blood impurity] were latently concealed and did not manifest, by what means would they provoke disease and cause illness? Moreover, even in high antiquity, there is no reason why parents should have been free of the fire of desire, nor any reason why a fetus should not have received the impurity of the mother's blood. In response to this, proponents of the traditional theory put forward the argument: "The reason there was no smallpox in high antiquity is that people's natures were pure and simple; the reason smallpox exists in the middle ages is that carnal desires became unbridled." However, it cannot be said that people of high antiquity were necessarily all pure and simple, nor can it be said that people of the middle ages were necessarily all given over to unbridled desires. Having already identified this as a "transmitted toxin" (*idoku*), they accordingly devise prescriptions such as *shōsai* (cauterizing the navel), *rensa* (refined cinnabar), *tokketsu* (rabbit's blood), and *kitō* (smallpox-thinning formulas)—yet not one in a hundred of these proves effective. All of this amounts to nothing more than forced and contrived reasoning, and does not constitute a universal theory. Allow me now to attempt a discussion of this. Consider those who share the same mother, the same placenta, and are born together [i.e., twins]:

[Left folio]

when they encounter an epidemic (*tenkō*) and develop smallpox, one may proceed smoothly and recover without harm, while the other may take a severe turn and die. Furthermore, during an outbreak, it may happen that in one village everyone's case is mild and smooth, while in another village everyone's case is severe and dangerous. Can it really be that the fire of desire and blood impurity were more plentiful in one case and scarcer in the other? What is particularly little understood is that this disease originated as a kind of malignant pestilential *qi* (*akurei no reiki*) from a remote foreign land, transmitted through the intercourse of merchant vessels and fishermen, gradually spreading and proliferating until it settled and became a distinct contagious disease. It circulates according to the prevailing seasonal *qi*, and its severity varies according to the conditions of the season, according to the strength or weakness of one's innate constitution (*hinshitsu*), and according to whether or not one is already afflicted with other illnesses. Because this disease comes from without, one should prudently seek to avoid it…
