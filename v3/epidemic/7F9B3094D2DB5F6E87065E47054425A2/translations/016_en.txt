"What I bring is likewise the same. We consider this a matter of deep regret." Mon-shi [the Dutch physician] said: "Even if one collects the vaccine fluid, seals it tightly to keep out the outside air, and prevents its potency from dissipating, the sea voyage already takes thirty days [*sanjun*, three ten-day periods], and amid the bustle and confusion before and after, another twenty days [*nijun*] pass; and after arriving at the trading post [*tōkan*, Dejima], one cannot but spend another fifty days [*gojun*] before the day comes when one can actually attend to work. For this reason, the fluid becomes stale and degraded, and it is only natural that it should have no efficacy in producing growth [lit. 'germination']. [Haraguchi then] said: "In the old method, there are those who use dried scabs [*tōka*] as the inoculation material. Even after several months have passed, it is still capable of being received [by the body] and producing its effect. What if cowpox were likewise administered using this method?" Mon-shi found this to be entirely correct, and so this year — the seventh month of Kaei 2, the Year of the Earth Rooster [己酉, 1849] — he brought cultivated cowpox scabs [*gyūtōka*] to Japan for the first time. When I [the narrator] observed the inoculation being performed on three children, first to the left arm...

[Left folio]

...a vesicant plaster [*happōkō*] was applied; the blister skin was peeled away; five or six pieces of cowpox scab [*tōka*] were taken, laid on the skin, and covered with an adhesive plaster [*kōkō*] spread on leather, then bound with a bandage. Next, using an inoculation lancet [*shūtōshin*], the right arm was pierced in six or seven places; pieces of cowpox scab were held over the steam of hot water to soften them, and once they had become pliable, they were placed into the puncture marks; a hard plaster and bandage were applied to the right arm in the same manner as the left. On the third day, the dressings were removed: on the left arm, not a single pustule had developed; on the right arm, clear inoculation marks were visible, identical in appearance to those of natural smallpox. By the sixth day, the vesicles had filled with fluid [*kanshō*], and the signs of vigorous life were brimming over. Success was thus declared. The other two children showed no effect whatsoever.
