…[a disease] from which none can escape. It is for this reason that variolation (*shussetsu* / inoculation) is administered in advance, to extinguish one's susceptibility and ensure that one is forever free from the disaster of smallpox. In the *Gyūtō Shinsho* ("New Writings on Cowpox"), quoting from the writings of Haukholz [*Hōkuhorusu*] (a personal name): "This disease poison possesses a certain power. Once one is infected by it and develops a fever throughout the entire body, the innate susceptibility (*hinsho*) to that disease is extinguished. Therefore, it should be known that once that susceptibility has departed, one will not suffer from that disease henceforth."

**Question:** If smallpox can be avoided so as to escape its disaster, why is it at all necessary to use inoculation?

**Answer:** Smallpox can indeed, in principle, be avoided and escaped. That is to say, in Kii Province — Kumano; in Suō Province — Iwakuni; in Iyo Province — Tsuyunomine; in Tosa Province — Betsueda; in Shinano Province —

[Left folio]

Akiyama; and also Ontake in Kiso; Shirakawa in Hida; the Iwamura domain in Mino Province; the Tsumari estate in Echigo Province; Ōmura and the Gotō Islands in Hizen Province; Amakusa in Higo Province; Shimoo Hakkamura in Mimasaka Province; Hachijōjima in Izu; and other such places — these have been free of smallpox since ancient times. This is not because of any special art or technique; rather, those who are ill with smallpox are not permitted to enter those localities, and one does not pass through places where it is epidemic. Those who happen to be infected by its poison through chance contact — called *tobibōsō* ("flying/traveling smallpox") — are moved into the mountains and valleys, and others who have already recovered from smallpox are made to guard and attend them; only after they have fully recovered and their scabs have fallen off are they permitted to return home. However, medical treatment and recuperative care are frequently neglected and inadequate, and many die. This is also not a path of compassionate care.

Moreover, women and girls who remain at home and do not go out are able to be entirely preserved [from the disaster]…
