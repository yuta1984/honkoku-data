**[Right folio]**

Both bowel movements and urination should be regular, the patient should be in good spirits, and eating and drinking should be progressing well — these are considered favorable signs.
On the third or fourth day, when the eruptions have spread all the way to the soles of the feet, this is called "desoi" (complete eruption).

**Kichō (rising and swelling stage) — days four, five, and six.** The sores swell up vigorously, shining in a peach-blossom color (light pink), the face gradually becomes puffy, the eyelids swell so that the eyes can barely be opened. When one strokes the sores with the hand, the skin is thick and no fluid leaks, as if feeling a smooth bead, with a lustrous sheen. Sleeping well, eating well, a clear voice, firm stools, and regular urination are all considered favorable signs.

**Kannō (suppuration stage) — days seven, eight, and nine.** There is a mild fever with some thirst; the sores are entirely filled with pus and emit a smell of pus. The face is fully swollen, the eyes are sealed shut, and the nose is blocked — these are considered favorable signs. During this period, constipation (no bowel movement) is considered favorable; even a slight bowel movement is considered unfavorable. By the ninth day, the beginning of scab formation (靨) spreading from around the mouth to both jaws is considered auspicious.

**[Left folio]**

**Shūen (crusting/scabbing stage) — days ten, eleven, and twelve.** The pus on the face gradually dries up; the patient sleeps well, bowel movements return to normal, urination becomes frequent, the swelling of the face gradually subsides, and the eyes begin to open — these are considered favorable signs.

**Rakka (falling of scabs stage) — days thirteen, fourteen, and fifteen.** The scabs gradually fall away, the remaining marks are a pale reddish color, the patient sleeps well, and eating and drinking progress well — these are considered favorable signs.

In general, what is recorded above describes cases where symptoms follow a favorable course; anything contrary to this should be recognized as a dangerous or adverse condition.

○ Once it has become known that the illness is indeed smallpox, the patient should be dressed in clean clothing free of grime. Even for persons of high rank, the undergarment should be made of red-dyed pongee silk (beni tsumugi) or red cotton (beni momen) of that kind. With silk fabrics, the pus adheres stickily, and each time the patient moves, the scabs of the sores are pulled away together with the fabric, causing pus and blood to flow and inflicting pain.
