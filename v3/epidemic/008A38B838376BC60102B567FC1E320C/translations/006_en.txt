One should take great care in nursing the patient. Even if fever and convulsions occur, one must not recklessly use strongly scented medicines. Once a fever has started, parents and caregivers should not leave the patient's side. Those with strong fevers are prone to convulsions. At such times, one should quickly hold and firmly embrace the child. In severe cases, dizziness and loss of consciousness may occur, leaving the patient unaware of their surroundings. In such situations, one should dissolve high-quality bear bile in water and administer it. One must not recklessly use various kinds of restorative medicines. Furthermore, massage, acupuncture, and moxibustion should be strictly forbidden.

○ Although there are numerous methods said to prevent smallpox in advance, when tried, they are found to have no effect and may even cause harm. One must not use them recklessly.

○ Smallpox generally takes twelve days as its standard course, which is further divided into four stages. The first three days are called "kenken" (appearance of spots), the next three days are called "kichō" (rising and swelling), and the next

**[Left folio]**

three days are called "kannō" (suppuration/filling with pus), and the final three days are called "shūen" (crusting/scabbing). However, before the kenken stage there is an initial fever, which may be long or short, with no fixed number of days. After shūen, there are three days of "rakka" (falling of scabs). The period from kenken to rakka, spanning fifteen days, constitutes the fixed course of smallpox.

During the initial fever (jōnetsu), there may be high fever or mild fever, and various symptoms such as vomiting, convulsions, delirium, and abdominal pain occur, resembling indigestion, the common cold, or fright convulsions, making it difficult to definitively identify smallpox. The ancients called this the "doubtful condition" (giji no shō). Furthermore, the prodromal fever may last only one day before the rash appears, or three to four days, or even several days before eruption. It is considered favorable when the fever subsides after about three days, the patient recovers their vitality, eating and drinking improve, the previous symptoms resolve, and the kenken stage follows.
