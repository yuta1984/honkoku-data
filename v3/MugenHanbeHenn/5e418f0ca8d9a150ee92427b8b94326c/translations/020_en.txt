Furthermore, in a steep and narrow mountain pass where the mountains rise high above and the ravines plunge a hundred jō below, in such terrifying terrain where orders cannot be properly conveyed, where progress along the road is slow, where it is impossible to know where ambushers may be lurking, and where there is no means of sending out lookouts—in such cases, one should rouse all the troops together, beat the bells and drums to urge them on, and advance with great battle cries of "Ei! Ei!" The echoes resounding off the mountains will create a tremendous clamor, and with such an overwhelming roar, any hidden ambushers will be so frightened that they will not dare to emerge. Taking advantage of that commotion and noise, one should pass through the difficult terrain before anyone notices.

《Section Twenty-One》 On Strong Winds

▲ When the wind is blowing at a tolerable level, the horo (cape-like ornamental gear worn on the back of mounted warriors) and personal battle standards (sashimono) should be folded up, and the flags should be furled so that only the poles remain, and the army should advance in this manner. When the wind is blowing strongly, it is most advisable to quickly find suitable ground to halt the troops and establish a firm encampment. Regarding sudden rainfall: even in a light rain, it is not good for equipment and weapons to become wet. In heavy rain, one should quickly assess the terrain and it is most advisable to set up a firm encampment in a place that will not be flooded. In great wind and heavy rain, signal fires (kagari) are difficult to keep burning, and sounds are hard to hear. Therefore, vigilance against night raids is of the utmost importance.
