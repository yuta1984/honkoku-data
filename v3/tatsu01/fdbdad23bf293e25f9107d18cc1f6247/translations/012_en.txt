...and withdrew all the way to Akita. Meanwhile, the Tōzawa forces searched for the enemy but did not know that Gensai had fallen into a deep, grass-filled ravine, and presumed that his body had been taken away. Without parting the grass at the bottom of the valley to search, they all withdrew and returned to camp. Gensai lay in the ravine as if dead, but had rolled to one side and lay hidden in the darkness. After the enemy had withdrawn, his breath returned, and when he tried to rise, he feared his head would hang forward. So, while still lying down, he stripped off his armor, bound the front sash around his back sash, and, reduced to bare skin, made his way back to his allies. He received treatment, ultimately recovered, and it is said that afterward he participated in battle many more times — and so the account goes.

It is further said: descending the slope called Aigaizaka (合貝坂) in Kawabe District — *[Note: in former times, this was the place where allied troops blew conch-shell signals to each other during battle. It is located on Mt. Funaoka. Today it is called "Awasekai" (合せ貝). The name is also applied to a field and a slope.]* — one reaches a short earthen bridge. The water of the marsh to the left (the bow-hand side) is called "Karinagasawa" — *[Note: also written as "Kariyasawa." There is a village called Kariya in Mikawa Province and a Kariyabara in Shinano Province; all are said to have originally been camp sites where temporary shelters were built.]* In ancient times, brushwood was cut and bundled there to construct military encampments, and barrier stations (*sekiya*) and the like were built there — hence the name "Karishiba" (苅柴). Some local people also transmit the place name in a corrupted form as "Kanezawa" (金澤). **Tsuchide Manor (土出荘).** The village name "Sakai" (境, "Border/Boundary"), as stated earlier, was perhaps first applied to this side of things because the Karishiba Barrier was established here. Also, just as the Yodo River takes its name (upper, middle, lower) from the river itself, "Tsuchide" (都知伊傳) — in the era when tidal waters still reached this area — would have been a place name derived from a promontory that jutted out into the shoreline. If so, "tsuchide" would be a transformed pronunciation of "tsukide" (突出, "projection, protrusion"), and it seems more naturally rendered as "Tsuchide Manor" (都知伝荘).
