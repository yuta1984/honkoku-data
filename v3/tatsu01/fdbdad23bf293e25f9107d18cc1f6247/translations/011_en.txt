However, that interpretation is mistaken. Until around the beginning of the Keichō era (late 1590s), the boundary between the domain of Akita Jōnosuke Sanesue and the domain of Tōzawa Kurō Moriyasu lay at the foot of Mt. Minemaki. Because this mountain's foot served as the boundary, the nearby village took the name "Sakai" (境, "boundary"). Furthermore, descending from Mt. Funaoka, there is a small stream called the *Karishiba River* (刈柴川). This place is believed to be the site of the ancient **Karishiba Barrier** (苅柴の関, *Karishiba no Seki*).

It must have been around the beginning of the An'ei era (c. 1772) that a monk named Gyokusetsu from Jakujō-in Temple in Harima Province came to this land of Dewa. He searched with great difficulty for the Karishiba Barrier and finally arrived at the post town of Kariwano, where he lodged for a night. He declared, "This Kariwano must surely be the site of the ancient Karishiba Barrier — how wonderful, I have finally found it!" The next morning, he presented the head of the post station with a *tanzaku* (poem slip) on which he had written the verse: *Karishiba no seki wa na ni fure aki no kaze* ("At Karishiba Barrier, famed in name — the autumn wind"). He also visited the home of Sayōan Gomei and left him the same verse before departing. The painter Nyūsensai Ranshi often spoke of this incident.

In the sixth volume of *Eikyō Gunki* (永慶軍記, *Military Chronicles of Eikyō*), in the section on the Battle of Akita Yamakita, the following appears: "Crossing the border of Akita Yamakita to take up positions, there was a man called Shindō Chikugo-no-kami Noritsugu stationed at a strategic fortification in a place called Arakawa on the border of Tōzawa Kurō Moriyasu's domain. He sent a swift messenger to urgently report this to Moriyasu. Moriyasu grew uneasy, but

In an appendix to the same work, in a passage titled "The Matter of Hirota Takahashi," it reads: "At the time of the Battle of Osaka, [etc.], and also [he] died in battle at the border of Senboku while serving under the Lord of Akita Castle in Dewa Province," as recorded in the old chronicle.

Finding it strange that a later military chronicle wrote the name as "Takahashi Gensai," the author met with a descendant of the Takahashi family and inquired about this. The descendant explained: "In the battle at the border of Yamakita, the one who struck down Gensai was on the Tōzawa side, and because he had a clear memory of killing him with his own hand, he must have recorded it as a death in battle."

At that time, Gensai was a veteran warrior past fifty years of age. He was surrounded by enemies, and every last one of his mounted attendants (*umamazari*, personal cavalry escort) was cut down. Gensai himself had his helmet struck off and received lance wounds in several places. As he lay face-down on the ground, the enemy wasted no time in striking off his head. However, his head fell away into the valley, still barely attached by the skin of his throat. Immediately, the kinsmen and retainers of Tōzawa turned back and rejoined the battle; though they had struck down Gensai, they were ultimately unable to retrieve his head and withdrew. This was a night engagement in which friend and foe were intermingled, pressing forward and falling back in turn. In the end, the Akita forces were routed, and there was no way even to search for the bodies of those who had fallen...
