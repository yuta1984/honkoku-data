Upon offering their prayers, whether by the power of the deity it is not certain, but the lady gave birth with great ease to a baby boy. As a votive offering of thanks (*kaeri-mōshi*), it is said that Lady Hisa-hime presented a lion's head (*shishi-gashira*) to the Karamatsu Shrine. Even today, people don that lion's head and go about dancing from place to place performing sacred entertainment (*kagura*) through the Hiraka and Senboku regions. This matter is also hinted at in the *Tokusei Yawa* [*Conversations of the Night on Governance*].

How remarkable it is that even across such a vast distance, prayers proved so strikingly efficacious! It is entirely fitting to speak of this deity as the birth-spirit deity (*musuhi-no-kami*) of all of Dewa Province. The *Rokugu Saishi-ki* [Record of Ritual Affairs in the Six Districts] [marginal note: authored by Naka Michihiro (Hekihō rōjin, the Old Man of Green Peak)] records: "On the eighth day of the fourth month: Festival of Karamatsu Gongen, located in Sakai-no-sato, Senboku District. The *bettō* [administrative monk] is the *shugendo* temple Kōun-ji. As a deity of safe childbirth, not only from the six districts but from distant places as well, women flock here in great numbers." That said, the principal formal festival (*moto-no-matsuri*) is on the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month.

To the north of this village is a mountain called the Mountain of Aigo Gongen (Atago Gongen). Today there is no longer a shrine there, nor any trace of the deity's presence. Nevertheless, the local people still call it "Mt. Atago" (Atago-yama). On this mountain is a great rock, and on its face is carved the form of a lion (*shishi*). The figure stands some six or seven shaku tall, but it is now almost entirely buried in the earth, with only the faintest trace of the lion's head still visible, it is said. It is a most ancient shrine site (*miya-dokoro*), and in former times,

**[Left folio]**

the deity Kagutsuchi-no-kami (the Fire Deity) was enshrined there, serving as the tutelary (*chinushi*) shrine of the land. The goddess of Karamatsu-hime was also enshrined there, in the manner of a main shrine with branch shrine (*sessha*), or perhaps in a joint sanctuary (*ai-dono*). Thereafter, when the village of Sakai was established, the deity was transferred to the present shrine grounds, and for this reason the twenty-fourth day was designated as the day of the principal festival.

At the foot of that Mt. Atago is a place called "Dōkaino" (堂海野). This is said to be a corruption of "Dakaido-no" (堂街道野, "the field by the hall-and-highway"). The present site of Karamatsu Gongen's enshrinement is not from the middle ages; it is said that the deity was relocated two or even three times. Among the nearby places — the headland point (*desaki*) of Hanawa, the location called Iwabuchi (Rock Pool) — might these be places of significance?

As for the connection between Karamatsu-no-kami and Atago-no-kami: according to the shrine's origin record (*engi*), "When Empress Jingū (Okinaga-Tarashi-Hime) was subjugating the Three Han kingdoms of Korea, various deities manifested and guarded the sacred ship. Among them, a fierce deity named Eijichi Tarō advanced as the vanguard and moved about with the freedom of a bird in flight. The king of Silla, seeing this, was filled with awe and trembled with fear, saying, 'Surely these are the divine soldiers of Yamato — how could we resist them?' — and submitted."

This Eijichi Tarō is a tengu deity (a high-flying bird deity), who resides even now in the inner shrine of Mt. Atago in the imperial capital (Kyoto). He is a great tengu (*dai-tengu*), whose original form (*honji*) is Jizō Bosatsu (Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva), but in truth he is none other than the deity Kagutsuchi-no-kami, and so forth.

It is therefore fitting that Empress Jingū and the Atago deity are enshrined together in a joint sanctuary (*ai-dono*).
