That he attained great enlightenment through this — none but the person himself could truly know. Yet let us briefly illuminate the meaning of the poem. The phrase "in early autumn, the wind blows and binds the reeds (*ogi*)" is a poetic association (*hikiyose*) drawn from an old poem, sharing the same sentiment as "if you bind them together, it becomes a brushwood hermitage." Nōin's verse — "In this transient world, again I sleep in travel, resting on a borrowed pillow; even within a dream I dream another dream" — and the hokku of the monk Sōgi of recent times, "To pass through this world is but a brief lodging in a rain-shower" — though composed in different ages, all share the same sentiment. Is it not noble indeed, the way the words of those who have truly attained mastery accord with the very heart of the gods and buddhas?

[Left page]

In the early autumn wind that rustles softly through the reed leaves, a brief dew settles — and from this image, the poem shows us the truth of the fleeting and illusory nature of human existence, and the swift passage of all things. If one chants and savors it deeply, surely there will come a time when one will be startled awake from the deluded dream of this provisional world. One must never forget this. The truth that all things are impermanent and pass with swift urgency is something everyone knows — and yet those who truly take it genuinely to heart are rare indeed. In the *Shinchokusen-shū* [*New Chokusen Anthology*], Prince Masanari composed the verse: "I am startled when I think of the world as a dream from afar — yet there is no dawn at which my heart truly awakens." How supremely admirable is the spirit of mind expressed therein. Saigyō —
