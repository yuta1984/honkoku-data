And yet, if one were to illuminate the meaning of the verse for a moment: the phrase "the wind blows and binds together in early autumn," used as a *kakekotoba* (pivot word) with *ogi* (reed grass), is of the same intent as the ancient poem that reads, "if you bind it together, it becomes a thatched hermitage." The poem by Nōin — "In this temporary world, again I sleep on a journey, a borrowed pillow; even within a dream, I see another dream" — and the *hokku* of the recent master Sōgi — "To pass through this world is but a brief lodging in the winter drizzle" — though composed in different ages, are all verses of the same spirit. How noble indeed is the fact that the words of men of profound attainment align perfectly with the minds of the gods and buddhas!

From the reed leaves swaying in the early autumn breeze, upon which dew briefly settles, one learns that the human world is fleeting — this verse reveals the truth of the swiftness of impermanence. If one chants it deeply and savors its meaning, there will surely come a moment when one awakens with a start from the deluded dream of this temporary world. One must never forget this. The truth of the swiftness of impermanence is something that everyone claims to know, yet those who truly take it genuinely to heart are rare indeed. In the *Shin Senzaishū*, Prince Masanari composed: "Though I am startled when I regard the dream-like world as something apart from me, there is never a dawn when my heart truly awakens." This is a verse of most admirable spirit and feeling. Saigyō —
