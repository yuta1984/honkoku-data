— night and day, doing nothing but cradling his *gidan* (ball of doubt) and applying himself to his *kūfu* (Zen investigation). One day, as he faced the garden and was working on a *kōan*, a voice arose — from no one he could identify — and intoned a single waka poem. The poem read:

*Hatsуaki ni / kaze fuki musubu / Oginodō / ha ni yado kari no / yo no yume zo samekerи*

(In early autumn, the wind rises and binds the grasses of Oginodō — on those leaves, the dream of this borrowed world of lodging now awakes.)

This was chanted four or five times. The moment the Zen practitioner heard this poem, the doubt he had harbored for so many years suddenly cleared away, and the words of the Buddha and the words of the patriarchs became, all at once, perfectly transparent to him. Just as the Bodhisattva Kanzeon (*Avalokiteśvara*) herself — as the great being (*daishi*) who attained the Way through the faculty of hearing — had entered *samādhi* through hearing, reflection, and cultivation, so too did he now hear this verse and find the ice of his doubt instantly melted, achieving a state of open, unobstructed

[Left page]

freedom. He understood at once that this too was the spiritual inspiration (*reikan*) of the great Bodhisattva. He made his way to the base of a single clump of reeds (*ogi*) in the garden, from where the voice of the poem had seemed to come, and looked — and there he found the poem he had just heard, written out on a strip of poem paper (*tanzaku*). The Zen practitioner leaped for joy, reverently took the paper, brushed away the dew from beneath the reeds, and built a small hall in proper form, in which he enshrined the sacred image. This was the founding (*sōsō*) of this temple. Thereafter, miraculous signs (*reikan*) occurred many times over, and as the years and months passed, the halls and buildings were completed in full, until the temple reached its present flourishing state.
