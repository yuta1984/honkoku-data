"Then I shall offer up worship and veneration," he said, and took care of this principal image (*honzon*), enshrining and moving it, and appointed that pilgrim as the keeper of the hall (*dōmori*). Thus, after a month and more had passed, the pilgrim vanished, none knew whither. Having heard this debate passed down through the ages, and having reviewed what the elders had written and recorded, I now set it down in this compilation, with the wish to silence the mouths of those Shinto adherents of perverse views and narrow-minded Confucian scholars who would reject Buddhism. As for the matter of the principal image having been moved to this place from Takashino — this came about in accordance with the divine oracle of a miraculous Buddha received by a resident monk of middle times. As for the devotional verse (*eika*) of this temple, it requires no further annotation. For this reason,

[Left page]

I omit it here. The devotional verse reads:

*Arataka ni / mairi te ogamu / Kanzeon / nise anraku to / dare mo inoram*

(How manifestly wondrous — I come and bow before Kanzeon; may all pray for peace and ease in both this world and the next.)

Upon reflection, the man who was the pilgrim was surely no ordinary person. He must certainly have been a manifestation of the Buddha (*kegen*). Just as it is written, "All sentient beings are every one my children" — that he said "My pity for you is as a parent's grief for a lost child" is, I feel, perfectly in accord with the Buddha-heart (*busshin*) that compassionates the prodigal son (*chōjashi*) in the Lotus Sutra. Fearing that people of the world might regard this with insufficient seriousness and fail to believe it, I repeat myself and explain at such length that the pilgrim was a manifestation (*ōken*) of the Buddha — it is thus.
