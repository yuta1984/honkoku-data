(Continued from previous page) ...having founded numerous temple complexes throughout the various provinces, his virtue became known even in distant foreign lands, and countless eminent monks came to be converted and submit to his rule. The Brahman bishop from South India (Bodhisena), Buttetsu from Linyi, and Jianzhen (Ganjin) from Tang China each came to Japan, praised the Emperor's virtue, and actively spread the Buddhist teachings. At this time, the two masters Gyōki and Rōben were appointed to conduct lectures on the Lotus Sūtra, the Suvarṇaprabhāsa Sūtra (Saishōkyō), and the Two Mandalas (Ryōbu), as prayers for the peace and safety of the nation. Furthermore, as provincial temples (Kokubunji) and provincial nunneries (Kokubun-niji) were ordered built throughout the provinces, the Bodhisattva Gyōki traveled across the roads in all four directions, and

[Left page]

wherever he discerned that a place was spiritually auspicious, he would carve a Buddha image and erect a hall, ensuring that the Buddhist teachings would never decline in that place. At this time, Gyōki came together with the Inspector (Anzatsu-shi) of the Eastern Circuit, and perceiving that this particular place was surely a sacred site with a karmic connection to Kannon (Avalokiteśvara), he carved the principal image and founded the hall. After this, more than two hundred years passed, and in the second year of Eien (998 CE, the year of Tsuchinoe-Ne, during the reign of Emperor Ichijō, the sixty-sixth sovereign), the Venerable Shōkū, following a revelation from a transformed being (ke-nin), opened Mount Shosha in Banshū (Harima Province) and founded Enkyō-ji temple.
