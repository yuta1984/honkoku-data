...clapped his hands and burst into great laughter. The Confucian scholar, bristling with indignation, drew himself up haughtily, raised his voice, and rebuked the monk, saying, "What manner of lawless priest is this? Who are you calling a 'decadent pedant'?"—and reached for his sword to confront him. At that moment, the old monk pointed at the Confucian scholar and declared: "That very state you are in right now—*that* is what you asked about: 'the black wind that tears a ship from its moorings and sends it drifting down into the Land of Rākṣasa Demons.'" He laughed and rose to his feet, and suddenly the Confucian scholar felt as if he had awakened from a profound dream. He reflected: "Until now I have dismissed Buddhist teaching as mere shallow and superficial words—something that neither cultivates the mind nor corrects social morality, and not the true path. How foolish of me." He was about to prostrate himself before the old monk in reverence, when suddenly the

[Left page]

monk vanished without a trace. Afterward, this Confucian scholar built a hermitage near this hall, took lasting refuge in the Buddha, and became a lay practitioner (*koji*) who held Buddhism inwardly and Confucianism outwardly. This episode was entirely identical to the recorded dialogue between Ziyü (*紫玉*) and Lord Di Xiangong (*頔相公*) from a foreign land, and fearing that readers might suspect the account had been fabricated by drawing on that source, the full exchange—though quite lengthy—has been omitted from this volume. The elderly monk in question must have been a manifestation (*kegen*) of the sacred image enshrined at this temple, who took pity on the Confucian scholar's one-sided prejudice and instructed him in this manner—truly a most awe-inspiring and sacred thing. The site where the lay founder of this mountain once lived is still known to this day as the "Chōja Yashiki" (the Elder's Residence), and...
