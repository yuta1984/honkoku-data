Genzū steadfastly upheld the final instructions, crossed hundreds of leagues of sea and land, and came to this place. Observing the nature of the land and soil and examining the local customs, he found that, as many years had passed since the temple's founding, the hearts of the people of that time were greatly corrupted — indeed, they could rightly be called "Eastern Barbarians" (tōi) — and so the conditions for forming a karmic bond with the teachings were not yet ripe. Deciding he should wait until the right time arrived, he hid himself in the nearby mountain forests and devoted himself to sutra recitation. After that, he passed through many springs and autumns, and then one day, the opportune conditions suddenly arose, and those of sincere faith

[Left page]

joined their efforts together, and the halls and pagodas were restored to their former appearance. Thereupon, a memorial service was held with the recitation of forty thousand volumes of the Wondrous Sutras, and a mound (tsuka) was built to mark this for future generations. From this, the place took its name (Shimanbu-ji, "Temple of Forty Thousand Volumes"). Truly, this is a miraculous and sublime sacred site beyond all ordinary understanding. The pilgrim's verse for this temple reads:

　*Arigataya / hitomaki naranu nori no hana / kazu wa shimanbuno tera no inishie*
　How wondrous — not just one scroll but flowers of the Dharma; the number: forty thousand volumes, this temple of ancient times.

To explain the meaning of the verse: How deeply grateful we must be, for the merits of the Wondrous Sutra are not in vain — not a single character, not a single phrase lacks its benefit. How much more so, then, the merit of forty thousand volumes, vast and boundless beyond all measure.
