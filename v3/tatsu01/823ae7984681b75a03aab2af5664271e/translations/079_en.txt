**The Great Nenbutsu of Mibu**

The Great Nenbutsu (Dainenbutsu) of Mibu was initiated by the reviving founder, the Venerable Engaku, and is performed each year for ten days and ten nights beginning on the fourteenth day of the third month, in the main hall. During this religious gathering, various kyōgen plays are performed. This is likely a skillful means (hōben) intended to draw those who are foolish and unenlightened into forming a good karmic connection and setting them upon the path to enlightenment (bodhi).

**Mibu-dera Temple**

Mibu-dera is located to the east of Gojō-bōmon and Suzaku. It belongs to the Shingon Ritsu sect and is affiliated with Tōshōdai-ji in Yamato Province (modern Nara). The principal image is a seated statue of Kṣitigarbha (Jizō Bosatsu), three shaku in height, and is a work of the master Buddhist sculptor Jōchō.

The temple was founded in the second year of Shōryaku (991), during the reign of Emperor Ichijō, and its founding patron was the Great Prelate Kaiken of Mii-dera (Onjō-ji). Kaiken was of the Fujiwara clan, a collateral relative of the Awata Regent, Fujiwara no Michikane. He accompanied the Great Teacher Chishō (Enchin) and mastered the innermost teachings of the Tendai school. He passed away on the sixteenth day of the eleventh month of the sixteenth year of Eishō (※).

Kaiken vowed to have a venerable image of Jizō sculpted, and commissioned the Buddhist sculptor Jōchō, who completed the work over the course of one thousand days. The statue's features (sōgō) are perfectly formed, as if one were in the presence of the living Buddha himself. This is the principal image of the temple.

Regarding the staff (shakujō) held by the principal image: on the day of the dedication ceremony (rakkei), a deep mist surrounded the four sides of the principal image, a wondrous fragrance filled the air, music was faintly heard, and it appeared as though the sacred host had come to welcome the departed (shōjū raigō). Around the hour of the Horse (noon), the mist gradually cleared, and when the principal image was venerated, it was suddenly found to be holding a six-ringed staff. Furthermore, on a certain night, the principal image appeared in a dream and announced: "This staff emerged from the ground when Śākyamuni Buddha was preaching the Enmei Jizō-kyō (Sutra of Life-prolonging Jizō) on Mount Gāyā (Garaida-zan)."

In its earliest days, the temple consisted of a simple thatched hall in which this principal image was enshrined. In the second year of Kankō (1005), a formal hall dedication was held and the temple was named "Ko-Mii-dera" (Lesser Mii-dera). Later, during the Kenpō era (1213–1219) in the reign of Emperor Juntoku, a man called Taira no Ason Munemasa, formerly an official of Yamato Province, received the miraculous blessings of the principal image, and thereafter rebuilt all the halls and monks' quarters. At this time the temple was renamed "Hōdō Zanmai-in." It was also called "Jizō-in." The Retired Emperors Shirakawa, Toba, Go-Shirakawa, and Juntoku also held the temple in deep reverence and paid it imperial visits, as is recorded in detail in the temple's founding records.

The reviver of the temple was the Venerable Engaku, a native of Hattori in Yamato Province and a son of Fujiwara no Hirotsune (Hiromoto).

**The Great Nenbutsu**

Beginning with the Venerable Engaku, various sarugaku (precursor performances to nō theater) are performed each year from the fourteenth to the twenty-fourth day of the third month. These are called the "Mibu Kyōgen."

---

**Danku (Altar Offerings)**

Each year during the New Year, mochi rice cakes (mirror cakes, kagami-mochi) are offered. People receive these offerings and are thereby protected from unexpected calamity (yokonan).
