## Sekizō-ji Temple · Hokukōzan Kangi-ji Temple · Iwagami Shrine · The Former Site of Jurakudai

---

**Karyūzan Sekizō-ji Temple** is located to the north of Itsutsuji on Senbondōri Avenue. It belongs to the Jōdo (Pure Land) sect, and its principal image of Amida Buddha is said to have been crafted by Kanpō (Sugawara no Michizane). In the Jizō Hall stands a stone image of Jizō in upright form, made by Kōbō Daishi (Kūkai). Those who worship it with sincere faith are said to receive miraculous responses, and the temple's name, "Sekizō-ji" (Temple of the Stone Image), derives from this tradition. Within the temple grounds stands a memorial pagoda for the former Governor of Kazusa, Fujiwara no Ason Karyu-kyō.

---

**Hokukōzan Kangi-ji Temple** is situated to the west of Kamitaturi. It belongs to the Shingon sect, and its principal image of Kangiten (Vinayaka/Ganesha) was made by Kōbō Daishi. This temple was established as a place of prayer by imperial decree of Emperor Saga, and its founding is attributed to Kōbō Daishi.

---

**Iwagami Shrine** is located to the west of Kangi-ji Temple. The object of worship is a great rock approximately six *shaku* (roughly 1.8 metres) in height, enshrined within a *dozō* (a fireproof storehouse). It is said that this stone was originally located within the precincts of the Great Imperial Palace (*Daidairi*).

---

**The Former Site of Jurakudai** lies to the south of Ichijō and to the north of Nijō, bounded on the east by Ōmiya and on the west by Suzaku Avenue (present-day Senbondōri). In the thirteenth year of Tenshō (1585), Taikō Hideyoshi built a fortress here, declaring that only magnificent splendour could command true authority. The palace halls were inlaid with the seven precious materials, and famous trees and extraordinary stones were gathered from across the land — a treasury of halls said to rival even the Epang Palace of Qin and the Weiyang Palace of the Former Han dynasty. In the sixteenth year of Tenshō (1588), the Emperor made an imperial visit, and poetry gatherings and *nō* performances were held. Afterwards, Kampaku (Imperial Regent) Hidetsugu resided here; however, following his downfall in the fourth year of Bunroku (1595), the palace buildings were dispersed to various temples throughout the city. Today, only the name "Juraku" survives, having become a collective designation for the neighbourhood. The number of districts grouped under the name "Juraku-gumi" amounts to approximately one hundred and twenty *chō*.
