**Sanjō Bridge**

Sanjō Bridge is the gateway through which one enters the capital city of Heiankyō (Kyoto) from the eastern provinces. Travelers of all social ranks constantly pass through here in great numbers, and the splendor of the imperial city is manifest upon this bridge. The railings are adorned with eighteen finials (擬宝珠, giboshi) cast in purple copper (an alloy resembling bronze), each inscribed with an engraving. The inscription reads: "The Sanjō Bridge of Rakuyō [Kyoto]; built to guide travelers of all future generations. The foundation stones are sunk five fathoms deep into the riverbed; sixty-three cut-stone pillars [support the structure]. This may well be the origin of stone-pillar bridges in Japan. On a lucky day in the first month of the eighteenth year of Tenshō (1590), the year of Kanoeto-tora, during the first reign of the Toyotomi [clan], Masuda Uemon-no-jō Nagamori constructed this [bridge] by order [of Toyotomi Hideyoshi]."

**Danō Hōrinji Temple**

Located at the eastern end of Sanjō Bridge. It belongs to the Jōdo (Pure Land) sect, and its principal object of worship, an Amida Buddha, is a work attributed to Eshin Sōzu (Genshin). In ancient times it was called Goshiuji Temple, and was founded by Dōkō Hōshi, a disciple of the Venerable Ryōchū. After the Ōnin War it fell into ruin. During the Keichō era (1596–1615), the Venerable Chūnai rebuilt it and renamed it Sendanō-in. (Today it is popularly called "Danō" for short.)

After the Venerable Chūnai retired from the temple, he took up residence east of Gojō Bridge in a hermitage called Chūnai-an. The temple is currently presided over by a nun abbess.

**The Shrine of Shuyashin (Lord of the Night)** was established through the devotional petition of the temple's founder, the Venerable Chūnai. According to the temple's founding legend: "On the fifteenth day of the third month of the eighth year of Keichō (1603), the Venerable Chūnai entered a period of special religious practice (betsugyō) and was reciting the nembutsu, when suddenly a figure appeared before him in the midst of radiant light, clothed in a crimson robe and blue surplice. This figure spoke to the Venerable, saying: 'I am Vasantī, the Shuyashin (主夜神, Guardian of the Night) described in the Avatamsaka Sūtra [Kegon-kyō]. I shall protect all those who practice exclusive nembutsu recitation.' The figure then bestowed upon him a secret talisman." Miraculous efficacy has been abundantly demonstrated, and many worshippers visit at all times. (Since the Keichō era, the deity had been enshrined in the temple's treasure storehouse, but in recent years it has been installed in its present hall. The torii gate is made of stone pillars, and the tablet [bearing the shrine's name] is in the calligraphy of Prince Arisugawa Taruhito [Shokujin].)

**The Guardian Shrine** enshrines the Kamo Daijingū. (It was petitioned before the founding of the present temple. It is said that when the Kamo River flooded in ancient times, the Tadasu Shrine was carried by the floodwaters to this location, and the deity came to be enshrined here for that reason.)

**Ryūō Shrine (Dragon King Shrine):** Long ago, there was a large deep pool on the eastern bank of the Kamo River, where a malevolent serpent dwelt and harmed people. In a year of drought, the serpent was slain, and its spirit was enshrined and worshipped.

**Sodome Jizō (Sleeve-Stopping Jizō):** Origins unknown.

**The Three-Trunk Pine:** It is said that the Venerable Chūnai petitioned and enshrined the deities of the Two Kumano Gongen here.

**Zuisenji Temple**

Located to the south of Sanjō Kobashi (the small Sanjō Bridge). It belongs to the Jōdo sect, and its principal object of worship, an Amida Buddha, is traditionally attributed to Prince Shōtoku. The temple was founded by the monk Sankū Keishuku, and the principal patron was the Zuiryūin, the mother of the Imperial Regent (Kampaku) Lord Hidetsugu, who built it in his memory. (Lord Hidetsugu is enshrined under the posthumous title Zuiseiin-dono.)

During the Bunroku era (1592–1596), Lord Hidetsugu was accused of plotting against the Taikō (retired regent) Hideyoshi, and entered Mount Kōya in Kii Province, where he took his own life. His head was brought to the Sanjō riverbed and put on public display. Moreover, more than thirty of his concubines, together with their young children, were executed at this very site and buried together in a single grave. Thereafter, a burial mound was raised with a cut stone placed on top, bearing the inscription: "The Mound of Hidetsugu's Treacherous Evil — the fifteenth day of the seventh month of the fourth year of Bunroku [1595]." (Beside it stands a stone grave marker serving as the tomb of the more than thirty concubines, with all of their Buddhist posthumous names inscribed upon it. Those who call this the "Chikushō-zuka" [Mound of Beasts] are mistaken.)

**Pontocho**

Pontocho lies on the western bank of the Kamo River, south of Sanjō. Along the riverbank, the eaves of the riverside pavilions (water terraces, 水楼) line up in a row. Guests seated there enjoy the scenic view of eastern Kyoto, and refined visitors who delight in drinking and singing gather here in great numbers.
