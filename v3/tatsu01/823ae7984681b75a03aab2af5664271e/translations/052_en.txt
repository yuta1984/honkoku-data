## Ryūchisan Daiunin (Great Cloud Temple)

Ryūchisan Daiunin is located to the south of Kyōgoku Shijō. It belongs to the Jōdo sect and is affiliated with Chion'in. The principal object of worship, an Amida Buddha, was created by the priest Eshin Sōzu. The temple was founded by the priest Teian. Teian was an exceptionally gifted figure within the Jōdo sect at the time of the Azuchi Religious Debate, and Lord Nobunaga held him in great reverence. Nobunaga built Saikō-ji in Ōmi Hachiman, where Teian served as head priest. It was during this time that Lord Nobunaga and his son were killed at the hands of Akechi Mitsuhide. Upon hearing this news, the priest Teian hastened to Kyoto, established a hermitage near Nijō Karasuma, and devoted himself wholly to prayers for their souls. Thereafter, by order of Lord Hideyoshi, toward the end of the Tenshō era, the temple was founded to pray for the repose of the spirit of Lord Oda Nobutada. Nobutada's posthumous Buddhist name is given as "Daiunin-den Sanpon Urin Sengan Koji."
　　(The temple's name derives from this. Memorial stupas for Lord Nobunaga and Lord Nobutada are located here.)

When Lord Nobunaga was residing at Azuchi Castle, he bestowed upon the priest Teian seven rare and unusual objects. These are now among the temple's treasured possessions. Among them is the Ichimai Kishōmon (One-Page Oath) of the monk Hōnen, written in the hand of the monk Ikkyū. At the end is a pictorial account: the illustration depicts Bodhidharma seen from behind. The inscription reads:

　　*That fellow Dharma who claims to have attained enlightenment —*
　　*the chest he reveals is nothing but bare, ordinary bones.*

　　*To sit in meditation for nine years is in vain;*
　　*the true moment comes with a single utterance of Amida.*

　　　　To the honorable Butsugosho-sama
　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　Ikkyū (seal)

## The Ceremony of the Gion Festival

Now, every year beginning with the period of ritual abstinence (chisai) on the first day of the fifth month, sakaki branches are erected in Shijō Otabi-chō. This is the former site of the first torii gate. From the twentieth day, the auspicious opening ceremony (kisshu-iri) is held, and in the neighborhoods of the hoko floats, the festival music (hayashi) begins. The ritual purification of the mikoshi (Omikoshi-arai) takes place on the last day of the same month, and the parade of welcoming lanterns and processional decorations is splendidly elegant, adding to the festive atmosphere of eastern Kyoto. On the first day of the sixth month, the children who ride the hoko floats make a pilgrimage to Gion — traveling by palanquin or on horseback — their procession magnificently adorned, resembling the passage of noble dignitaries. On the fifth day comes the first pulling of the hoko floats. In the early morning of the sixth day, at Rokkaku-dō, lots are drawn to determine the order of the procession of the yama and hoko floats. That same evening, in what is called the "Eve Decoration" (Yomiya-kazari), the floats are adorned as if for the festival day itself; numerous lanterns are strung in rows, music continues until late into the night, and crowds of people of all social ranks gather in indescribable numbers. On the seventh day, for the Gion Festival proper, beginning from the Hour of the Rabbit (around 5–7 a.m.), the rows of yama and hoko floats are assembled and proceed from Shijō-dōri southward along Kyōgoku, then westward along Matsubara. The festival rites of the mikoshi on this day take place at the Hour of the Sheep (around 1–3 p.m.), and the divine procession travels from Kanjin'in (Yasaka Shrine) to the Otabisho (temporary shrine). From the eighth day onward, preparations are made for the yama and hoko floats of the fourteenth, and lots are drawn on the morning of the thirteenth. On the fourteenth, the yama and hoko floats proceed eastward along Sanjō-dōri, southward along Kyōgoku, and westward along Shijō. The mikoshi ceremony proceeds westward from the Otabisho along Shijō, and from Higashinotōin the mikoshi separate and proceed in different directions — northward and southward. Reuniting at the temporary shrine to the west of Sanjō, they return in procession eastward along Sanjō. On the eighteenth of the same month, the ritual purification of the mikoshi (Omikoshi-arai) is held (similar to that on the last day of the previous month), and the banks of the Kamo River near Gion are thronged with people like reeds and bamboo.

　Only one or two illustrations of the yama and hoko floats are included here. These are not complete depictions. The detailed procedures of ancient customs for each annual festival are described fully in the *Gion-e Saiki* (Detailed Record of the Gion Festival), and are therefore omitted here.
