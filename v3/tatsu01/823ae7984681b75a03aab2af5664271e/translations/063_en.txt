*(Continued from the previous page)* Upon hearing this, the Saint composed the following poem:

　　　*Yo no naka wa tada tsuyu no ma no amayadori / tsui no sumika wa raise narikeri*
　　　(This world is but a brief shelter from the rain, lasting only a moment like the dew; / our true and final dwelling place is the world to come.)
　　　— Saint Kūya

**Supplement (additional poem):**

　　　*Hitokoe mo "Namu Amida Butsu" to iu hito no / hasu no ue ni noboranu wa nashi*
　　　(There is no one who utters even a single recitation of "Namu Amida Butsu" / who does not ascend upon a lotus flower [in the Pure Land].)
　　　— Same (Saint Kūya)

## Chinnouchi-ji Temple (Chinpōji / Rokudō Chinnō-ji)

Chinnouchi-ji Temple is located along Matsubara-dōri, to the south of Kennin-ji Temple.
　　　It is known by the name "Rokudō" (the Six Paths).
　　　The principal image, a Yakushi Buddha (Medicine Buddha), is the work of Dengyō Daishi. The temple was founded by the monk Keishun Sōzu, and restored by Kōbō Daishi. The Takamura-dō hall enshrines a statue of Ono no Takamura.
　　　It is said that a path leading to the underworld (*meido*) passes through this very location.

The Emma-dō (Hall of Emma, King of Hell) is to the east. The "Welcoming Bell" (*mukae-gane*): on the ninth and tenth days of the seventh month, worshippers visiting the temple ring this bell to summon the spirits of their ancestors.

**The Rokudō Crossroads**
　　　Located in front of the main hall.

This temple has served since ancient times as the burial ground of the capital. When Emperor Kanmu moved the capital from Nagaoka to this city (Heian-kyō) in the thirteenth year of Enryaku (794 CE), he designated this site as the public burial ground, as recorded in the *Sentoki* (Record of the Capital Transfer). This area is also called "Atago."

　　　In *The Tale of Genji*, the passage describing the burial of Kiritsubo no Kōi — "she was taken to a place called Ōtagi, and the proceedings were carried out with great solemnity" — refers to this very location. According to the *Kakaishō* (a commentary on *The Tale of Genji*), this was a sacred site of Kōbō Daishi and was once administered by the head of Tō-ji Temple. It is now under the jurisdiction of Daishō-in, a sub-temple of Kennin-ji.

## Hokuto-dō (Northern Dipper Hall)

In former times, this hall stood approximately two *chō* (about 220 meters) to the east of the Rokudō crossroads. It was dedicated to the North Star (*Hokushin*), and tall lanterns were hung from its pillars. These lanterns shone as all-night lights (*jōyatō*), serving as navigational markers for boats traveling from south of the capital along the Yodo River. The line in the *Nō* chant "Kumano" — "the stars of the Northern Dipper shine without a cloud" — refers to this hall. It was destroyed in the fires of the Ōnin War.

　　　On one occasion, Kanamori Sōwa, while traveling from Edo to Kyoto, visited Kiyomizu-dera Temple and, finding this lantern toppled and buried in moss, requested it from the temple monks. He took it back to Edo as a curious Eastern treasure, and it is said to remain to this day at his Shiba residence.

## Seimei Shrine

The Seimei Shrine is located north of Matsubara, to the east of Miyagawa-chō. In ancient times, a burial mound (*tsuka*) of Abe no Seimei stood on this site, but as new roads were laid and houses built, the mound gradually crumbled away and the land became flat. A shrine was therefore built here to enshrine and venerate his spirit.

---

## Tei Zenji Shrine

The Tei Zenji Shrine is to the south of the Seimei Shrine. In former times, the precinct was vast and thickly wooded. It is said that Ushiwakamaru (the young Minamoto no Yoshitsune) hid in this grove and cut down a thousand men. It is also said that Musashibō Benkei, before this very shrine, made his vow of loyalty to his lord.

---

## Wakamiya Hachiman Shrine

Wakamiya Hachiman Shrine is located five *chō* (about 550 meters) to the east of Gojō Bridge. The enshrined deity is the same as that of Iwashimizu Hachiman Shrine. Originally situated at Sameushi in Rokujō, it is also called "Sameushi Hachiman."

　　　The annual festival is the *Hōjōe* (Life-releasing Ceremony) on the fifteenth day of the eighth month. The shrine's former location was along Wakamiya-dōri, in the area now known as Butsugu-ya-chō (Buddhist Goods Street).
　　　To the south of Rokujō (now Uonotana-dōri), a small shrine stands behind the houses — this is the remnant of its former site. During the Tenshō era (1573–1592), it was moved to its present location.

---

## Gojō Bridge

Gojō Bridge was originally located on Matsubara-dōri — this was the ancient Gojō-dōri. During the reign of Lord Hideyoshi (Toyotomi Hideyoshi), it was moved to its present location, and the street is therefore called "Gojō Bridge Street," though in reality it corresponds to the old Rokujō Bōmon. The balustrades are fitted with sixteen giboshi (ornamental bronze finials) of purple copper on each side. On the fourth finial from the west on the north side is the bridge's inscription:

　　　*"Rakuyō Gojō Sekkyō"* (Gojō Stone Bridge of the Capital)
　　　*Shōhō 2nd year, Year of Kinoto-Tori, Eleventh Month, an Auspicious Day* (1645)
　　　Magistrate (*bugyō*):
　　　　　Ashiura Kannon-ji, Shunkō
　　　　　Ogawa Tōzaemonojō Masanaga

From the middle of this bridge looking eastward, the scenic spots of eastern Kyoto reveal themselves through the trees, and here the beautiful views of the ancient capital reach their finest expression.

　　　*Futon kite netaru sugata ya higashi yama*
　　　(Clothed in quilts of cloud, lying in slumber — the Eastern Hills)
　　　　　　　　　　　　　　　— Ransetsu
