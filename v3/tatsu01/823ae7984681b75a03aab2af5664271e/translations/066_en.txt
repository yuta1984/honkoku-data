## Matsutoyo Hachiman Shrine (Kadode Hachiman Shrine)

Matsutoyo Hachiman Shrine is located at the western end of Gojō Bridge and is also called Kadode Hachiman ("Departure Hachiman"). It was founded during the Jōgan era (859–877) of Emperor Seiwa's reign. Later, the spirit of Imperial Prince Sadazumi was enshrined there, and the Prince's son, Rokusonnō Minamoto no Tsunemoto, held the shrine in great reverence, thoroughly rebuilding the palace and gate tower, and the precinct became vast.

> It is said that there were twelve gates in the outer precinct.
> During the time of Taira no Kiyomori, the power of the shrine declined and the grounds became much smaller.

## Shin-Zenkōji Miedō (Portrait Hall of New Zenkōji Temple)

The Shin-Zenkōji Miedō is located to the west of Kadode Hachiman. Long ago, during the Tenchō era (824–834), it was founded by Empress Danrin, with Kōbō Daishi (Kūkai) as its founding priest. The reviving patriarch, Saint Ōa, converted the temple from the Shingon sect to the Ji sect. The principal image of Amida Buddha was made by An'ami.

> The original principal image was a copy of the Nyorai of Zenkōji Temple in Shinano Province and served as the main object of worship.
> For this reason it is called the Miedō (Portrait Hall). It is currently enshrined in Zenkō-an within the temple complex.

Statues of Saint Ippen and Saint Ōa are enshrined here. The principal image of the abbot's quarters is the "Ikkō Sanzon" (one-halo trinity), consisting of Amida, Kannon, and Seishi, which are works of Kōbō Daishi and were the personal devotional objects of Emperor Saga. The "Mirror's Shiogama Well" is situated on both the north and south sides of the main hall. The Jizō Hall is to the east of the abbot's quarters.

The temple originally stood at Higashinotōin Kasuga, as a sub-temple of Danrinji, functioning as a nunnery. During the Jōan era (1171–1175), it was destroyed by fire and relocated to the Higashi Kawara-in site. In the 28th year of Ōei (1421), it moved to the north of Samenushi Muromachi; in the 2nd year of Kyōroku (1529), it moved to the north of Gojō Shinmachi; and in the 15th year of Tenshō (1587), it was relocated to its present site.

> The reason why making fans became a livelihood within the temple complex is as follows: Long ago, the Nun of Renge-in, wife of the Mukanno Tayū Taira no Atsumori, resided quietly in this temple and crafted "Akome fans." Around that time, when the Retired Emperor Gosaga fell ill, the temple's resident priest, Ajari Yūkan, performed ritual prayers for recovery and presented to the Emperor a fan inscribed with sacred incantations. The Emperor immediately recovered, and so the Imperial son, the Lady of Take-no-Gosho, restored the temple, took holy orders, and became known as Saint Ōa. Fans, following this auspicious precedent, became a renowned specialty of the temple across generations, treasured as prestigious gifts and prized by people both in the capital and the provinces.

## Site of the Kawara-in Palace

The former site of the Kawara-in Palace is located eight chō square to the east of Gojō Bridge Avenue and Madenokōji Road.

> It appears that the Kamo River once flowed through the garden of this palace.
> This place was the villa of Minister of the Left Minamoto no Tōru, who perfected every elegance of pavilions, water features, and rocks, indulging freely in pleasurable pursuits. Mountains were built with lush vegetation so that flowers bloomed in every season; ponds were dug and filled with water where fish played in the waves; the scenery of Matsushima in Mutsu Province was recreated; seawater was transported daily from Naniwa Bay; music rang out as if in a celestial realm; and books were enjoyed as if in a palace on the moon. After the Minister's passing, Retired Emperor Kanpyō (Emperor Uda) visited and toured this splendid site, naming it "Higashi Rokujō-in." It was later converted into a Buddhist temple, and a man of spiritual guidance named Saint Jinkō, the head of Girinji Temple and the third son of Lord Tōru, encouraged the carving of a jōroku Śākyamuni Buddha, which was enshrined in the hall and gave the site the name "Kawara-in."

> To the south of the present Gojō Bridge, between the Kamo River and the Takase River, there is a grove.
> This is called "Magaki no Mori" (the Grove of the Fence), and it is a remnant of the Kawara-in.

**From the Kokinshū (Anthology of Ancient and Modern Poems):**
> *Kimi masade / kemuri taenishi / shiogama no / ura sabishiku mo / mie wataru kana* — Tsurayuki
> (Now that you are gone, the smoke has ceased at Shiogama; how desolately the bay stretches before me)

**From the Shokugo-shūi-shū:**
> *Shiogama ni / itsuka ki ni ken / ato naki ni / tsuri suru fune wa / koko ni yoranan* — Narihira
> (When did I come to Shiogama? There is no trace left; let the fishing boats moor here)

## Hongakuji Temple

Hongakuji Temple is located at the corner of Shimoteramachi and Gojō. It belongs to the Jōdo sect and is affiliated with Chion-in. The principal image of Amida Buddha is a work of An'ami and is also called "Nyhohōbutsu" (the Buddha of the True Law). Its founding patron was Saint Gyokuō.
