**Inabadō (Byōdōji Temple)**

Inabadō Byōdōji Temple is located on Matsubara-dōri Karasuma. Its temple administration falls under the jurisdiction of the Tendai sect's Shōgoin monzeki (princely abbot), while the resident monks follow the Shingon sect. The principal image, a standing statue of Yakushi Nyorai (Bhaisajyaguru, the Medicine Buddha), stands six shaku and two sun tall (approximately 188 cm) and is enshrined upon a go-board shaped pedestal. As attendant figures, the temple houses Nikkō Bosatsu (Suryaprabha), Gakkō Bosatsu (Candraprabha), the Twelve Divine Generals, and Eight Bodhisattvas.

According to temple records, this principal image was originally the honzon of the Ryōbyōin (Hospital Temple), located at the northeast corner of the forty-nine sub-temples within Jetavana Monastery (Gion Shōja) in India. It is an image carved personally by Śākyamuni Buddha himself from sandalwood, made to his own likeness. When the monastery was on the verge of destruction, the image flew eastward.

Subsequently, in the third year of Chōtoku (997 CE), during the reign of Emperor Ichijō, strange lights appeared nightly over the sea at Karotsu Bay in Inaba Province (present-day Tottori Prefecture). The provincial governor, Tachibana no Yukihira, ordered fishermen to lower their nets and search the sea floor, whereupon they retrieved a brilliantly glowing statue of Yakushi Butsu. Seven years later, on the seventh day of the fourth month of Chōhō 5 (1003 CE), the statue suddenly flew to and alighted at Lord Yukihira's residence at Karasuma Takatsujii.

> The nimbus (halo) and pedestal remained behind in Inaba Province, where a temple called Zakōji was established and still exists today.

Lord Yukihira converted his residence into a Buddhist hall and enshrined the statue there. This is the origin of the present Inabadō. The founding patron was Lord Yukihira's son, the Zen master Kōchō, who also took charge of the temple's administration. On the eighth day of the fourth month of Jōan 1 (1171 CE), the temple received an imperial tablet from Retired Emperor Takakura and was given the name "Byōdōji." In Eiryaku 2 (1161 CE), Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa visited this site. The present hall was rebuilt by Ashikaga Yoshinori. A portrait image of Lord Tachibana no Yukihira is enshrined in the western chamber of the main hall, while a Yaksha deity is enshrined in the eastern chamber.

> There is a well in the rear hall, kept for use in the event of fire.

The tutelary deities of the temple were invited (kanjō) from eighteen shrines by imperial edict of Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa.

> Later, a divine oracle was received, and the deity Hiruko (Ebisu) was added, bringing the total to nineteen shrines.

The principal image of the Kannon Hall was made by the Great Master Jikaku (Ennin); within the hall are also enshrined Aizen Myōō (Rāgarāja) and the Great Master Kōbō (Kūkai). The bell tower is located to the west of the main hall and always has sacred straw ropes (shimenawa) stretched across it.

> Every year on the nineteenth day of the second month marks the anniversary of the first bell offering ceremony. It is a longstanding custom to stretch the shimenawa on this day.

The Yakuōin sub-temple, which manages the temple's affairs, enshrines Daikokuten.

> This sub-temple, as the Gion-in, has joint administration of the Gion Otabisho (temporary resting place for the Gion Festival procession) and the Shōshōi Shrine. Every year on the seventh day of the sixth month, festival children (chigo) depart from this location.

Kangiten (Vinayaka) and Fudō Myōō (Acala) are enshrined here. At the Momo-no-Bō and Yanagi-Bō sub-temples, three shrines—Inari, Himuro, and Akiba—are also venerated. Kokuzo Bosatsu (Akasagarbha) is also enshrined. The Nishi-no-Bō sub-temple enshrines Konpira. Keihōin has an Inari shrine and also enshrines Fudō Myōō and En-no-Gyōja. Chōhakuji Temple enshrines a naked-form Amida Butsu, created by the Great Master Jikaku at the wish of the Empress of Nijō as a testament to the possibility of women's attaining Buddhahood. The Amida Butsu of Kinrenji Temple is the work of the Kasuga sculptor.

> This image is said to be of the same type as the principal image of Kyogoku Seigani Temple.

Awashima Myōjin and Myōken (Bosatsu) are also enshrined here. At the corner sub-temple (Kado-no-Bō), Inari Daimyōjin is enshrined.

The principal image of this temple is one of the Three Tathāgatas of Japan (the other two being the principal image of Zenkōji Temple in Shinano Province and the Śākyamuni Buddha of Saga), and is a sacred image created during the lifetime of Śākyamuni Buddha himself. During the periodic unveilings (御戸開, go-tobiraki) of the image, solemn imperial ceremonies, religious services, and sacred music performances are held. In years when reigning emperors reach a traditional unlucky age (yakudoshi), imperial envoys visit every month to offer prayers. This practice is called "Yakushi-māri" (Yakushi pilgrimage).

**Hanjō Shrine**

Hanjō Shrine is located to the east of Takatsujii Shinmachi. The enshrined deity is Benzaiten (Sarasvatī, the goddess of fortune and arts).

> Currently, a Shingon Buddhist monk serves as its caretaker. The shrine serves as the tutelary deity (ubusunagami) of the surrounding temple gate town (monzenmachi). The annual festival is held on the twentieth day of the ninth month.
