## Zato Sekito (The Stone Stupa of the Blind Musicians)

Regarding what is called the "Zato Sekito": ever since Princess Amenoyo Naishinnō, daughter of the 58th sovereign Emperor Kōkō, lost her sight, she summoned blind women from throughout the capital to serve as her companions. Even those of humble status were granted official ranks and allowed to attend at her presence, which gave rise to the custom of calling them "goze" (御前, "before the honorable presence"). From that time on, blind men also came to be granted official ranks and called "zato" (座頭), and the practice of appointing them to the offices of kengyō and kōtō originated as a tradition bequeathed by this princess.

Every year on the sixteenth day of the second month, which is the princess's memorial day, the zato gather in assembly, pay their respects before her portrait, proceed to the eastern riverbank, and pile stones there to express their gratitude. This is called "Sekito" (stone stupa). They also hold an assembly on the twenty-fourth day of the sixth month, which is called the "Zato no納涼" (the blind musicians' summer cooling). This too is said to be a memorial observance.

Today, there is an assembly hall to the north of Gojo-bōmon on Takakura Street, and the two sites of stone-piling ceremonies are now held here, where participants play the biwa and recite the Tale of the Heike, and carry out various traditional ceremonies to observe this memorial rite.

Furthermore, after Princess Amenoyo passed away, out of compassion for the blind musicians who might suffer from cold and hunger, she had a tenement built in Sameushi in the capital to provide for them. The saying among the children of Kyoto — "ameuji mekura ga tsue tsusite tōru" (the blind of Ameji pass by with their canes) — refers, it is said, to these blind inhabitants of Sameushi.

> The details of Princess Amenoyo Naishinnō are not clearly recorded in the imperial genealogies, and this matter should be investigated further.

## The Evening Cool at Shijo Kawara

The evening cool (yūsuzumi) at Shijo Kawara begins on the seventh day of the sixth month and concludes on the eighteenth. From the pleasure quarters (seirō) on both the east and west banks, platforms are set up along the river's edge; lanterns shine like stars, and along the riverbed, folding stools are lined up for banquets held in the shimmering light of the current. Deep-purple hats flutter in the river breeze, and handsome young men in the bright moonlight half-dazzle the eye as they hold up their fans with an alluring and elegant grace, drawing the heart ever closer so that one cannot look away. The courtesans, in the full bloom of their beauty and adorned beyond what even the lotus flower could rival, diffuse the delicate fragrance of orchid and musk as they stroll south and north. Resting at tea stalls, one dispels the haze of drink with the scent of kerria blossoms, and in the herbal drinks (kōsen), one scoops water from the Kamo River to appreciate the lightness of Kyoto's water.

Witty conversation surpasses even Guo Xiang of the Jin dynasty, flowing like a torrent of water poured from a great height. Mimicry is said to be second to none — not even to the passes of Hanguguan. Monkey performances, dog wrestling, trick riding, acrobatic feats, and tightrope walking by a "kirin" evoke the image of a swing (shūsen), and the clamoring voices fill the air. On the left, shops have waterfalls cascading noisily to ward off the heat; the tinkling of glass reverberates coolly, summoning a breeze. Famous birds from China and Japan, fierce beasts from deep mountains, all gather here for people of all ranks to view and enjoy. The festive riverside revelry follows the precedent of the purification river (misogi-gawa), dispersing troublesome spirits like small flies, and is indeed a continuation of the summer purification ritual (natsugare) taught by the Gozu Tennō (Ox-Head Heavenly King) to Somin Shōrai.

> In the days of the Great Imperial Palace, all the court officials assembled at the Suzakumon Gate together to perform the purification ritual [haraibi­to]. This was called "ara[hito] no harae." In the four seasons, creation generally follows the cycle of mutual generation (sōshō); however, [summer alone] does not proceed by mutual generation but transitions directly to autumn, and because the fire element (kaki) overcomes the metal element (kinki), purification rites are performed to pacify this fire energy.
