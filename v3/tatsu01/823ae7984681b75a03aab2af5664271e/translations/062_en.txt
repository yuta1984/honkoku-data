## Ebisu Shrine

The Ebisu Shrine stands before the gates of Kennin-ji Temple. Its enshrined deity is Hiruko no Mikoto, and the hall was established through the prayers of National Master Eisai.
　　　It serves as the guardian shrine (ubusuna) of the Kennin-ji precinct, and the annual festival is held on the sixteenth day of the ninth month.

## Tōgakuzan Nembutsu-ji Temple

Tōgakuzan Nembutsu-ji Temple is located to the west of Rokuhara Mitsu-ji Temple.
　　　In former times, this area was called Atago-sato, but now the name survives only in connection with this temple, and people generally refer to it as "Atago-dera."

It belongs to the Shingon sect, and was founded by Kōbō Daishi (Kūkai), with Senkan Naikū as its restorer. The principal image of Kannon (Bodhisattva of Compassion) is a work of Senkan. The flanking attendants are Bishamonten and Jizō Bosatsu, statues carved by Senkan Naikū himself. Senkan was of the Tachibana clan, the son of Middle Counselor Yoriaki, the governor of Sagami Province. His childhood name was Senkan-maru. Upon coming of age, he entered the cell of Unshō Naikū on Mount Hiei, took holy orders, and became a distinguished scholar of both Exoteric and Esoteric Buddhism. Throughout his life, he never ceased reciting the six-character Buddha's name (*Namu Amida Butsu*). For this reason, he came to be called the "Nembutsu Shōnin" (Saint of the Nembutsu). The hall also enshrines a Jizō Bosatsu. This statue is known as the "Fire-Warding Jizō," and every year on the second day of the first month, sutras are read and protective talismans against fire are distributed to worshippers. This ceremony is called the "Tengu Feast."

**The Carriage-Rest Pine**
　　　It is said that Senkan Naikū would always travel by carriage and would draw his carriage up to this pine tree.

## Fudarakusan Rokuhara Mitsu-ji Temple

Fudarakusan Rokuhara Mitsu-ji Temple is located to the west of the Rokudō crossroads. It belongs to the Shingon sect and is affiliated with Chishaku-in. The principal image is a standing eleven-faced Kannon, one *jō* (approximately three meters) in height, carved by Saint Kūya.
　　　It is the seventeenth sacred site of the Saigoku (Western Provinces) Pilgrimage of Thirty-Three Temples, and also one of the temples on the Rakuyō Kannon Circuit.

According to tradition:
In the fifth year of Tenryaku (951 CE), during the reign of Emperor Murakami, a plague spread throughout the capital and countless people perished. Saint Kūya, moved by compassion, carved an image of the eleven-faced Kannon, placed it on a cart, and personally pulled it through the streets of the capital. The ceremonial tea (*tencha*) offered to the Kannon—the principal image of this very temple—was given to the sick, and all of them recovered. Emperor Murakami heard of this and declared it an auspicious precedent, partaking of the tea every year at New Year (*Ganzan*). The common people continue this practice to this day, calling it *Ōbuku* ("Royal Drink"), and it is said to ward off illness throughout the year.

To the north, a Jizō Bosatsu is enshrined.
　　　In former times, this statue was venerated as the principal image, and the hall was known as the "Rokuhara Jizō-dō."

As recorded in Yasuyori's *Hōbutsushū*:
There was a poor woman in the Eastern Hills. She had long been a devotee of this Jizō Temple. This woman had an elderly mother, who one day passed away. The woman was deeply troubled, wondering how she might arrange for the burial. One evening, a wandering monk appeared and asked why she was lamenting so. She explained the situation in full detail. The monk said, "That is an easy matter," and carefully wrapping the body, carried it on his back to the mountains, where he performed the funeral rites. The woman was overcome with gratitude beyond words. Believing this to have been the work of the Jizō of Rokuhara, she went to worship before the statue, and found that the Jizō's feet were covered with soil. From this episode, the Jizō came to be called both the "Mountain-Sending Jizō" and the "Wig-Hanging Jizō," because the statue was holding the old mother's hairpiece in its hand.

To the south, a Yakushi Buddha (Medicine Buddha) is enshrined, a work attributed to Dengyō Daishi. In the Founder's Hall stands a self-carved image of Saint Kūya. The "Sugatami Pond" (Mirror Pond) is said to be where the Saint reflected his image in the water to carve his self-portrait.

## Akoya-zuka (Akoya's Mound)

　　　Located to the north of the main hall, this is the burial mound of Akoya, a courtesan of Gojō-zaka. Atop it stand an unmarked stone pagoda and a small Benzaiten shrine.

**When Saint Kūya was in retreat at the Grand Shrine of Ise, a noble voice issued from the shrine, bearing the following verse:**

　　　*Mida tanomu hito wo munashiku nasu naraba / Ware kono kuni no kami to iware ji*

　　　(If I were to abandon in vain those who place their trust in Amida, / Then I could not be called a deity of this land.)
