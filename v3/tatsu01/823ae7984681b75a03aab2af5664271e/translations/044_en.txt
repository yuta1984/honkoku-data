## Shiunzan Gokurakuin Kōshōji (Kūya-dō)

Shiunzan Gokurakuin Kōshōji is located in Tatakichō, to the east of Horikawa on Shijō-Bōmon street. It is known by the name Kūya-dō (Kūya Hall). The inscription "Kūya-dō" on the plaque above the main gate is written by the Ōbaku Zen monk Kōsen. The plaque reading "Gokurakuin" on the main hall is in the hand of the Takeuchi monzeki (abbot of imperial lineage).

The sect is called the Nembutsu sect (念仏宗). The principal object of worship in the main hall is a statue carved by Saint Kūya himself. The flanking attendants are Jizō (Kṣitigarbha) and Bishamonten (Vaiśravaṇa). On the north side altar, a seated Amida Buddha is enshrined — a work attributed to Gyōki. There is also a statue of Saint Kūya, made by the monk Jōsei from the ash of an incense burner.

On the south altar, a statue of the monk Jōsei is enshrined.

Saint Kūya was the second prince of Emperor Engi (Emperor Daigo). His aspiration to dwell in a state of non-action beyond the worldly realm grew ever deeper, and he finally took religious vows, leaving behind his palace of jewels and gold, to live quietly in reclusion deep in the mountains of Kurama in the northern hills. Deer would come every night to comfort his tranquil meditation. The saint cherished them deeply and was greatly moved by their cries. One day, the deer did not come. Soon, a man named Taira no Jōsei arrived, bringing a deer he had hunted, saying he had killed it in these very mountains. The saint was deeply grieved. He took the carcass, made a fur garment from its hide, and inserted its antlers into the head of his walking staff, which he always carried with him. The hunter Jōsei was converted by the saint's spiritual virtue, became his disciple, and together with his wife and children — wearing lay clothing with unshaven head (the garment being of the Tendai style) — he would strike a gourd and sing the wasan (devotional hymns) composed by the saint. During the cold midwinter, night after night, he wandered through the go-sanmai (cremation grounds) and the city streets, cultivating the causes of rebirth in the Pure Land.

Today, within the precincts of Kūya-dō there are eight establishments known as "hachi-tataki" (bowl-strikers). They are: Tokushō-an, Konkō-bō, Jushō-bō, Tōbō, Shōtoku-bō, Riseido, Nanbō, and Seigan-bō. All are descendants of the monk Jōsei. They are engaged in the craft of making tea kettles.

**Poem presented by the saint to the monk Jōsei:**

> *Yamakawa no sue ni nagaruru tochikara mo / mi wo sutete koso ukamu se mo are*
> (Even as water flows to the end of mountain rivers, / only by abandoning oneself can one find a ford to float upon.)
> — Saint Kūya

**From the Senzaishū (anthology):**

> *Gokuraku wa harukeki hodo to kikishika to / tsutomete itaru tokoro nari keri*
> (I had heard that Paradise was a far distant place, / yet it is a place one reaches through diligent practice.)
> — Same author

---

Saint Kūya was born in the third year of Engi (903). The exact month and day are unknown. He passed away on the eleventh day of the eleventh month of the third year of Tenroku (972), at Hachiyō-ji in Kurokawa-gō, Aizu, Ōshū (present-day Fukushima Prefecture). He was seventy years of age.

He departed from the capital toward the eastern provinces on the thirteenth day of the eleventh month. On this day each year, a "joyful leaping nembutsu" ceremony is performed at this temple.

### Five Treasures of This Temple

**The Broken Wani-guchi (Clapper Bell) — one piece**

When the saint went on retreat at the Kamo Shrine, the deity of the shrine appeared and, as proof of the teaching that all sentient beings of the latter age could attain rebirth in the Pure Land through the nembutsu, split the wani-guchi (bronze clapper bell) of the treasure hall in two. The left half was given to the saint; the right half remained at the Kamo Shrine.

**The Robe-Exchanged Wani-guchi**

The deity of Matsuo Shrine appeared in the form of an old man and came to see the saint, saying: "Of late, I have received the Hannya-kyō (Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra) services offered as divine provisions, but have not yet tasted the profound essence of the wondrous dharma. Because of this I have grown thin and weak, and the wind passes through my robes. I hear that the saint reads the Lotus Sūtra most excellently — do you not have compassion for me?" Upon hearing this, Kūya said: "I shall remove this robe. I have worn this garment while reciting the Lotus Sūtra for forty years. Its wondrous fragrance has permeated these robes. I will offer them to you." The old man was delighted and put on the robe. This robe is the deer-hide garment. It is said to be at the Matsuo Shrine to this day.

**The Illustrated Biography (E-kotoba-den)**

This is the biography of Saint Kūya. It is written by Sonnō Hosshin-nō, the monzeki (imperial-lineage abbot) of Shōren-in. The postscript is a collaborative work by court nobles; the illustrations are by Kaihō Yūsetsu.

**The Kesa (Priestly Robe)**

A gift donated by Tōfukumonin (consort of Emperor Go-Mizunoo).

## Shinsen-en (神泉苑 — Sacred Spring Garden)

Shinsen-en is located to the west of Ōmiya on Oike-dōri street. It belongs to the Shingon sect, under the administration of Tō-ji Hōbodai-in. The Zennyo Ryūō (Virtuous Dragon King) Shrine is on the island in the pond. The annual festival is held on the first day of the eighth month.

The two-storied pagoda enshrines the Great Sun Buddha (Dainichi Nyorai) as its principal deity. The pond is called Hōjōju-ike (Pond of Dharma's Fulfillment). In ancient times, during the era of the Great Imperial Palace, the grounds were vast in extent and served as a place of imperial recreation.

The Shūkaishō records: "South of Nijō, west of Jōgū, eight chō; north of Sanjō, north of Mibu, east of Mibu..."
