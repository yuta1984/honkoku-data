**Gojō Tenjin-gū Shrine** (Located on Matsubara-dōri at Nishitōin; also known as Tenshi-sha)

The enshrined deity is Sukunahikona no Mikoto, with Amaterasu Ōmikami and Ōnamuchi no Mikoto enshrined in the same hall (aiden).

This shrine was established at the time of Emperor Kanmu's relocation of the capital to Heiankyō (Kyoto), as a guardian shrine of the new city, and is venerated as the ancestral deity of medicine. In former times, the shrine buildings were magnificent and imposing, and the shrine held a domain of four chō (city blocks) from east to west and five chō from north to south, with dense groves of trees surrounding the grounds. According to the shrine records, both Great Masters Dengyō Daishi and Kōbō Daishi offered prayers here for a safe voyage when they traveled to Tang China and upon their return. According to the Genpei Seisuiki (Chronicle of the Rise and Fall of the Minamoto and Taira), in the second year of Jōan (1172), when the monk Mongaku was sent into exile, he buried gold beneath the torii gate of this shrine as a stratagem to avoid a perilous storm. In the first year of Angen (1175), the young Minamoto no Ushiwakamaru (later known as Yoshitsune) fought with Kiichi Hōgen over a grievance concerning a military treatise, and it was here that he instantly received divine inspiration and won the battle. It is also said that it was in this grove that he encountered Musashibō Benkei. In the first year of Itoku (1384), Shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu rebuilt the shrine structures. The festival is held on the tenth day of the ninth month.

On the occasion of Setsubun, atractylis (okera herb), small rice cakes (komochi), and treasure ship talismans (takarabune) are presented to the Imperial Palace (Kinri).
The provision for the small rice cakes was granted through the patronage of Keijuin, the mother of Shogun Ashikaga Yoshiteru, in the second year of Tenbun (1533). From that time until the present day, the cost of these offerings has been provided annually as an official public duty. On this night, crowds of people gather to pray for the removal of misfortune and disasters, and receive the three sacred offerings (okera, rice cakes, and treasure ships).

**Ichion-ji Temple** (Adjacent to the west of Tenshi-sha)

The principal image (honzon) is an eleven-faced Kannon (Ekādaśamukha Avalokiteśvara), measuring three shaku and seven sun in height, and is said to have been created by Kōbō Daishi. During the reign of Emperor Junna, epidemics of various kinds spread throughout the land. The Emperor, for the sake of all people, dispatched golden-coin envoys (kinpei-shi) to both the Ise and Kasuga shrines, and in accordance with a divine oracle, the Kannon image from Hasedera Temple in Yamato Province (present-day Nara Prefecture) was replicated, and Emperor commanded Kōbō Daishi to create this venerable image.

This temple is the twenty-fifth station on the Rakuyō Kannon Pilgrimage. It is also known as the deity of smallpox (Hōsō-gami).

In the second year of Taian (848), during the reign of Emperor Montoku, a man named Hoshinohara Masatoshi, while crossing the sea to the kingdom of Silla (on the Korean peninsula), fell ill with smallpox (hōsō). Masatoshi prayed fervently to the principal image of this temple, and the miraculous response was profound—he recovered suddenly and returned home safely. Thereafter, people began to enshrine the [deity] here for the cure of smallpox.
