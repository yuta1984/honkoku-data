**Shiogama Shrine**
Shiogama Shrine is located to the west of Hongaku-ji Temple and serves as the amulet guardian of Jōtoku-ji Temple. The enshrined deity is Minister of the Left Tōru (Minamoto no Tōru), and the shrine is called Shiogama-yama. The principal image, an Amida Buddha, was crafted by the Yawata master, and the temple was founded by the Venerable Denyō.

---

**Taishi-dō / Byakugō-ji Temple**
Byakugō-ji Temple lies to the south of Jōtoku-ji. It is known by the name Sokujōju-in (Temple of Speedy Fulfillment). Its Buddhist denomination is the Ritsu sect. The principal image is a self-carved statue of Prince Shōtoku in the form of "Namubutsu" (Homage to the Buddha), measuring over one shaku (approx. 30 cm) in length. The Four Heavenly Kings on the side altar are said to be of Chinese (Tang) workmanship. The temple was founded by the Vinaya master Ninshō. Originally, it was located to the north of the middle gate of Chion-in, behind Kōgen-in. Even now, an ancient well remains on that original site, called "Taishi-sui" (Prince's Water). During the Keichō era (1596–1615), when Chion-in was rebuilt, the temple was relocated to its present location.

---

**Raigō-dō / Shin-Zenkōji Temple**
Shin-Zenkōji Temple is to the south of Hongaku-ji. Its principal image, an Amida Buddha, is said to be the same body as that of Zenkōji Temple in Shinano Province (present-day Nagano). Honda Yoshisuke received a divine revelation from the Tathāgata, traveled to the kingdom of Baekje (ancient Korea), presented seven kin of Jambu-nada gold to King Saimei, returned to Japan, cast a statue of the Tathāgata, and constructed an altar furnace. From within its light, a manifestation of the revered image appeared — this is the principal image of this temple.

---

**Nagori-wakare Amida Buddha**
The Nagori-wakare (Parting in Reluctance) Amida Buddha is at Renkōji Temple, to the south of Raigō-dō. This principal image dates from the Katei era (1235–1238), when a high-ranking monk from the eastern provinces came to the capital and commissioned the Buddhist sculptor An'ami (Kaikei) to create an Amida Buddha statue. When the statue was completed and the monk was about to depart, An'ami, finding the image exceedingly rare and precious, was deeply reluctant to part with it and followed after the monk, catching up with him in Yamashina. When he told the monk of his feelings, the monk opened his carrying box (oi) and the statue miraculously split into two identical images. Both men marveled at this wonder, and each took one of the two statues, departing in opposite (east and west) directions. The place is still called "Nagori-wakare-yama" (Mountain of Reluctant Parting) in Yamashina. An'ami carried his image home with great reluctance, and it became the principal image of this temple.

**Uma-dome Jizō (Horse-Stopping Jizō)**
This is a stone Buddha at the same temple. When this image was buried in the ground, Taira no Kiyomori once happened to pass by on horseback, but his horse stopped and refused to move forward. Wondering at this strange occurrence, the stone image appeared (was unearthed). It is said that it was thereafter kept at the execution ground on the Rokujō riverbank.

---

**Portrait of Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa**
The imperial portrait (shinkei) of Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa is in the Long Hall (Nagadō) to the south of Raigō-dō. This temple was founded by the Retired Emperor himself, who made frequent imperial visits and, without distinction of high or low birth, recorded the names of deceased souls whose voices reached his imperial ears in a memorial register, performing memorial services and conducting regular Buddhist lectures. For this reason, it was called the "Chōkō-dō" (Hall of Long Lectures).

　　As the Tale of the Heike states: "Thus, in the death register of the Chōkō-dō of Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa, the souls of Gio, Ginyo, Hotoke, and Toji were all entered together in one place."

**Tenman-gū Shrine of Mannen-ji Temple**
The Tenman-gū Shrine of Mannen-ji Temple is to the south of Chōkō-dō. It is said to have originally been located to the south of Ton-no-chō, along Mannen-ji Street. On its former site, an old plum tree with red blossoms still survives.

---

**Kitō Ten'nō (Demon-Head Deity)**
Kitō Ten'nō is enshrined within the hall of Chikurin-in, to the southeast of Hongaku-ji.

In the spring of the second year of Shōan (1300), when Retired Emperor Fushimi made an imperial visit to this mountain, a northern guard (hokumen) warrior named Kuzuhara Hyōbu Shigekiyo accompanied him and fell in love at first sight with a court lady called Asagiri, entering into a conjugal relationship with her. He later also took Yaehime as his wife, whereupon Asagiri, consumed by deep jealousy, refused all food and drink and died. Shigekiyo, taking this as a cause for spiritual awakening, renounced the world, traveled to Futaoni in Kii Province, built a hermitage there, took the religious name Kurakubō, and devoted himself to ascetic practice. In time, he fell ill with a plague and suffered greatly. At that moment, the vengeful spirit of Asagiri appeared as a demon woman and touched Kurakubō's head, whereupon he was instantly cured. Through the accumulation of merit, both attained Buddhahood together. As proof for future generations, the head (of the demon) was left behind and enshrined as Kitō Ten'nō (the Demon-Head Deity).

**Tachibana Yukihira's Mound**
Located at Tōzen-ji Temple, to the south of Chikurin-in.

**Ichinaka-yama Kinkō-ji Temple**
Kinkō-ji belongs to the Ji sect (Jishū). Its principal image, an Amida Buddha, was crafted by Jōchō, and the temple was founded by the Venerable Kūji (Kūji Shōnin). It was originally located to the north of Horikawa and Shichijō, on what is now within the precincts of Hongan-ji Temple. In ancient times, as this area was a merchants' marketplace, the temple was also called "Ichiya Dōjō" (Marketplace Hall).

　　**Ichihime Shrine**
　　Located within this temple precinct. It is regarded as the guardian tutelary shrine of this temple. Festival: the 13th day of the 5th month.

　　**Tenshini Well**
　　Located to the west of the main hall. It is one of the famous waters of Rakuyō (Kyoto).

---

**Enjū-ji Temple**
Enjū-ji is to the south of Kinkō-ji. The principal images are three bronze Buddhas — Dainichi (Mahāvairocana), Shaka (Śākyamuni), and Amida (Amitābha) — crafted by Unkei. Originally located on Abura-no-kōji (Oil Lane) at Gojō, the area is now called Kinbutsu-chō (Gold Buddha Town).
