**The Great Torii Gate** stands on Kasugano (the Kasuga plain).
　(According to the *Kasuga Koki* [Old Records of Kasuga]: Two vermilion torii pillars were erected with a single green willow tree placed alongside them.) A poem composed about this place:
　　*With shide streamers tied to sacred sakaki leaves and swept about, not a cloud or mist of impurity remains upon my body.*

**Matashi no Hashi (Horse-mounting Bridge)** is located a short distance east of the torii.
　(According to the *Kasuga Koki*):
　　*O mighty one — drawing near the black steed of Kai and mounting it, riding with vigor across the plain of Kasugano.*

**The site of the Two Pagodas** lies to the north of Matashi no Hashi. The Eastern Pagoda, designated "Hon Gogan" (the Original Votive Pagoda), was built in the second year of Ten'an (858) by the Minister of Somedono, Fujiwara no Yoshifusa. Its principal images are of Shakyamuni, Yakushi, Jizō, and Kannon. These were sacred Buddhist images of red sandalwood (shakusendanzō) brought back from China by an envoy to Tang China in the third year of Jōgan (861). The Western Pagoda, designated "Shin Gogan" (the New Votive Pagoda), was constructed by the former Archbishop Kakushō, and like the Eastern Pagoda enshrined four silver Buddhist images. Later, a Mañjuśrī (Monju) image was added to make five Buddhas, all mounted upon lion pedestals.
　(In the eighteenth year of Ōei [1411], both pagodas were struck by lightning and reduced to ashes. However, the sacred Buddhist images survived unharmed, and when the Chōkōdō hall was rebuilt, they were moved and enshrined there.)
　(As recorded in Saigyō's *Senshūshō*):
　　*The scenery of Kasugano — the two pagodas standing there — how I must have thundered across Matashi Bridge with trembling steps.*
　　*If there is a connection to the young purple [lavender], one picks violets; upon the jeweled bamboo grass of Ogasawara, jeweled hailstones pile up, making them hard to gather. The green of the pines of Kataoka — does it not hold the color of a thousand ages for your sake?* (And so on.)
　(Kataoka is likely somewhere in this vicinity, though the exact location is unclear.)

**The Mikurumaya (Temporary Shrine) of the Wakamiya** lies to the east of the Great Torii Gate, and is also called the "Kasuga Ankyū" (Kasuga Temporary Palace). In ordinary times there is no shrine building here; the dew rests on the turf, the silver grasses are frost-withered, and there is not even a path through the fallen leaves. However, during the sacred festival of the eleventh month (Shimotsuki), black-wood pillars are erected with eaves of green pine needles, and a temporary shrine is built, to which the Wakamiya deity is ceremonially transferred (togyo).
　(Every year on the eleventh day of the eighth month, the timber needed for this temporary shrine is harvested from various locations throughout Yamato Province, following established custom, and transported to the Kasuga repair magistrate. On the first day of the ninth month, there is a ceremony called the "Onawamune no Shikirei" [ridge-binding rite], performed by the senior craftsman of Kasuga. On the twenty-first day of the eleventh month, the shrine structure is constructed. Sixty-four loads of pine timber are contributed by the seven villages and sixteen districts of the surrounding area. Thirty-six rush mats (omoko) are offered alternately by the villages of the Ikoma valley. Craftsmen wear eboshi caps and suō robes while performing these repairs and constructions.)

**Yukike no Sawa (Snow-melt Marsh)** is a narrow path leading from the eastern side of the Mikurumaya grounds toward the south.
　(From the *Fūga Wakashū* [Poetry anthology]):
　　*When spring comes, I trail my sleeves in the snow-melt marsh and pick still-tender young greens.* — Emperor Sutoku
　(From the *Horikawa Tarō Hyakushu* [Hundred Poems of Horikawa Tarō]):
　　*In the snow-melt marsh of Kasugano, brushing my sleeves [through the water], I pick water celery for your sake.* — Nakazane

**Izakawa (Rate River)** refers to the thin stream flowing to the east of the path. It is the river where pilgrims wash their hands. In former times, ritual purification (misogi) was performed here. This place is called "Rokudō" (the Deer Path) because it is said to be the route along which the deity of Kasuga Myōjin traveled, riding upon a deer. In Priest Saigyō's *Senshūshō*, this place is written as "Rokudō" (六道, the Six Paths of Existence).

**Zenshū Bridge**, **Kurumaya-dono**, **Goi Bridge**, and **the Second Torii Gate** and other landmarks lie further ahead along this road.
