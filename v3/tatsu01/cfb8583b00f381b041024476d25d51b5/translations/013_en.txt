【Right Page】

The imperial capital of Heijō (Nara) was first established at Nara by Empress Genmei, mother of Emperor Monmu, in the second year of Wadō (709 CE).
　《Note: The city was also written as 那羅, 諾樂, 寕樂, 乃樂, 平, or 楢, all read "Nara."》 In the following year (Wadō 3), the capital was officially moved there, and seven successive emperors 《Note: Genmei, Genshō, Shōmu, Kōken, the Deposed Emperor (Junnin), Shōtoku, and Kōnin》 maintained their imperial residence at Nara.
　Emperor Kanmu relocated the capital to Nagaoka Palace in Yamashiro Province in the third year of Enryaku (784), and then moved again to Heiankyō (Kyoto) in the thirteenth year of Enryaku (794). The Left Capital (Sakyō) refers to present-day Nantō (Nara), while the Right Capital (Ukyō) refers to the Western Capital.

　《From the Man'yōshū》
　　　The capital of Nara, fragrant with the colors of blossoming flowers—how glorious it is now in full splendor!

　《From the Shin Chokusen-shū》
　　　The lodge built of dark timber in the capital of Nara—though I tire of seeing it, I can never tire of it enough.　　Emperor Shōmu

　《From the Shūi-shū》
　　　Oh, the wild geese flying through the sky—when at last might I send a message to the capital of Nara?　　Hitomaro (Kakinomoto no Hitomaro)

　The site of the former imperial palace is not located within the present town of Nara, but rather to the west of Kōfuku-ji, in the Chōshōji district, south of Nijō village, to the southeast (tatsumi direction) of the highway, where there is a plot of land called "Tsuiji no Uchi" (Inside the Earthen Wall). Even today, no rice is cultivated there. Also at this spot stands a small shrine called "Dairi no Miya" (Shrine of the Imperial Palace).

　《From the Kokin-shū》　《Note: A poem by the Nara Emperor; according to a commentary, this refers to Emperor Heizei, the Emperor of the Daidō era.》
　　　In the old capital of Nara, now become a distant hometown, the flowers bloom with colors as brilliant as ever.

　《From the Hōan Hyakushu, on the theme of violets》
　　　In the capital of Nara, where violets bloom, all that remains as a memento is the stone foundation.　　Minamoto no Nakamasā

　《From the Zoku-shūi-shū》
　　　The cuckoo, bearing the name of the nara oak leaves, of this ancient palace—let it tell the tales of the long-ago past, age after age.　　Hōin Kōchō

【Left Page】

Kasuga Field (Kasugano) refers to the area from the Great Torii gate eastward to the Kasuga Shrine. The reason this place is named "Kasuga" derives from ancient times of the Age of the Gods, when Amaterasu Ōmikami subdued 790,000 evil deities and restored righteousness to all hearts, bringing peace to all people. Because of this, the divine spirit of Amaterasu—pure, harmonious, and tranquil—was likened to the gentle warmth of a spring day (haru no hi), and the deity rejoiced, naming the place "Kasuga" (Spring Day). The surrounding village was likewise named "Kasuga."
　Furthermore, this Kasuga Forest was granted to the deity Amenokoyane-no-mikoto (referred to here as the elder of Kototamusubi-no-mikoto's lineage), who was praised as the "Lord of Kasuga." At the base of Mount Mikasa, the shrine pillars were firmly planted in the bedrock, and the four shrine pavilions are splendid and magnificent. There is never a moment without worshippers, and divine miracles manifest anew each day.

　《From the Kokin-shū》
　　　Through the gaps in the snow of Kasuga Field, barely visible as it grows forth—is that you, my love, like the tender new grass?　　Mibu no Tadamine

　《From the Shūi-shū》
　　　Just as the warbler begins to sing, on this day alone the snow of Kasuga Field appears as blossoms.　　Tadafusa

　《From the Shin Kokin-shū》
　　　Along the path through the small oak grove of Kasuga Field, even the hidden spring water—let it show at least some sign of the gods' presence.　　Fujiwara no Toshinarī (Shunzei)

　《From the Eight Views of Nara》　《Deer of Kasuga Field》
　　　(Chinese verse) No odor, no sound—the colors of the field are beautiful. One simply watches the great deer and fawns grazing and resting.
　　　The deep mountains are like the realm of the sage-king Shun; this place is one with the sacred park of literary virtue. This land has been set apart by the holy gods.　　Kajūji Sangi Udaiben Tsuneshige

　《Same theme》
　　　How cold must the gale blowing from the peaks of Kasuga Mountain be—at the foot of the hills, in the meadows, the deer cry out.　　Shimizu no Gon-chūnagon Kimitatsu
