### [Right Page]

**[The Sacred Water of Nigatsudō (二月堂)]**

While the names of the various deities were being read aloud and memorial rites performed, the deity Onyū Myōjin of Wakasa Province appeared at this assembly and declared to the head monk: "If you please, I shall offer the sacred water (*aka*)." Thereupon, two cormorants — one black, one white — flew out from within the rocks below, and from the place where they had been, sweet pure water welled up and began to flow. In a year of drought, when the sacred water dried up, the assembled monks gathered at the well, turned toward the direction of Wakasa, and prayed. Before their eyes, the water filled to the brim. This occurs every year on the night of the twelfth day of the Second Month. At that time, the stream of the Mitarashi River before the Onyū Shrine in Wakasa ceases to flow and falls silent. For this reason, it was named *Otonashi-gawa* ("the Soundless River").

> [Marginal note] It is written in Buddhist records (*shakusho*) that drinking this sacred water (*aka*) cures all manner of illness. Also, the two cormorants are enshrined as deities, and the place is called "Cormorant Shrine" (*U-no-miya*).

> [Marginal note] Located to the south of the well. The northern shrine of Nigatsudō is called Onyū Myōjin; the southern shrine is called Onjiki Myōjin.

**Kisaragi Falls (二月滝 / *Kisaragi no taki*)**

> [Marginal note] This is the ritual purification site (*kori-ba*) for Nigatsudō. It is now located to the northeast (*ushitora*) of Hokkedō.

> [Caption: "Composed while in retreat at Nigatsudō"] *(Matsuo Bashō)*

> *Mizutori ya / komori no sō no / kutsu no oto*
> (The water-drawing ceremony — / the sound of the retreating monks' / wooden clogs)
> — **Bashō**

**Hokkedō (法華堂)**, commonly called *Sangatsudō* ("Third-Month Hall"), is also known as Kinshōji [per *Shakusho*], Konshukuji [per *Gojunreiki*], and Kenjaku-dō. It was founded by the high monk Rōben in the fifth year of Tenpyō (733). The principal image is the Fukūkenjaku Kannon, carved by Rōben himself. To the east stands a Fudō-son, and to the west a Jizō Bosatsu, both said to be the work of Empress Kōmyō. The Daikokuten is said to have been made by Kōbō Daishi (Kūkai).

**Sanmaidō (三昧堂)**, commonly called *Shigatsudō* ("Fourth-Month Hall"), was perhaps given this name because it follows in succession after Nigatsudō and Sangatsudō. The principal images are Fugen Bosatsu, the Eleven-Faced Kannon (*Jūichimen Kannon*), Fudō Myōō, and Bishamonten.

### [Left Page]

**The Tutelary Hachiman Shrine (鎮守八幡宮)**: In the twelfth month of the first year of Tenpyō Shōhō (749), a new shrine was built at Nashihara-no-miya and established as a divine sanctuary; it was later relocated to the vicinity of the Great Buddha Hall, and subsequently, by order of the Lord of Saimyōji of Kamakura [Hōjō Tokiyori], it was moved and enshrined to the south of Sangatsudō.

**Sorigami-no-tō (剃髪塔, "Hair-Offering Pagoda")**
> [Marginal note] Located before the shrine; a stone pagoda in which the shorn hair of Emperor Shōmu is enshrined.

**The Lecture Hall (講堂)**: Its remains are the foundation stones behind the Great Buddha Hall. When first constructed during the Tenpyō Shōhō era (749–757), the principal image was a thousand-armed Kannon (*Senju Kannon*) five *jō* in height. It is said that during the memorial service attended by ten thousand monks, celestial beings descended from heaven, scattering flowers while a wondrous fragrance filled the air. The hall was destroyed in the upheaval of the Eiroku era, and now only the foundation stones remain.

**Tamukeyama (手向山)**, commonly called *Hachiman-yama* ("Hachiman Mountain"):

> [Marginal note] On this mountain there is an ancient burial mound of Lord Yoshimine no Yasuyo, Grand Counselor and Governor of Musashi, known as "Musashi-zuka." It is also said that the lord was revered and enshrined as a deity.

> [Note: From the *Kokinshū*; composed at Tamukeyama when the Suzaku-in visited Nara]

> *Kono tabi wa / nusa mo toriaezu / tamukeyama /
> momiji no nishiki / kami no manimani*
>
> On this journey / I could not even prepare offerings —
> At Tamukeyama / may the brocade of autumn leaves /
> be at the gods' disposal.
> — **Sugawara no Ason** *(Sugawara no Michizane)*

> [From the *Shokugosen*:]

> *Tamukeyama / momiji no nusa wa / chiriniken /
> yuki no shirayū / kakenu hi zo naki*
>
> At Tamukeyama / the crimson-leaf offerings / have scattered away;
> not a day passes / without white sacred streamers of snow being hung.
> — **Nyūdō Sesshō Daijō-daijin** *(Retired regent and Chancellor)*

> [From the *Shokusenzai*:]

> *Momiji o mo / hana o mo oreru / kokoro o ba /
> tamuke no yama no / kami ya shiruran*
>
> The heart that breaks off / both autumn leaves and blossoms — /
> does the god of / Tamuke Mountain / know of it?
> — **Tsurayuki** *(Ki no Tsurayuki)*

---

**Musashino (武蔵野)** is a place at the foot of Wakakusa-yama where pine trees grow thickly. It is called by this name because of the presence of Musashi-zuka.

> [From the *Ise Monogatari*:]

> *Musashino wa / kyō wa na yaki so / wakakusa no /
> tsuma mo komore ri / ware mo komore ri*
>
> Do not set fire to Musashino today! /
> Among the young grasses / my spouse is hidden, /
> and I too am hidden.

> [Marginal note: According to the *Kokinshū-shō*, this poem was composed by the consort Nijō-no-kisaki when Ariwara no Narihira abducted her and took her to the old capital of Nara.]
