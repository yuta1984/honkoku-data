## [Right Page]

Hearing one thing and understanding ten, Ryōben entered the Hossō school of Buddhism and attained the innermost teachings of the Kegon (*Avatamsaka*) doctrine. He became the monk of personal devotion to Emperor Shōmu, and the Great Buddha Hall of Tōdai-ji and other edifices were built at his urging. In the fourth year of Tenpyō Hōji (760), he was appointed Bishop (*Sōjō*), and on the sixteenth day of the eleventh month of the fourth year of Hōki (773), he passed away.

From the time the eagle had first seized and carried him off, his mother searched and wandered for years on end. She pressed through steep mountain paths and crossed vast seas; by day she rested on the grasses of open fields, unable to hold back tears at the cry of a pheasant longing for its young; by night she lay down at crossroads in lonely hamlets, equally unable to restrain her tears at the sound of a dog yearning for its master. How many times must she have suffered, with scarce provisions for even a single day, and life itself hanging by half a day's thread. Yesterday passed, today faded—fully thirty years she had wandered thus. Though her heart was like an aged thoroughbred [#1] still dreaming of a thousand-league journey, her exhaustion was like that of a starving hawk barely able to wait for a single call. Thinking she might as well return to her home province, she boarded a boat on the Yodo River. Strangers from unknown lands and unfamiliar villages were also aboard and fell to talking among themselves; in the course of conversation, someone said: "The monk Ryōben, Bishop of Nara, enjoys the deep and devoted faith of the Emperor, and his fame in the world is without parallel—yet this very Bishop was, as a small child, seized and carried off by an eagle, and the person who picked him up brought him here." Hearing this quite by chance, her heart was so stirred she felt as if it might fly out of her chest.

## [Left Page]

She came to the capital of Nara and was at last reunited with her own child, Bishop Ryōben. The Bishop devoted himself wholly to the filial care of his mother, serving her without distraction in any other matter, and in the end he saw her through to the last.
> Marginal note: *Details may be found in the* Shakusho *[Biographies of Eminent Monks]. The mother was venerated by later generations as the deity Koyasu no Kami [a deity of safe childbirth], and her shrine stands to the west of the Great Buddha Hall.*

**Nigatsudō** (二月堂, the "Second-Month Hall") is designated by the name Kenzakuin (羂索院, "Hall of the Lasso of Compassion"). It was constructed in the fourth year of Tenpyō Shōhō (752) by the Venerable Jitchū (実忠和尚), a disciple of Bishop Ryōben, by imperial decree. The principal image of Kannon (Guanyin/Avalokiteśvara) is an Eleven-Faced Image of Great Compassion (*Jūichimen Daihi*) brought from the shores of Naniwa in Settsu Province (present-day Osaka). It is a bronze image of seven *sun* (approx. 21 cm) in height, warm to the touch like human skin. Jitchū experienced a wondrous response from this image and enshrined it in the hall. From that time onward, religious services have been held each year from the first to the fourteenth day of the second month, and on the fifteenth day a *Nehan-e* (涅槃會, Nirvana Ceremony) is held in the rear hall.
> Marginal note: *[Source:] Shakusho.*

Furthermore, during the reign of Retired Emperor Toba, when a record was made of the most efficacious sacred images throughout Japan, the Kannon of Nigatsudō was described as "a living body" (*nikujin*). It is recorded in the *Buppō Dentsūki* (仏法伝通記, *Records of the Transmission of the Dharma*) that the great Kannon image was summoned by Jitchū from Mount Fudaraku (Potalaka), enshrined there, and that he performed the rites of penitential confession (*sange no hō*).

> Marginal note: *During the military fires of the Jishō era [1177–1181], the wind suddenly shifted direction and the hall was spared. Again, in the great fire of the second month of the seventh year of Kanbun [1667], the hall buildings were consumed in smoke, but the small Kannon image was carried out by a monk of Tōdai-ji. The great Kannon stood amid the smoke, yet the tassel of the prayer beads it held as a sacred implement was not damaged by the fire, and even when great timbers fell, they were deflected from around the image. Moreover, the handwritten copy of the* Nirvana Sūtra *in the hand of Emperor Shōmu, the* Avatamsaka Sūtra *in the hand of Empress Kōmyō, and the* Gōin *(牛王印, a sacred talisman seal) were not harmed by fire in the slightest. This matter is also recorded in the inscription on the bell of this hall.*
