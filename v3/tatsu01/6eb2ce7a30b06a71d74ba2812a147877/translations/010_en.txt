### On the Name "Tōkaidō" and Similar Designations

These names refer to the routes along which imperial envoys (chokushi) travel from the capital (Kinai) to the various provinces. It is for this reason that the term "Five Provinces of Kinai" is used.

---

### On the Province of Yamashiro

Long ago, during the time of Prince Umayado (Shōtoku Taishi), son of Emperor Yōmei, the timber for Shitennō-ji Temple was taken from a place that corresponds to the present-day ruins of Taira-no-Kyō (the old capital site). In the language of timber cutters (soma-bito), a place where timber is harvested is generally called "sankai" (山開, "mountain opening"). Thus, that place was anciently written as "Sankai."

As for why the name later came to be written as "Yamashiro" (山城): a place where seeds are sown is called "shiro" (代). This is used in words such as "nawashiro" (seedling bed) and "ueshiro" (planting bed). In the same way, because it was the place where timber had been taken and stored, it came to be called "Yamashiro" (山代, "mountain store"). However, the character "kai" (開) should not simply be read as "shiro" (代).

As for why the character "shiro" came to be written with the character for "castle" (城) rather than "代": When Emperor Tenmu was contending for the throne against Prince Ōtomo (Emperor Kōbun), and was passing through Yamashiro Province, the troops of Prince Ōtomo lay in ambush and attacked him, causing arrows to strike him in the back. Consequently, that place came to be called "Yase-no-Sato" (Arrow-Back Village / 矢背里).

Furthermore, as enemy soldiers suddenly gave chase again, Emperor Tenmu fortified himself in a castle deep in the northern mountains. Since even his horse was left tied up with its saddle still on, that place came to be named "Kurama" (鞍馬, "Saddled Horse").

It is from this time that the character "城" (castle/shiro) came to be used in writing "Yamashiro."
