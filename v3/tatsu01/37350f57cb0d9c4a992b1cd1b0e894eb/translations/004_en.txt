【Right folio】

...sixty-six provinces, each province having districts (gun), each district having townships (gō), and each township having manors and villages (shō and mura). Although their territorial forms differ, the principle that the root is established first and then the branches divide is the same reasoning shared by both Japan and China. In China, the systems of successive dynasties underwent changes and reforms, and thus geographical records—such as the Junigoku (Commandery Records), Kōto (Yellow Maps), Hōyo (Comprehensive Geographies), Katchi (Gazettes), and Fuken (Prefecture and County Records)—number in the hundreds and thousands of volumes. Yet during that time, the realm sometimes stood divided like the legs of a tripod, or was split between north and south, and thus it was impossible to encompass the entire world within a single work.

【Left folio】

It was not until the Ming dynasty that the Four Seas (all under heaven) were united and comprehensively examined across all ages, and the records of unification became detailed and complete.

In our realm (Japan), since the founding by Emperor Jinmu, one hundred generations of emperors of a single lineage have succeeded one another without alteration. The names of provinces and districts were gradually established from the reigns of Emperors Keikō and Seimu. During the Wadō era, the compilation of fudoki (topographical records) for the provinces and districts of the Home Provinces and Seven Circuits commenced for the first time. Thereafter, through successive imperial courts, additions, deletions, and revisions were made.
