...and by the Enchō era, the work was brought to great completion here. The mountains and rivers, local products, notable persons, and population of all sixty-six provinces could be known without stirring from one's seat. This achievement was equal in glory to the unification of the Han and Tang dynasties, and was perhaps nearly on a par with the golden age of Tang and Yu above, and no less admirable than the gazetteers of the Ming dynasty below.

However, as the realm entered its middle age (the medieval period), the authority of imperial rule ceased to prevail, armed conflicts arose frequently, and fire disasters compounded the destruction, so that the fudoki too were lost and obliterated. From this time forward,

【Left folio】
...the envoys sent to survey the provinces (the "light-carriage messengers") ceased to travel, the reports on local conditions and dialects were no longer submitted, and it became impossible to revive the fudoki again. Nevertheless, we are fortunate that the record of Izumi province survives in its entirety, that there remain fragmentary texts of Bungo province, and that scattered passages and isolated accounts can be found within older records. Through these, one may trace and seek out the old forms and precedents of antiquity—yet there is nothing to be done about the lack of documentary evidence to corroborate them, and how can one not sigh with deep lamentation?
