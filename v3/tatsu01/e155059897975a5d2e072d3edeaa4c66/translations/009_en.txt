...(in the various provinces,) although there are many temples established by imperial vow (*chokuganjo*), the Kokubunji temples are sacred sites built throughout more than sixty provinces by the great vow of the Emperor himself, where (copied) sūtras were enshrined and prayers were offered for well-being in both this life and the next. It is precisely because they are distributed (*bu*) across more than sixty provinces that the practice is called the "sixty-six-province" (*rokujūroku-bu*) pilgrimage.

Someone further asked: "There are many Kokubunji temples across the provinces that share the same mountain name (*sangō*). Has this been the case since ancient times? How did this come about?" I replied: "The designation 'Temple of the Golden Light, the Four Heavenly Kings, and the Protection of the Nation' (*Konkōmyō Shitennō Gokoku no Tera*) was the temple name (*jigō*) conferred by imperial decree in the thirteenth year of Tenpyō (741), and it serves as the temple name for all Kokubunji temples across the provinces. This has been abbreviated and is still used today as the mountain name (*sangō*)."

Someone further asked: "There are Kokubunji temples among the provinces that have not established Bhaiṣajyaguru (Yakushi Nyorai) as their principal image (*honzon*), but have instead designated other buddhas or bodhisattvas as the principal image. How is this to be understood?" I replied: "The founding (*kaiki*) of these temples dates from the ninth year of Tenpyō (737), and several hundred years have elapsed since then. During this long period, there must surely have been repeated occasions when fires broke out during times of war and conflict, resulting in destruction. Thus, the images of the Four Heavenly Kings, the sūtra scrolls, and the principal images that the Emperor had enshrined were all lost together,

and whatever principal images were later installed in their place would naturally differ from the originals. Furthermore, the temple landholdings (*denroku*) of ten thousand *se* (畝) established by Empress Kōken were also lost, and the extensive forested precincts that each temple once possessed were seized and plundered by local officials (*gunri*) during times of war. Therefore, it is entirely understandable that the principal images of the temples today should differ from the originals. Moreover, since approximately one thousand years have passed, every one of these temples has fallen into ruin and disrepair, to the point where even the locations of the Kokubunji temples throughout the provinces have become largely unknown. As a result, the majority of pilgrim monks (*kai-koku no sō*) making the circuit of the provinces are unable to locate them, and their intentions go unfulfilled, leading them to submit their sūtras at other temples instead. For this reason, I have now sought out and recorded in writing the locations of the Kokubunji temples in the various districts, villages, and hamlets across more than sixty provinces — to serve as an aid to those making the provincial pilgrimage. Although there are many who undertake the circuit of the provinces, no one has yet compiled such a work as this. It is for this reason that I have set it down in writing. This record of what I have heard and gathered — I have named it the *Engi* (Record of Origins)..."
