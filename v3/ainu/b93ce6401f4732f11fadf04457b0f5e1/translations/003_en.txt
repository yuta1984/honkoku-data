...having departed port, they encountered severe storms, and on the seventeenth day of the fifth month of the same year, they drifted ashore at this place called Moyoro. This was thirty years prior to the year Tenmei Hinoe-Uma (1786). After that, since no Japanese had crossed to this island, there were many Ainu people who had never yet seen a Japanese person. When I (the author) passed through this place in the fifth month of Tenmei Hinoe-Uma (1786), as it was the first time Japanese persons had traveled through, the news was passed from one neighboring village to the next, and great crowds of the native people (Ainu) gathered, all coming out to the shore, waiting eagerly for me to pass. Even the women and children all came out to the sandy beach to greet me, bowing in prostration where they sat, lined up in a row. Furthermore, in this vicinity there is a type of kelp called "Shashito." It resembles wakame (young bracken-frond kelp), with thin leaves that have cuts here and there, much like a painting of a phoenix's tail feathers,

【Left folio】

and it also resembles the leaves of a banana plant (bashō). The depth (width) of the leaves ranges from one shaku to about two shaku. Inside the leaves there are vein-like ribs resembling stems, and these ribs are hollow inside like thin bamboo, with internal fibers. The shorter leaves measure two to three shaku, while the longer ones extend to approximately twenty ken or more. It is truly the finest quality of kelp.

Proceeding further northward along the rocky coastline by boat, after approximately twenty-some ri, there is a high mountain called "Atsusanohori." In this vicinity there is a tree called "Kui." It resembles the tree known in Japan as the "hime-matsu" (姫松, dwarf pine/拇松), and has five needles (a five-needle pine). Its grain is fine and free of knots, its wood is red in color, and there is nothing comparable to it in Japan. A mushroom grows on this tree. It is called "Kuikarushi," which is the eburiko (猿の腰掛け, a type of bracket fungus/polypore). The Ainu of Matsumae also call this "Touhoshi." Furthermore, the eburiko produced from the northern regions of Ezo grows on the Ezo spruce (蝦夷松)...
