As it had been a long time since the father-in-law and son-in-law had met, a banquet was held to celebrate their reunion, with both parties expressing how much they had missed each other. I accompanied the son-in-law Ikotoiku to the home of Mauraka-Aino, where our host Mauraka sat in the upper right corner of the room, while the guest of honor, his son-in-law Ikotoiku, sat in the lower left corner. There, they both sat in fearful reverence of each other, not uttering a word. After some time, one of Mauraka's senior retainers stepped forward and addressed the son-in-law Ikotoiku. There is, in the formal Ainu mode of address called "Chaarake," a set form of response. At this time, the language used differed greatly from everyday speech, and I could not understand it at all — I could only watch intently.

After this, the two drew closer together in their seats, and father-in-law and son-in-law pressed their foreheads together, each holding the other's ears with both hands —

— each pressing the other's ears, and in silence they wept with deep emotion for quite some time. When the tears subsided, they drew back from their seats, bowed to each other twice, and then delivered formal speeches of greeting through the "Chaarafu" address. Inquiring about this afterward, I was told that this ceremony is observed when relatives or family members who have been separated for a long time are reunited.

After this, the banquet began, and a great crowd of Ainu gathered. Among them were three Russians (Akazin), and people from three nations — the Ainu, the Russians, and myself representing Japan — came together. Enlivened by the convivial drinking, Ainu songs were sung, Ainu dances were performed, and the Russians sang Russian songs and danced Russian dances.
