Among family members — parents, children, and spouses — they do nurse and care for one another, but all others are abandoned and left to die as people flee. This is all because there is no medical practice available to them. These are matters too painful to bear. Reflecting upon my own situation and thinking of people who must live under such pitiable governance, I find myself feeling all the more deeply grateful for the benevolent reign of our Japan — a gratitude that is impossible to forget.

**On the Subject of Language**

One entry: In the "Age of the Gods" chapter (*Jindai-kan*) of the *Nihon Shoki*, the two characters for "nomi" (ノミ) are glossed as meaning "prayer" (*kitō*). In the Ainu language as well, prayer is likewise called "nomi." Furthermore, the Ainu carve shavings from willow wood, fashioning them into objects resembling ritual offerings (*nusa/heike*), which they present before their deities, calling these objects "tasa." This too must be a word preserved from the Age of the Gods.

**[Left folio]**
Upon reading a certain Shinto text, I found that when a carpenter builds a commoner's house, during the ridge-raising ceremony (*munage no shūgi*), objects are placed atop the ridgepole: those hung with wood shavings are called "green offerings" (*ao-nusa*), and those hung with white hemp are called "white offerings" (*shiro-nusa*). It is for this reason, the text explains, that the character for hemp (麻) is read as "nusa." Furthermore, the Ainu refer to a deity as "kamui." This is likely a transformation of the word "kamii" (神居, "divine dwelling"). "Kamii" (神居), "miyai" (宮居, "palace dwelling"), and "torii" (鳥居, "bird dwelling/gateway") — in all of these, the character 居 (*i*, "to dwell") functions as an auxiliary element, and "kamii" likewise refers to the deity itself. Just as the word "shamisen" is pronounced "shyamisen" in rural regions, the transformation of "kashii" into "kamui" follows the same principle. Similarly, the Ainu word for hemp, "nushiya," represents a shift from "sa" to "shiya." This is analogous to how the act of discarding something — "utsu" combined with "yaru" — becomes "utchiyaru."
