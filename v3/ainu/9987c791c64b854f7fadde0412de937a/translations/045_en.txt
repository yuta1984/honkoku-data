Nevertheless, [Furiuento] eventually came to master the *kana* syllabary, and thereafter gradually came to feel grateful for it. The fact that he looked up to me as a parent only added further to my feelings of pity for him. Now, I brought this Furiuento along with me to Matsumae, and on the way, we lodged for one night at the home of a peasant named Shiratori Shinjūrō in the village of Hakodate. There, as a diversion, I had paper brought out and made [Furiuento] write something — and he rendered his meaning admirably. At that point, the host remarked, "This is something unheard-of — a most extraordinary thing," and praised him most enthusiastically. From there we returned to Matsumae, and while we remained there, I had him write things from time to time. This matter apparently became the subject of rumor of its own accord, and word of it reached the domain lord. Declaring that "to teach written characters to the Ezo natives and to instruct them in the Japanese language is a prohibition that has existed since ancient times," the blame for this offense was placed upon

me, and I was greatly despised by the Matsumae domain — a truly lamentable state of affairs. Since Ezo has now already ripened to the moment of opening to the world, it should before long become a fine land — and one can thus understand why the Matsumae domain resented me. Upon learning of the various articles of prohibition, I found that virtually all of them ran contrary to the way of benevolence (*jindō*) and were numerous matters that caused great harm to the local people. I intend to find an opportunity to set each of these down in writing in due course. Although such human conduct is prohibited as described in the previous chapter, in recent years great numbers of Russians ("red people," *akahito*) have crossed the sea, presenting the laws and ordinances of their country and nurturing and instructing [the natives], so that the natives have come to greatly embrace them and become assimilated into Russian customs.
