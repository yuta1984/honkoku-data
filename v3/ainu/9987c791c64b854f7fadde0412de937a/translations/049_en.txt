【Right folio】
　　— loaded goods such as these onto boats and came to the trading post (*unjō-goya*) on Kunashiri Island to trade, receiving in exchange rice, *kōji* (malting agent), and sundry goods, then built a temporary hut on the beach and stayed there. He distributed rice, *kōji*, and sundry goods to the concubines living five or seven *ri* away in the surrounding area. The rice came in bales of eight *tō* and the *kōji* in bales of eight *shō*; when three bales of each were sent to them, the concubines brewed cloudy sake (*doburoku*) from the rice and *kōji* and sent a messenger to Tsukinoito. Tsukinoito, led by the hand together with the concubines he had brought along on his travels, visited each concubine's home, where he was welcomed with the cloudy sake they had prepared, and he held banquets and made merry with the concubines. Even when the concubines gathered together in large numbers, there was no jealousy or envy among them; they were all admirably harmonious and affectionate toward one another.

【Left folio】
　　This is the custom of the Ezo lands, and regardless of one's social standing — whether high or low — when going on trading journeys, people bring along all members of their household and travel with their belongings in tow. Such is the custom of Ezo. Furthermore, although the concubines are provided only with houses built for them — receiving no provision for food or clothing beyond that — they each support themselves independently: they process the bark of a tree called *ohyaw* (*ヲヒヤウ*), weave it into a thick cloth-like garment called *attush* (*アツシ*), and it is the custom of Ezo women to make this into clothing and present it as a gift to their husbands. High-ranking headmen (*otona*) have many retainers called *utashi* (*ウタシ*) — hereditary retainers passed down through generations — and when the master sets out on a trading journey, the wives and children of these *utashi* also accompany him.
