## (Right folio — main text)

…Among them were Nagamatsu; Ihei, from Miyako Village (*Miyakomura*) in the same area; Naiko; and Nagasuke, also from the same village — five survivors in total, who became subjects (*dojin*, naturalized persons) of the Russians. These five men likewise settled in various parts of the country. Rihachi from Okuto Village (*Okutomura*) became the son-in-law of a man named Hyotoro, a Japanese interpreter (*tsūji*) who was himself a native of Kamchatka (*Kamusasu*). Katsuemon settled in a place called "Irukutsuko" (Irkutsk), near the royal capital of Russia. He became a subject of the Tsar, was granted a stipend of two hundred silver coins, rose to the position of an official (*yūshi*) in Irkutsk, and fathered a son. This son was exceptionally gifted and surpassed others in ability; in the third year of Tenmei, the Year of the Rabbit (1783), he was seventeen years of age. ## (Left folio — main text)

…serving as master navigator (*hakushi*), they set sail from the port called "Yorowotarahanerisko" (Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky, or possibly another port), heading southward, and thereafter their whereabouts became unknown. This was told to me by both Inyuyo and Sasunosukoi, and I cannot but feel that there is some deeper significance to this matter.

That great ship made for the lands of Ezo and Japan, and came ashore on Karafuto Island (Sakhalin) in western Ezo. Whether it arose from disputes over trade with the indigenous people of that island or from some other cause, hostilities escalated greatly into open battle, and it appears that all the Russians aboard the ship were shot and killed. The vessel then drifted and ran aground at a place called Atatsutoi, on Urutsufu Island (Iturup Island) in eastern Ezo.
