It is a great natural principle that it is difficult to advance from a southern, sunny land (*nan'yō*) to a northern, shadowed land (*hokuhin*), whereas it is easy to advance from a northern, shadowed land to a southern, sunny land. If one travels southward from the land of Kamchatka, each day's journey southward brings one day's worth of greater warmth, and furthermore the natural products gradually become more abundant. Therefore, it stands to reason that such southward advance is easy. Ezo and its various islands, as seen from Japan, are all northern, shadowed lands. That it is difficult to advance into them thus follows from the preceding argument.

In order to convey this reasoning to the responsible officials (*yūshi*) of Japan, it is said that the Dutch *kapitein* (trading-post chief) named "Ashinto Wellem Hei," who came to Japan accompanying a certain Natsume as part of the retinue of the Nagasaki magistrate, lamented and spoke of the matter to his companions, telling of certain circumstances concerning the islands of Ezo, which are Japan's dependent territories.
