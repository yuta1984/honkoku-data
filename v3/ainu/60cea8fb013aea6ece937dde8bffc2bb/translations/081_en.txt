## (Right folio — main text)

The people of Matsumae also reportedly caught sight of this vessel, which then headed west and crossed the sea, after which its whereabouts became unknown. As I examined the *manju*-like cakes mentioned in the preceding section, they appeared to be made not of wheat flour alone, but with other grain flours mixed in, kneaded together, and cooked in fish oil — for they carried the distinct smell of oil. The aforementioned Sōya village lies at the northwest tip of the island where Matsumae is situated (Hokkaidō), and serves as the point of departure for crossing over to Karafuto (Sakhalin). It is my belief that the Russian vessel was likely making a circuit of the Matsumae island region by way of reconnaissance. This, I fear, may bode ill for the security of Japan. It would appear that their design was to demonstrate Russian customs and institutions across the twenty-one islands of eastern Ezochi and beyond, to rename the islands, to send goods and cloth, to nurture and instruct the native peoples and win their loyalty — and furthermore, to bring the island on which Matsumae stands, as well as Karafuto, under the same arrangement as the twenty-one islands.

### On Sea Distances and Trade

The distances between the islands and the sea routes between them are not yet known even to the local people of Matsumae, and needless to say, beyond Kunashiri Island, where Japanese vessels do not sail, there is no knowledge whatsoever. The islands are numerous. Even Japanese mariners have no means of accurately measuring distances at sea, and so they can only estimate the distances by eye, gauging near and far by sight alone. I must humbly acknowledge that such estimates are not a sufficient basis for precise reckoning. When I inquired of the Russians, their method was exceedingly efficient and direct…
