Taking [the eight degrees of latitude] as the horizontal measure and the thirteen degrees of longitude as the vertical measure, the breadth of land that Japan occupies diagonally within this vertical-horizontal framework nowhere exceeds one or two degrees across the entire country; Japan is a country whose territory extends diagonally from the direction of *Saru* (south-southwest) to *Tora* (north-northeast). From Kunashiri Island to the land of Kamchatka, taking twenty-two degrees of latitude as the horizontal and over twenty degrees as the vertical, within this framework the twenty-one islands extend diagonally in a band — just like the breadth of Japan's territory. Their location fans out from *Hitsuji-Saru* (south-southwest) toward *Ushi-Tora* (north-northeast), extending broadly, widely, and at great length. Knowing all this, the following should be understood.

In the year 1713 by the reckoning of the country of the Redmen (Russia) — corresponding to the fourth year of Shōtoku in Japan — the Redmen reached the land of Kamchatka for the first time and brought this

**[Left folio]**
country under their dominion. Thereafter, from around the Anei era (1772–1781), they built fortifications in Kamchatka, rotated administrative officials throughout its districts, and engaged vigorously in the work of colonial development. As a result, they renamed all twenty-one islands, collected taxes from the indigenous inhabitants while educating and civilizing them, and sent the revenues to the imperial capital "Musukuha" (Moscow). The native peoples of those islands have already been assimilated into the customs of the Redmen. Is this not cause for lamentation?

Here lies a matter of great importance. Whether a country should undertake colonial development depends on the latitudes (the degrees of the two poles) of the country in question, and one should thoroughly comprehend the general principles before embarking on such development — so runs this argument.
