**On Fishing and Hunting**

　In the year Tenmei Hinoe-Uma (1786), in service of an inspection mission to survey the borders of the Ezo lands, I boarded a vessel called the *Jintsūmaru*, belonging to one Tomaya Kyūbei of Edo Honchō, under the command of the offshore ship's captain Tahei, and arrived at Matsumae. From there, I crossed the sea to "Nisheheshi" in the Eastern Ezo lands. A man well acquainted with the sea routes along the eastern coast of the Ezo lands was to be selected by the Matsumae domain; accordingly, a man from Matsumae Karatsu-machi, serving as navigator (michisaki-yaku) named Jirōbei, joined the crew, and we arrived by boat at a great river called "Nishehets." At this "Nishehets," every year when the autumnal equinox arrives, salmon come swimming upstream toward the headwaters of the river in great numbers. As I was in charge of this duty, I gave instructions to the men, and on the seventeenth day of the eighth month of that same year, at around the seventh hour of the afternoon,

[Left folio]

　the nets that were pulled in yielded approximately three thousand salmon. Furthermore, in the nets pulled in at the sixth hour of the morning of the following eighteenth day, after removing those of poor quality from among the great catch and carefully selecting the good ones, there were ninety-seven bundles. One bundle consists of one hundred fish. "Removing" means discarding those that are exceptionally small, discarding those that are exceptionally large, and selecting those of middling size as the best quality. Those of middling size are gathered together and loaded onto the ship. It is the established rule of the Matsumae domain that large ships load no fewer than approximately fifty thousand fish.

　That such quantities can be caught within roughly five to seven days is proof of how abundant the yields of hunting and fishing are.
