**[On the Excavation of Clay Figurines at Tobetsu Village and Ainu Customs]**

In the spring of the tenth year of Kansei (1798), twenty-two barrel-shaped objects were excavated near the tutelary shrine at Tobetsu Village (located five ri to the west of Hakodate). These appear to be artifacts from ancient times. The Ainu of Mombetsu (a place name) call these sacred objects "Toriashi," and they take the form of a figure dressed in a fur garment. Objects have also been excavated from a hill at Fushikootan (a place name); though their shapes differ slightly, the patterns of the hair and clothing are the same as those described here. Upon examination, these appear to be female figures. The dots pricked around the eyes and mouth resemble the tattoos (mojiri) practiced today, and the hair is depicted braided and bound atop the head.

One account notes: Among the Ainu, when a childless person prays to the gods and is blessed with a child, after the child grows up, the hair is left uncut and braided into three plaits, left to hang freely. This is called "Kamoi Odobi" (sacred hair of the gods). Ainu bearing this sacred hair can be found at Mukawa (a place name), Hoinkaru (an Ainu place name), Kunsui (a place name), and Peberekes (an Ainu place name), and it can also be seen in various other locations. Upon reflection, this practice likely originated in ancient times when there were no tools for cutting hair — to avoid the dishevelment of loose, hanging hair, people braided it into three plaits.

The fur garment is fashioned like a bag and is worn by pulling it over the body from the hem upward. This style of dress is entirely absent in the Ainu lands closer to Wajin territory, but in the more distant Ainu lands it serves as women's clothing, said to be worn in order to discourage clandestine meetings and sexual misconduct. These clay figurines were most likely buried in graves as substitutes for concubines and wives in the earliest times.
