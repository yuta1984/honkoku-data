There are no doctors, and when someone falls ill, someone who appears to be a village elder prays continuously, day and night, until recovery. They have neither sandals nor wooden clogs; when it rains, they hastily fashion something and wear objects resembling the lid of a tea-grinding mortar on their heads. Their floor coverings are woven from reeds, and their provisions along the way consist entirely of fish, to which they apply shark oil regardless of what they are eating. Side dishes are placed in small bowl-like vessels about eight sun (approximately 24 cm) in size and eaten by hand. When taking meals, food is placed in a bowl and scooped up with a ladle-like spoon. They cook food over an open hearth. The climate of the region is such that every three days the sky clears, then becomes misty and heavily fogged; snow falls until the eighth day of the fifth month. There are no ceremonies equivalent to the third-month festivals, and people's ages are unknown. It appears that they count one year by the accumulation of snow. When asked "How old are you?", they say "snow falls, snow falls" and count as many (snows) as they can. There are no laws or regulations, no public notice boards to be seen, no temples, and no Buddhist monks

can be seen either. They have no writing system, and when shown written characters, they clasp their hands together in reverence. When meeting people, they clasp their hands together. They do the same when meeting those on ships. This appears to be the etiquette of the Ezo (Ainu). They are always seated in a cross-legged position. I searched here and there for burial grounds but found no grave mounds. Wondering if they ate humans, I inquired, but it seems that when someone dies, they grieve especially deeply. The dead are wrapped in straw matting, buried in a hole dug in a distant hill, with no marker left, and the house of the deceased is burned and the family moves elsewhere. Having stayed from the fourth through the fifth month, we eventually came to understand a little of what they said to us and grew familiar with what they said, and among their words there are some Japanese terms.
　What corresponds to "magistrate" is "Mishiba"; the tail feathers of an eagle are "hishi-hishi."
