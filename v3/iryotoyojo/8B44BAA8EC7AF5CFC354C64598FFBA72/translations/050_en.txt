[Right folio]

(Continuing from the previous page) … from the great things above — the sun, moon, and stars — down to the small things below — birds and beasts, plants and trees, fish and shellfish, insects and crawling creatures — all of them, within this qi that is fused and unified with our very own bodies, form distinctions between self and other (this side and that side).

Therefore, in Heaven there are the four seasons — spring, summer, autumn, and winter — and wind, rain, frost, and dew: none of these are without their teaching (to all things). The earth carries the divine qi, breathes in and out the thunder and lightning, and flows its form into the ten thousand things: none of this, too, is without its teaching. As it is said, strike here and the echo resounds there; what is set forth on this side receives its response on that side. Heavenly calamities and earthly portents, the abundance or failure of the harvest year, the upheavals of pestilence and war, and whether a nation or household achieves harmony or not — all of these depend on the "stimulus" (kan 感) and "response" (ō 応) of this qi. Both the Way and its teachings arise entirely from this.

Hence the *Zhongyong* (Doctrine of the Mean) also says: "What has not yet been expressed in joy, anger, sorrow, or delight is called the Mean (chū 中). When all such expressions emerge and each hits the proper measure, this is called Harmony (wa 和). The Mean is the great root of all under Heaven. Harmony is the universal path of all under Heaven. Let the Mean and Harmony be fully realized, and Heaven and Earth will settle into their proper places, and the ten thousand things will be nurtured."

[Left folio]

— just as a fine musical instrument's notes strike together in accord — thoughts and actions each and all attaining their proper measure: this is called the Harmony of "hitting the proper measure in all expressions." It is explained that, following naturally the inborn nature and life-endowment (*seimei* 性命) received from Heaven, and through the sincerity of the single mind that keeps watch over oneself in solitude (*dokushin* 独慎), Heaven and Earth each attain their proper place and the ten thousand things are transformed and nurtured. And it is with precisely this same mind that — from filial piety and fraternal respect, loyalty and trustworthiness, joy, anger, sorrow, and delight, and all manner of endeavors, to music and dance, poetry and song, down even to the most trivial of matters — there is not one thing that does not participate in the harmony-and-union (*wagō* 和合) and sympathetic resonance (*kan'ō* 感応) of this qi.
