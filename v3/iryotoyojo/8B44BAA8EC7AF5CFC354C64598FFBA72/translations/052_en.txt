In Laozi it is written: "Concentrate the qi and achieve suppleness — can you become like an infant?" This means: by entrusting one's body to the natural Great Qi and bringing about the harmony of utmost softness — being like an infant, which desires nothing — one will surely be able to preserve one's original and innate nature in its entirety. The infant's natural *kantsū* (感通, intuitive resonance) is swifter than that of an adult, and it is far less likely to fall victim to harm from external things. Indeed, because an infant is without deliberate thought (*mushin* 無心) and its inner being is empty and open (*kyо* 虚), its sympathetic resonance is supremely swift — like the way an empty valley carries an echo. Since the sympathetic resonance of the Great Qi is so remarkably rapid and keen, one is taught: constantly nurture your heart-mind and return to the state of the infant.

During the Bunka era, on the day of the Fukagawa Hachiman Festival, a woman carrying a one-year-old child set out to cross the Eitai

Bridge. But the child suddenly broke into crying and would not stop. The mother said, "Very well then, I shall not cross," and turned back — whereupon the child quickly fell silent. She thought it strange, yet did not dwell upon it, and was just about to step into a teahouse by the side of the bridge to rest for a while, when at that very moment the bridge pilings sank some five or six *ken* down to the riverbed, the bridge collapsed from its middle, and over a thousand people fell into the water and perished. Startled by the commotion of the crowd, she marveled: "How could this child have known and cried out so suddenly? Surely it must be that the gods and buddhas have saved us."
