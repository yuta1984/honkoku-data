...to *upward generation* (*jōsei*), one is added; [pitches are derived] by *sanzun son'eki* (the method of subtracting and adding one-third). (This method appears in the *Lüshi Chunqiu*, the *Huainanzi*, the *Shiji*, and the *Book of Former Han*.) To understand why the count of angular [pitch] numbers stops at sixty-four, one uses the diagram on the right. This is the investigation (*kanga*) of Tomonobu [Chishin / Hirano Shigenari, the author]. This diagram generates numbers using a triangle, building up from one to arrive at sixty-four. Doubling one yields two; doubling two yields four; doubling four yields eight; doubling eight yields sixteen; doubling sixteen yields thirty-two; doubling thirty-two yields sixty-four. One and sixty-four are adjacent to each other, and at sixty-four the series reaches its limit. From the two principles (*ryōgi*) of *yin* and *yang* in the *Book of Changes* (*Yi Jing*), the Four Symbols (*sishō*) are generated; from the Four Symbols, the Eight Trigrams (*hakke*) are generated; from the Eight Trigrams, accumulating further, the sixty-four hexagrams (*rokujūshi ke*) are generated, exhausting thereby the principles of all things in heaven and earth. The underlying principle is the same: one is accumulated to become sixty-four.

This is the beginning from which the numbers of the cubic solid (*hōtai*) arise, and from this one, accumulating up to nine (as in diagrams A and B). This merely constitutes [the cubic] form (*tai*) itself, and there is no variation in high or low *kyokusetsu* (melodic inflection, musical pitch contour). Here, taking this one unit and adding it to the three of the *dai-ittō* (first-order/primary grade) to make four, one obtains the sequence four–three–two (as in diagram C).

In musical instruments of both court (*ga*) and popular (*zoku*) music, instruments such as the *kin* (qin, seven-stringed zither), *shitsu* (se, twenty-five-stringed zither), *shō* (xiāo, vertical flute), *shō* (shēng, mouth organ), *yokobue* (transverse flute), *shakuhachi*, *sansen* (three-stringed shamisen), and *kokyū* (bowed lute) are slim and elongated in form. Instruments such as the *ko* (drum), *taiko* (large drum), *kei* (stone chime), and *dora* (gong) are thin and flat in form (as in diagram D).
