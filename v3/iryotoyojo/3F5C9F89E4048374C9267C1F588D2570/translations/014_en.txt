And who was there among them? Maruyama Awa-no-kami Karuyaki — for visiting the sick during a bout of smallpox, none could surpass these. Then in the northern provinces: the *ame-mochi* (sweet malt rice cake) of Takata's Shirō Okina; and from Kaga's rice cakes, Imasaka's *yōkan* (sweet bean jelly) heading the list, joined by several hundred men of proven daring and repute.

　　　　Chapter Four

Coming considerably later were: Abegawa Kinako-no-suke (Mr. Roasted-Soybean-Flour of Abe River), Tsurumi Manjūrō (Steamed-Bun Tenth Son of Tsurumi), and Kashiwa-no-Mochizō (Rice-Cake Storehouse) of Saru-ga-Banba. Among the female warriors were: Otafuku Gozen (Lady Pudgy-face), Okame Dango (Round-faced Dumpling), Otetsу Botamochi (Clumsy-hands Sticky-Rice-Cake), and even the old crone of Kusatsu.

Beyond these, all things bearing the word *mochi* — *kanemochi* (the wealthy), *iemochi* (householders), *mogura-mochi* (moles), *oya-mochi* (those under parental support), *komochi* (those with children), *nyōbō-mochi* (the married), *nimotsu* (baggage carriers), *kachi-mochi* (foot soldiers), *senki-mochi* (those afflicted with colic), the wilful *kanshaku-mochi* (the hot-tempered), *mushi-mochi* (steamed cakes), and children of their ilk — all such were set aside, and the names given were those of Edo's celebrated specialities, known to all: the flour-shop's wares, *shiratama-mochi* (white dumpling rice cakes), and in numbers beyond counting — *ara-tama* — the fresh *waka-mochi* (New Year's rice cakes) of spring, *higan-mochi* (equinox rice cakes), the *kusa-mochi* (mugwort rice cakes) of the third month, the *uno-ha-mochi* (deutzia-leaf rice cakes) of the "ever-awesome" eighth day of the fourth month, the *chimaki* (rice dumplings) and *kashiwa-mochi* (oak-leaf rice cakes) of Boys' Day, the *kōri-mochi* (ice-preserved rice cakes) from the icehouse in snowy midwinter, the celebrated *bota-mochi* (sticky rice cakes with bean paste) of the Bon festival, and in autumn…
