"I have such a sad thing to tell you," she says, her eyes brimming with tears — and even a strapping fellow who might well hold his own in an arm-wrestling match with the demon Shuten-dōji finds himself, much like a visitor confronted by the Niō figures in a nirvana painting, stricken with something ill-befitting his character, and wilts, shedding tears alongside her. Seeing this, one can only conclude that the lure of women is truly an axe that severs bone and a poison that clouds the heart beyond all else. In the *Busso Sankyō*, it is written as the words of the Buddha: "There is nothing more formidable than carnal desire among all things of form." Furthermore, looking at how Song dynasty's Jing Zhiyuan lost his life on account of a beautiful woman, it is entirely understandable that he declared with firm conviction, "To die in loyalty is a very light thing indeed." "Esteem the worthy as worthy, and exchange it for beauty" — this is precisely that sort of situation. Moreover, since men of the Ming dynasty have also said such things as "A woman's coquetry surpasses even the fox spirit, and her stratagems are deeper than the tiger's pit," the ruination of one's self through a courtesan is no different in its human nature whether in China or Japan.
