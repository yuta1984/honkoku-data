Following after the crow, he arrived to find what appeared to be a magistrate's office for birds, where all manner of petitions were being heard. In the matter of a quarrel among chickens, both parties were reprimanded. As for sparrows in the rice fields and quail in the millet, they were all ordered to take only fallen grains and nothing more. "As we have already instructed the geese and ducks: it is inexcusable to simply eat what the farmers have labored over. Fallen grain, though precious as a cereal, may be eaten at your own discretion. Furthermore, woodpeckers are strictly forbidden from pecking anything other than dead trees."

A matter of suspicious inquiry had arisen regarding a bush warbler playing in the cherry blossoms, and a kite was brought in bound with rope before the tribunal for examination. "You have been warned time and again, yet how do you account for yourself? You have been stealing tofu and deep-fried tofu [aburaage]. There can be no excuse that you were compelled to eat such things — this is entirely your own indulgence. Furthermore, it appears you intended to snatch fish from a fishmonger. Not only were you caught stealing a tobacco pouch thinking it was aburaage, and this on top of having already stolen a single four-mon coin, but you also scratched a child's hand, and even the matter of snatching Sanbyaku's [a person's name, lit. "Three Hundred"] kabayaki [grilled eel] order — all of these have come to light one by one. Your brazenness is extraordinary. Having been found guilty of all these offenses upon full examination, you are hereby sentenced to be placed in an orange crate and handed over to the eta [a low-caste group]." The punishment: to be put on public display before people's homes.

[The kite's speech:]
"Everyone seems to think of us as they please, but a 'daytime kite' [hiru tonbi], as they say, is apparently what humans are."
