As he continued toward Gotenyama, a flock of birds flew past chattering "chachaku, chachaku," and thinking "this looks interesting too," he donned his magical cloak and hat and followed after them. The path led deep into a dense forest. As he observed, he found a small hermitage, and looking toward what was approaching from the far side, he saw it was a dove. One dove, wearing two swords and formal upper-and-lower garments (kamishimo), happened to meet another dove dressed in hakama and haori. Their exchange was so proper and courteous that even humans should be ashamed by comparison. Indeed, it is said doves observe the "three-branch rule of propriety" — they always perch three branches below their parents out of respect. Even refined people of good standing behave this way, so surely this should be how one treats one's parents — and again, Shinemon felt humbled before the birds.

He then peered into the hermitage and saw a young crow serving food attentively to its elderly parent crow, urging it to eat more. "Please have seconds if anything is to your liking," said the young crow. "No, no, it's all so delicious — I've had quite enough," replied the old one. Observing this, Shinemon thought, "This must be what they call the crow's 'virtue of reciprocal feeding' (hanpo no ko)." It is said that for one hundred days the chick receives its parents' nourishment, and then for one hundred days, it nourishes its parents in return — meaning the account is exactly balanced, like a trade at cost with neither gain nor loss. Yet the young crow's tender care for its aged parent appeared to be a devotion of a hundred years' worth. Truly, as a human being, Shinemon felt greatly humbled before the birds.

When the young crow said "Today we have rice," he thought to follow it.
