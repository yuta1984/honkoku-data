　Gazing up into the breaks in the clouds, Tametomo spoke: "I have heard it said that when a serpent has lived for several hundred years, it will certainly have a jewel within its body. The dragon knows of this, and in order to take that jewel, first sends thunder to shake the serpent loose. I believe this great serpent must surely contain such a jewel. Shigezue, try cutting it open and see." Shigezue replied, "As you command," and once again thrust his blade into the area around the serpent's throat, attempting to split it open. Rain poured down as though overturning a great basin, and the thunder grew ever more fierce. Tametomo, thinking that should the thunderbolt come falling down he would shoot it, nocked an arrow to his bow and withdrew a short distance from the spot. Sudo, soaked through and through by the rain, heedless of the raging thunder, had already split the serpent open as far as the tail and looked within, but found nothing that seemed to be what he sought. Thinking that perhaps the jewel might be toward the head, he took up his blade again, peeled back the skin of the head, and searched beneath the jaw — and there, between the bones, was something. "Ah, surely this is it!" he thought joyfully, and just as he was about to draw it out, in that very instant, the four directions went pitch dark, a mass of black cloud covered and piled upon Sudo, and a single crack of thunder roared so mightily that heaven and earth seemed to shake—

— as the bolt came crashing down. Tametomo drew his bow to its full length and released the arrow with a sharp twang, and it seemed to find its mark; but in an instant the rain ceased, the clouds settled, and the morning sun rose over the eastern peak. Tametomo was desperately anxious about Shigezue, and without even waiting for the sky to clear, ran to his side and looked — and there, most pitifully, Shigezue lay: his skull shattered, his flesh torn apart, his whole body blackened and scorched, his limbs no longer connected — and yet he had not let go of the blade in his hand. His left hand, drenched in blood, was clasped around a single jewel, and he had died holding it thus. It appeared that the thunderbolt had risen from this very spot, for a great camphor tree of more than one armspan in girth had been split in two from crown to root as though cleaved by an axe. But Tametomo paid it no heed, and mourned only the violent death of Shigezue. "I," he said, "took pride in the courage of hot blood, and twice went in search of peril: before, I caused Yamao to be killed through my error, and now I have lost Sudo as well. Even were this a jewel worth a hundred strings of coins, could it ever be traded for the life of one retainer? Above all, he had served me faithfully and sincerely through years of hardship, and we had spoken our hearts to one another, finding comfort in each other's company. That he said his dream was ill-omened and tried to avoid it — that was the foreboding of his death here."
